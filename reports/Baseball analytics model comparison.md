# Fancy models rarely beat the humble baseline

Across win probability, player projection, pitching analytics, postseason simulation, and betting markets, the academic and sabermetric literature converges on one uncomfortable finding: **model architecture matters far less than data granularity and problem framing, and almost every documented accuracy gain over a simple baseline is small, inconsistent, or confined to a narrow subpopulation.** Logistic regression beat gradient boosting, neural nets, and FiveThirtyEight's Elo system outright in the one rigorous multi-metric MLB win-probability benchmark found ([arXiv:2511.02815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02815)); the best Statcast-enriched LightGBM model beat a 2004-vintage Marcel baseline by only 12.1% on batter wOBA MAE ([dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)); and a full hierarchical Bayesian model with age splines, park effects, and a latent elite/non-elite mixture lost on aggregate RMSE to PECOTA and only modestly beat Marcel ([arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)). For this project's specific baseline — Pythagenpat + 4-factor Elo, Marcel, closed-form empirical Bayes shrinkage, a Monte Carlo bracket simulator, and proportional-devig/fixed-Kelly betting — the clearest, best-evidenced upgrades are modest and additive (team-quality-adjusted win probability, Statcast contact-quality features bolted onto the existing shrinkage pipeline, Shin/power devigging, uncertainty-aware Kelly shrinkage), while the genuinely transformative methods (pitch-sequence transformers, player-level postseason simulation, risk-constrained dynamic Kelly) all require standing up new data infrastructure rather than swapping a formula. The betting-market literature adds a further wrinkle: the market segment this project would compete in is close to efficient by closing time, so the highest-value engineering investment is not a smarter edge-finder but a more honestly-calibrated probability engine and more principled risk management around whatever edge exists.

## Logistic regression beats Elo and gradient boosting on win probability

The single most directly relevant academic benchmark for the win-probability engine is Allen & Savala's 2025/2026 arXiv study, which trained logistic regression, SVM, KNN, XGBoost, and a neural network on 46,159 MLB games (2001–2015 train, 2016–2019 test) and compared all of them against FiveThirtyEight's Elo model using accuracy, AUROC, log-loss, and Brier score simultaneously ([arXiv:2511.02815](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02815)). Logistic regression won on every metric (62.94% accuracy, 0.6418 log-loss, 0.2254 Brier), while **FiveThirtyEight's Elo system was the weakest method tested** (56.96% accuracy, 0.2420 Brier) and showed a visibly compressed, underconfident probability range (25th–75th percentile of only 48.3%–58.7%). XGBoost, despite its reputation on tabular data, finished last among the ML classifiers (61.08% accuracy), and ensembling via majority voting barely moved the needle. This is a direct, if indirect, caution for a 4-factor Elo engine: Elo's simplicity may be leaving real accuracy on the table relative to even a basic logistic-regression meta-model, but the fix the literature actually supports is modest — stacking a logistic-regression layer on top of existing Elo/Pythagenpat outputs as input features, not discarding the interpretable rating system for a tree ensemble or neural net, since added model complexity did not pay off in the one controlled comparison available. Market/Vegas-line baselines cluster near 59% accuracy in secondary sources, implying the entire field of methods — Elo, Pythagorean, logistic regression, gradient boosting — is converging on a hard information ceiling around 55–63% for pregame MLB prediction, a ceiling tied to baseball's intrinsically high game-to-game variance rather than any specific model's shortcomings.

Two augmentation strategies carry stronger, more targeted evidence than a wholesale architecture swap. Russell Carleton's Baseball Prospectus analysis of 1993–2016 play-by-play data found that standard in-game win-probability tables, which ignore team quality entirely, need a median correction of about 3 percentage points league-wide and up to 26 points in lopsided-team mismatches, with the correction shrinking to about 1.2 points by the seventh inning ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/31219/baseball-therapy-is-win-probability-broken/)) — a small, additive fix (fold team-level run-scoring/allowing rates into the existing WP table) rather than a redesign. Separately, FiveThirtyEight's own starting-pitcher adjustment term, `4.7 × (pitcher_rGS − team_rGS)`, computed from a Tangotiger-modified Bill James Game Score, is reported to have been worth about 109 Elo points (roughly 15 percentage points of win probability) for Pedro Martínez starts in 2000 ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-our-mlb-predictions-work/amp/)) — functionally the same category of adjustment the project's 4-factor engine already implements, confirming the general approach rather than suggesting a different one. On the theoretical side, Luo & Miller's Weibull-based derivation of the Pythagorean exponent (arXiv:1406.3402) supplies a principled statistical justification for why an exponent near 1.83–2 (and Pythagenpat's scoring-environment-dependent exponent) fits empirically — useful validation, but it changes no code, only documentation, since Pythagenpat already implements a dynamically-varying exponent consistent with the Weibull-implied range ([arXiv:1406.3402](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.3402)).

## Marcel's floor is nearly unbeatable, and Statcast buys single digits

Two decades of projection-system elaboration — Marcel (2004) through PECOTA's nearest-neighbor matching, ZiPS's aging curves and Monte Carlo simulation, Steamer's calibrated regression, and THE BAT X's Statcast "stuff" layer — have produced accuracy gains Tom Tango himself calls "only a small increase... despite doing substantially more work" ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/tangotigers-projection-tests/)). Dan Meyer's 2014 Hardball Times study (177,967 PA) found hitter wOBA MAE spanning just .0274 (ZiPS, best) to .0289 (Marcel, worst) — a 0.0015 spread Meyer himself called "subtle" ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/evaluating-the-2014-projection-systems/)). No peer-reviewed, JQAS-caliber paper has ever benchmarked Marcel, PECOTA, ZiPS, Steamer, and THE BAT against each other on a common metric; every rigorous head-to-head is a sabermetric blog analysis. The one system with a confirmed, disclosed Statcast/ML component — THE BAT X — has topped FantasyPros' annual accuracy study for six straight years through 2024, becoming the first non-consensus system ever to beat blended-consensus projections ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/2025/02/most-accurate-fantasy-baseball-projections-2024-results/)), which is suggestive but not conclusive evidence that Statcast-informed modeling measurably helps, since THE BAT X is a bundled, partially black-box system.

The empirical Bayes/hierarchical Bayes literature tells the same story at a different scale. Efron & Morris's 1975 James-Stein estimator cut total squared error for batting average from 0.086 (raw mean) to 0.027 versus no shrinkage at all — a roughly 3.2x improvement that is universal across every study reviewed ([Efron & Morris](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios602/MultilevelData/EfronMorrisJASA1975.pdf)). But the marginal gain from *simple* closed-form shrinkage to a *fully hierarchical* model compresses sharply: Jensen, McShane & Wyner's 2009 three-level Bayesian hierarchy (position effects, ballpark effects, cubic-spline age curves, a hidden Markov elite/non-elite mixture, estimated via Gibbs sampling) beat a naive baseline by 36% internally but **lost to PECOTA on aggregate RMSE (7.33 vs. 7.11) and only modestly beat Marcel (7.82)** when tested against 118 elite hitters — its one clear edge was among players aged ≤26, where it won 62% of head-to-head comparisons against both commercial systems ([arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)). Removing just the elite/non-elite mixture degraded RMSE by roughly 30%, more than removing the position/park covariates — meaning the single highest-leverage structural addition is letting shrinkage amount vary by latent subgroup, not adding more covariates. Brown's 2008 *Annals of Applied Statistics* field test reinforces the same lesson from a different angle: a sophisticated nonparametric empirical Bayes procedure won on the full heterogeneous player population but **lost to simpler parametric methods once the population was split into homogeneous subgroups** ([arXiv:0803.3697](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3697)) — evidence that homogeneity of the shrinkage target matters more than estimator sophistication. For the project's existing closed-form EB shrinkage on wOBA/ISO/xFIP/K%/BB%, this ladder maps cleanly onto adoption cost: shrinking toward a park- or position-adjusted mean instead of one global mean, or adding a two-component mixture to stop over-shrinking elite performers, is achievable **without MCMC** (Brown's regression-EB variants and David Robinson's beta-binomial-regression extension remain closed-form) and is a **small addition**; full Jensen-et-al.-style hierarchical modeling with B-splines, HMM elite-status transitions, and Gibbs sampling is a **large architectural change** whose payoff is real but concentrated in young players rather than a uniform win. Notably, no peer-reviewed hierarchical Bayes paper targets ERA, FIP, K%, or BB% directly as an outcome — the pitching side of this literature is thin, and any extension needs separately-calibrated prior variances per stat regardless of architecture, since K% is markedly more ability-driven (lower year-to-year volatility) than batting average and would be over-shrunk by batting-calibrated weights.

## Tree ensembles are a cheap swap; pitch-sequence transformers are a new department

The most rigorously controlled machine-learning result in this entire research base is a chronological backtest (train 2015–2019, holdout 2025) of Statcast-enriched LightGBM against Marcel, which beat Marcel's batter wOBA MAE by **12.1%** (.0331→.0291) and pitcher xFIP MAE by 4.0%, winning in 6 of 6 backtested batter-years ([dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)). A FanGraphs Community study found a shallow neural net classifying wOBA above/below .320 improved from 69.19% to 81.31% accuracy once exit velocity and barrel rate were added to plate-discipline features alone ([FanGraphs Community](https://community.fangraphs.com/predicting-woba-using-process-based-statistics/)). Both results point the same direction: swapping the regression/shrinkage step for gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost) on the same rolling box-score features is a **small, low-risk addition** — a model-library change, not a new pipeline — and adding Statcast contact-quality features on top is **small-to-moderate**, since the data is free via Baseball Savant/`pybaseball` from 2015 onward and the real cost is feature engineering, not acquisition ([pybaseball docs](https://github.com/jldbc/pybaseball/blob/master/docs/statcast.md)). Sequence and transformer architectures are a different proposition: the peer-reviewed LSTM-vs-ZiPS home-run paper (Sun, Lin & Tsai, 2022/2023) reported 10–13% exact-match accuracy beating ZiPS, concentrated in sub-30-HR players ([arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654)), but a more methodologically careful open-source ablation building GRU/transformer models over plate-appearance sequences found, across three random seeds, that sequence architecture matched a plain MLP within noise — "without pretraining, the sequence adds nothing" ([GitHub Issue #414](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/issues/414)). Where sequence models *do* show convincing, reproducible gains — Heaton & Mitra's Penn State contextual-event-embedding work, which produced single-game strikeout/hit predictions competitive with major sportsbooks from just 10 games of pitch-level history ([Sloan](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/learning-contextual-event-embeddings-to-predict-player-performance-in-the-mlb)) — the gain traces to pitch-level data granularity, not the architecture itself. This is a **large architectural change**: it requires standing up event-level Statcast ingestion and embedding infrastructure the baseline doesn't have.

Pitching-specific analytics reinforce the same granularity-over-architecture lesson but add a sharper warning about context confounding. Baseball Prospectus's most rigorous head-to-head of ERA estimators found context-adjusted mixed-model metrics beating both the FIP family and raw pitch-quality metrics on year-ahead predictive correlation: DRA 0.26, cFIP 0.25, xwOBA/xERA 0.23, xFIP 0.19, FIP 0.19, SIERA 0.18 ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/82426/prospectus-feature-an-updated-evaluation-of-hitting-and-pitching-including-stuff-metrics/)). Critically, the same analysis found raw Stuff+ correlates with next-year run prevention at 0.41 for pitchers who stayed on the same team but only 0.14 for pitchers who switched teams — strong evidence that **Stuff+-family models conflate team/park/catcher context with portable pitcher skill**, a caveat for any team adopting it as a standalone predictor. This produces a clean three-tier cost structure directly relevant to the baseline's xFIP component: SIERA/xFIP require no new data (closed-form formula over existing leaderboard rate stats, a **trivial addition**); DRA/cFIP-style mixed-effects estimators need play-by-play participant context (batter, catcher, umpire, park) — a **moderate addition**; and Stuff+-family pitch-quality models plus all pitch-sequencing research (directed-graph embeddings over 3.5 million pitches, a 2026 Transformer counterfactual-optimization paper estimating sequencing effects could shift K/9 by more than 1.0) require full pitch-by-pitch Statcast ingestion — a **large architectural change** ([Sloan graph-embeddings paper](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/decoding-mlb-pitch-sequencing-strategies-via-directed-graph-embeddings); [arXiv:2606.17345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17345)). A separate finding should temper expectations about Statcast's own "expected stats": Baseball Prospectus found xwOBA's next-year predictive correlation (r=.35) essentially tied with FIP (r=.36) and DRA (r=.36) ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/40026/prospectus-feature-siren-song-statcasts-expected-metrics/)), and FanGraphs called the first-half-xwOBA-to-second-half-wOBA relationship "feeble" at r=.406 ([FanGraphs](https://fantasy.fangraphs.com/the-in-season-predictiveness-of-xwoba/)) — meaning the real lift comes from adding Statcast contact-quality features as inputs to a regression/ML model, not from swapping the target stat to its "expected" equivalent.

## Postseason simulation has a known blind spot: player-level granularity

The project's Monte Carlo bracket simulator, which samples team-level win probabilities round by round, is methodologically a simplification of the one peer-reviewed MLB postseason simulation paper found: Rudelius (2012, JQAS) simulates playoff series from the bottom up, modeling each plate appearance as a multinomial outcome over single/double/triple/HR/walk events using Bill James's log5 matchup formula to combine individual batter and pitcher rates before aggregating up through games and series ([JQAS](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/1559-0410.1344/html?lang=en)). Adopting this full player-level approach would be a **large architectural change** — a new data layer for lineup construction, per-plate-appearance event sampling, and a log5 matchup engine — not an incremental addition, and no study was found simulating the *current* expanded-bracket format (3-game Wild Card, 5-game LDS, 7-game LCS/WS) end-to-end with validated player-level inputs. On the question of whether team-strength inputs (Elo, Pythagorean) need adjustment for short-series variance, the research suggests the project's existing architecture is already doing the mathematically correct thing: Monte Carlo sampling of individual games under a fixed per-game probability already produces the correct series-level variance implied by series length, and the compounding math itself (a 55% single-game favorite becomes roughly a 61% series favorite in a best-of-7, but only a 2.5-point amplification in a best-of-3 Wild Card round) requires no external correction factor ([Math Goes Pop!](https://www.mathgoespop.com/2011/10/playoff-probabilities); [EconOne](https://econone.com/resources/blogs/brian-krieglers-statistical-take-on-mlb-playoffs/)).

Two specific, directly implementable findings stand out as **moderate additions** worth layering onto the existing per-game simulator rather than requiring a rebuild. A SABR study of 27 best-of-seven postseason series (2014–2022, 880 relief appearances) found a .076 wOBA-against gap between "rested" and fatigued reliever appearances ([SABR](https://sabr.org/journal/article/balancing-starter-and-bullpen-workloads-in-a-seven-game-postseason-series/)) — a concrete parameter a simulator could apply as a win-probability penalty by tracking each reliever's days-rest/batters-faced state across series games, additive state-tracking rather than a redesign. And on home-field advantage, ZiPS's widely cited .535 home-win-probability-for-equal-teams parameter is close to the Wild Card-era (1995–2024) aggregate of .540 ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/does-home-field-advantage-really-evaporate-in-october/)), though five-year rolling postseason home win% has ranged from .517 to .581 historically — volatile enough that a simulator should treat HFA as a small fixed constant rather than a precisely-era-tuned figure, making any refinement here a **small addition**.

## Betting-engine upgrades: cheap devig swap, principled Kelly, skeptical CLV

The market-efficiency literature delivers a blunt verdict for the odds engine: MLB moneyline and totals markets are close to efficient by closing time. A 2024/2025 *Applied Economics* paper brute-force-tested 1,547 simple MLB moneyline strategies (1999–2016) and found only 2.46% profitable at 5% significance and 0.45% at 1% — rates consistent with pure chance after multiple-testing correction ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115)). Where real, replicated inefficiency survives, it clusters in lower-liquidity corners: the MLB season win-totals market shows bettors overreacting to prior-season record ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x)), and Jay Simon's 2024 *Management Science* study of 3,681 games found MLB closing lines are "mostly reliable" but exhibit statistically significant negative autocorrelation — overreaction that partially reverses and "could be exploited by sophisticated bettors" ([Management Science](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html)). This means the odds engine's job is less about finding a dramatic edge and more about honest calibration and risk discipline around whatever small edge the probability model produces.

Within that frame, three concrete upgrades are supported by evidence stronger than the project's current baseline. First, **devigging**: Shin's method and the power method both reliably beat proportional devigging on calibration in peer-reviewed comparisons — Štrumbelj (2014) found Shin's probabilities more accurate than basic normalization "for all bookmaker/sport pairs tested" across tennis, horse racing, and greyhound markets ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533)) — though no MLB-specific study was found testing either against proportional devigging head-to-head, and the Shin/power advantage is known to shrink in larger, more liquid markets like MLB moneylines. Both are drop-in swaps: for a two-outcome MLB market, Shin reduces to a closed-form additive calculation, and the power method requires only a single-parameter numerical solve, both available as off-the-shelf library calls (R `implied`, Python `shin`) with no new data inputs — a **small addition**. Second, **Kelly staking**: Chu, Wu & Swartz (2018, JQAS) explicitly criticize fixed fractions like quarter-Kelly as having "no theoretical underpinnings" ([De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jqas-2017-0122/html?lang=en)), and Baker & McHale (2013) derive a formal shrinkage factor that discounts the Kelly stake based on the *measured* uncertainty of each probability estimate rather than a constant multiplier, validated on simulation and real tennis data ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262425087_Optimal_Betting_Under_Parameter_Uncertainty_Improving_the_Kelly_Criterion)) — a **moderate addition**, since it requires the probability model to output or the engine to estimate a variance around each win-probability estimate. Third, **drawdown-constrained dynamic Kelly** (Busseti, Ryu & Boyd) embeds the drawdown constraint directly inside the sizing optimization rather than bolting a separate stop-loss onto a fixed fraction, reportedly outperforming fractional Kelly "for the same drawdown risk level or growth rate" ([arXiv:1603.06183](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06183)), with sports-betting simulation evidence showing a drawdown-modified variant achieving 0% ruin while retaining strong growth ([arXiv:2107.08827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.08827)) — but this is a **large architectural change**, replacing the sizing-plus-stop-loss control flow with a convex optimization solved per bet, and none of this Kelly literature is MLB-specific; all validation is borrowed from tennis, horse racing, basketball, soccer, and football. Finally, Closing Line Value — which the project may be tempted to track as a quality signal — turns out to be industry terminology with no peer-reviewed validation: no paper regresses bettor ROI on measured CLV, and the one rigorous independent check (Karl Whelan's 3,670-game NBA decile analysis) found three of five positive-CLV deciles actually lost money ([Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/sportsbetting/the-truth-about-closing-line-value/)). CLV should be logged as a soft diagnostic only, never wired into automated sizing decisions.

## Conclusion

The pattern that recurs across all five domains — win probability, projection, pitching, postseason, and markets — is that the baseline methods under discussion were each, in their own era, deliberately engineered to be a hard-to-beat floor (Marcel explicitly so, Elo and Pythagenpat implicitly so by virtue of decades of refinement), and the literature confirms they have mostly succeeded at that goal: every fancier alternative tested against them wins by single digits, loses outright on some metric, or wins only in a narrow subpopulation (young players, specific bookmakers, specific seasons). The more useful implication for a roadmap is that the project's highest-leverage next moves are not "replace X with a fancier Y" but "add a well-evidenced, narrowly-scoped correction layer to X" — a team-quality adjustment to in-game win probability, a park/position-adjusted or mixture-aware prior to the EB shrinkage step, Statcast contact-quality features bolted onto the existing xFIP/wOBA targets, Shin or power devigging in place of proportional, and parameter-uncertainty shrinkage in the Kelly engine — while treating the genuinely large investments (pitch-sequence transformers, player-level postseason simulation, risk-constrained dynamic Kelly) as a separate research track whose payoff depends entirely on committing to new data infrastructure (pitch-by-pitch ingestion, lineup-level simulation, measured probability uncertainty) rather than on choosing a cleverer algorithm.
