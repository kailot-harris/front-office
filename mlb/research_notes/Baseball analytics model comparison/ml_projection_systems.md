# ML baseball projections nibble at Marcel's edges

Machine learning has not dethroned traditional MLB projection systems — it has quietly colonized their margins. Across academic papers, Sloan/SaberSeminar conference work, and independent technical writeups, gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) and classical ensembles (random forest) remain the dominant algorithms for season-level hitter and pitcher rate-stat projection, while sequence models (LSTM/GRU/transformers) show real but inconsistent gains concentrated in narrow, skewed targets like home-run counts rather than full stat lines. The one rigorous time-series backtest found in this research — a LightGBM model enriched with Statcast features beating Marcel by **12.1% on batter wOBA mean absolute error** ([dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)) — is the strongest evidence that Statcast-plus-ML meaningfully improves on a pure box-score baseline, but it sits alongside a broader literature (Baseball Prospectus's xBA/xwOBA correlation study, the sabermiketrics decision-forest study, the New College of Florida pitcher-ERA thesis) consistently finding that "expected stats" and pitch-tracking features add only modest, sometimes contested, incremental signal once multi-year regression-to-the-mean is already in place. Commercial systems split the difference: Marcel, Steamer, and classic ZiPS remain transparent regression-plus-aging-curve engines with no disclosed ML core, PECOTA is fundamentally a k-nearest-neighbor comparable-player system, and only **THE BAT X** — the system that has led FantasyPros' accuracy rankings for six straight years through 2024 ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/2025/02/most-accurate-fantasy-baseball-projections-2024-results/)) — explicitly fuses Statcast pitch-tracking data with disclosed machine-learning sub-components. Peer-reviewed MLB-specific deep learning work is thin and recent (2021–2025), concentrated in a handful of arXiv/journal papers and a sustained Penn State research thread at Sloan, while most "ML beats traditional system" headlines trace back to a single 2022/2023 LSTM paper benchmarked against ZiPS on home runs, not a comprehensive stat-line comparison. The practical takeaway for anyone building a pipeline: gradient boosting on rolling box-score-plus-Statcast features is a modest, low-risk upgrade to a traditional pipeline, while pitch-sequence transformers and contextual embeddings are a genuine architectural leap that only pays off when paired with event-level data most public projection systems don't use.

## Tree ensembles dominate tabular projection, but no algorithm family wins decisively

The clearest pattern across academic and quasi-academic sources is that **random forest, gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost), ridge regression, and shallow neural networks perform comparably** on tabular, season-level MLB prediction tasks — no single algorithm shows a decisive, consistent edge. The 2017 Stanford paper by Bierig, Hollenbeck, and Stroud, "Understanding Career Progression in Baseball Through Machine Learning" (arXiv:1712.05754, Dec 2017, peer status: arXiv preprint, not journal-reviewed), trained four parallel models — ridge regression, a multi-layer perceptron, a 100-tree random forest, and epsilon-SVR — on players with 7+ MLB seasons since 1970, predicting future-season WAR from first-six-season features. All four models explained roughly **60% of variance (R²≈0.60)** in future batter WAR and "handily beat" Tom Tango's simple Delta Method baseline, but no model clearly outperformed the others ([arXiv:1712.05754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.05754)). Pitcher models in the same paper only reached R²≈0.30–0.40, which the authors attribute to injury variance. A 2026 peer-reviewed KBO (Korean league, not MLB) paper in the *Journal of Information and Communication Convergence Engineering* directly pitted CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost against each other on 30 pitching features (ERA, WHIP, BB, SO) trained on 2009–2023 data and validated against 2024, ultimately selecting **XGBoost as the best overall performer** for batting (R²=0.933, Spearman=0.973), though pitching-specific accuracy figures were not retrievable from the available summary ([JICCE](https://www.jicce.org/journal/view.html?doi=10.56977%2Fjicce.2026.24.1.18)). For MLB specifically, the dominant pattern in non-peer-reviewed technical writeups — Towards Data Science's 2021 XGBoost pitching-projection piece training 11 separate single-target models (ERA, WHIP, K, etc.) on 2016–2019 data ([TDS](https://towardsdatascience.com/baseball-and-machine-learning-part-2-a-data-science-approach-to-2021-pitching-projections-530dbfe6dcc4/)), and the dev.to LightGBM/Marcel backtest — confirms XGBoost/LightGBM as the practitioner default for tabular rolling-stat projection, consistent with these algorithms' strong out-of-box performance on structured sports data generally.

A separate, smaller thread uses random forest or gradient boosting on biomechanical sensor data rather than traditional stats: a 2025 *Scientific Reports* paper (Shibata et al., peer-reviewed) used bat-mounted IMU sensors (bat speed, attack angle, rotational acceleration — not Statcast) to predict annual home run totals, with random forest achieving **test R²=0.56±0.08, RMSE=5.30±0.51 HR**, beating gradient boosting (R²=0.49) and linear regression (R²=0.52) on a small cross-player holdout of 295 training swings versus 30 test swings ([PMC12464232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12464232/)). This result is notable for using SHAP/partial-dependence interpretability tools but should be read cautiously given its very small sample. On the fantasy-sports-adjacent Kaggle "MLB Player Digital Engagement Forecasting" competition (2021) — which predicted fan engagement scores, not on-field stats — the top two solutions split cleanly along the tree-vs-sequence fault line: 1st place used a from-scratch GRU processing 96 days of raw sequential data with minimal feature engineering, while 2nd place used a blend of five LightGBM models plus one XGBoost model per target, relying on historical-target aggregates and date features ([Kaggle discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/c/mlb-player-digital-engagement-forecasting/discussion/274255)). This single, real-world instance is one of the only direct architecture-forks-on-identical-data examples found, and it shows sequence deep learning *can* win against gradient boosting given enough raw signal and compute — but it is one competition, predicting a fundamentally different (and probably easier-to-model-sequentially) target than season stat lines.

## Sequence models win on home runs, go quiet on rate stats

Deep learning's clearest documented success is narrow: Sun, Lin, and Tsai's 2022 arXiv paper (published in peer-reviewed form in *International Journal of Data Science and Analytics*, 2023 — the strongest peer-reviewed academic head-to-head found in this research) trained five LSTM variants (including GRU, BiLSTM, and Attention-LSTM) on 5,401 MLB players from 1961–2019 to predict home run totals, comparing against linear regression, SVM, random forest, and the **ZiPS** projection system ([arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41060-022-00313-4)). On the 2018–2019 holdout, LSTM models reported **10–13% exact-match accuracy** and **20–24% accuracy within ±1 home run**, outperforming ZiPS and the other baselines, especially for players hitting fewer than 30 HR — though all methods, LSTM included, struggled on elite power hitters (40+ HR). Precise MAE/RMSE deltas versus ZiPS could not be extracted from available summaries, a real limitation on how confidently this "LSTM beats ZiPS" claim can be quantified. Notably, no peer-reviewed or arXiv paper was found applying LSTM/RNN/transformer architectures directly to pitcher ERA/FIP/WHIP/K% as season-ahead targets using only traditional data — the one close analogue, "Pitcher Performance Prediction Major League Baseball (MLB) by Temporal Fusion Transformer" (Lee and Kim, *Computers, Materials & Continua*, May 2025, peer-reviewed), applies the Temporal Fusion Transformer architecture to pitcher ERA and reports it "consistently outperforming" RNN baselines and existing projection systems, but specific data granularity and quantitative deltas were not confirmed in available sources ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391148375_Pitcher_Performance_Prediction_Major_League_Baseball_MLB_by_Temporal_Fusion_Transformer)).

Beyond home runs, evidence for sequence architectures is genuinely mixed, and the most methodologically careful recent test found a **null result**. A Stanford CS229 course project (2019, not peer-reviewed) reported a stacked LSTM dramatically beating SVR on one-year-ahead OPS+ prediction (MSE 0.0019 vs. 0.088), but on a tiny 44-player test set ([CS229 PDF](https://cs229.stanford.edu/proj2019aut/data/assignment_308875_raw/26382632.pdf)). By contrast, an open-source GitHub project explicitly built GRU and transformer models over a hitter's last ~600 plate appearances with recency-aware features, and across three random seeds found the sequence models matched a plain MLP baseline within noise, concluding "without pretraining, the sequence adds nothing" ([GitHub Issue #414](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/issues/414)). A 2017 SaberSeminar talk by Kiri Oler using game-by-game LSTM modeling (non-peer-reviewed, covered by FanGraphs/The Hardball Times) found predictions clustered near averages and struggled with rare events like home runs and stolen bases ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/using-recurrent-neural-networks-to-predict-player-performance/)). The pattern across these results suggests that where sequence models show convincing gains — Connor Heaton and Prasenjit Mitra's Penn State research program (arXiv:2109.05280, 2021, plus Sloan conference papers in 2022 and 2023) treating pitch-by-pitch data as a language-model-style token sequence to build "contextual event embeddings," which produced single-game strikeout and hit predictions competitive with major sportsbooks using only 10 games of pitch-level history per player ([Sloan paper](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/learning-contextual-event-embeddings-to-predict-player-performance-in-the-mlb)) — the gain traces to *finer-grained input data* (pitch-level Statcast context) rather than the sequence architecture per se. Architecture and data granularity are consistently confounded in this literature: season-sequence LSTM on the same box-score stats shows disputed benefit; pitch-sequence transformers on genuinely granular Statcast event data show the strongest and most reproducible gains.

On distributional (vs. point-estimate) projections — the brief's interest in mixture density networks — this research found **no confirmed neural MDN applied to MLB hitter/pitcher projection**. MDNs are well-established for basketball trajectory prediction ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319209740_Applying_Deep_Bidirectional_LSTM_and_Mixture_Density_Network_for_Basketball_Trajectory_Prediction)), but baseball's distributional-projection work instead runs through classical Bayesian/compositional statistics: a January 2025 arXiv paper introduces a Nested Dirichlet Distribution model over plate-appearance outcome categories to study age-related performance decomposition (arXiv:2501.09153), and a nuclear-norm-penalized multinomial regression (arXiv:1706.10272, ~2017) outputs full categorical probability distributions over at-bat outcomes — both pre-deep-learning statistical-learning methods, not neural networks. This is a genuine white space: architectural novelty (deep learning) and distributional rigor (probabilistic outputs) are, in the literature found, pursued on separate tracks rather than combined.

## Marcel is the floor, and elaboration buys little

Understanding whether ML beats traditional systems requires first understanding what the traditional systems already are. **Marcel** (Tom Tango, 2004) is a deliberately minimal three-year-weighted-average-plus-regression-plus-age-adjustment formula, explicitly designed as "the minimum level of competence that you should expect from any forecaster" — a floor, not a target ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/tangotigers-projection-tests/)). **Steamer** (Jared Cross et al.) sits in the middle, deriving its weighting and regression amounts from historical regression analysis rather than fixed weights. **ZiPS** (Dan Szymborski) layers aging curves, comparable-player matching, and Monte Carlo simulation on top of a four-season weighted base. **PECOTA** (Nate Silver, 2002–2003, rebuilt entirely in 2020) is architecturally distinct — a k-nearest-neighbor comparable-player match across a historical MLB/minor-league database rather than a parametric regression — and its post-2020 "completely new engine" internals are undisclosed. **THE BAT X** (Derek Carty, ~2020) is the one system in this group documented as directly incorporating Statcast pitch-tracking data (via a proprietary "stuff model," THE BATcast) and machine learning for specific sub-outputs like DFS ownership projections, though Carty has never published a technical methodology paper — available documentation is marketing copy and social-media threads, not a disclosed architecture ([RotoGrinders](https://rotogrinders.com/pages/the-bat-x-projections-the-bat-plus-statcast-3361569)).

Two multi-year accuracy benchmarks anchor how close these systems actually are. Dan Meyer's 2014 Hardball Times study measured weighted MAE on projected wOBA across Marcel, ZiPS, Oliver, PECOTA, and Steamer: **ZiPS .0274 (best), Steamer .0277, PECOTA .0279, Oliver .0280, Marcel .0289 (worst)** — a spread of just 0.0015, leading Meyer to conclude "all these projections do a fine job and the differences between them are subtle" ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/evaluating-the-2014-projection-systems/)). A decade later, FantasyPros' annual z-score-based accuracy study (running since ~2019) found **THE BAT X most accurate for six consecutive years**, becoming in 2024 the first *original* (non-consensus) system to top even blended-consensus rankings, scoring -1.874 versus Steamer's -0.649 and ZiPS's -0.138 ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/2025/02/most-accurate-fantasy-baseball-projections-2024-results/)). Because THE BAT X is the only system here with a confirmed, if partially undisclosed, ML/Statcast component, its sustained top ranking is suggestive — though not proof, since it is a bundled system — that Statcast-informed, partially ML-driven modeling does measurably outperform pure traditional-regression systems in practice. Tango's own characterization of the broader pattern is blunt: more elaborate systems "have only added a small increase in accuracy" over Marcel "despite doing substantially more work" ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/tangotigers-projection-tests/)) — a claim corroborated independently by Beyond the Box Score's observation that ZiPS/Steamer/PECOTA's added pitch-level data and ML components "only marginally outperform Marcel on aggregate accuracy" ([Beyond the Box Score](https://www.beyondtheboxscore.com/2016/2/22/11079186/projections-marcel-pecota-zips-steamer-explained-guide-math-is-fun)). No source in this research found a direct, numeric ML-model-vs-PECOTA, ML-vs-Steamer, or ML-vs-THE BAT accuracy comparison — the only rigorous ML-vs-named-system benchmark is the Sun/Lin/Tsai LSTM-vs-ZiPS home-run comparison, a single-stat result that does not generalize to full stat-line projection.

## Statcast adds a real but bounded edge, mostly through contact-quality features

Every controlled source that tested adding Statcast features to a box-score-only model found measurable improvement, but the magnitude varies by task and none approaches a dramatic leap. The cleanest classification-level result comes from a FanGraphs Community writeup (Michal Kokta, 2021, not peer-reviewed): a shallow neural network classifying whether a batter-season's wOBA exceeds .320 improved from **69.19% accuracy using plate-discipline features alone to 81.31%** once exit velocity and barrel rate were added — a 12-point gain ([FanGraphs Community](https://community.fangraphs.com/predicting-woba-using-process-based-statistics/)). At the regression level, the dev.to LightGBM-vs-Marcel backtest (strict chronological 2015–2024 training/tuning, 2025 holdout) found Statcast-enriched LightGBM beat Marcel on batter wOBA MAE by **12.1% (0.0331 → 0.0291)**, winning in all six backtested years for batters (five of six for pitchers, where the improvement was a smaller 4.0% on xFIP) ([dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)). An undergraduate thesis at New College of Florida comparing Box Score, Run Estimator, Raw Statcast, and Enhanced Statcast feature domains for next-season pitcher ERA found the best hybrid model (blending sp-stuff, K/9, pitch-type diversity, Hard%, and workload) reached only **R²≈0.27** ([NCF thesis](https://digitalcommons.ncf.edu/theses_etds/6736/)), while a fan-analyst blog study on 1,548 player-seasons found a decision-forest model's wOBA RMSE improved only modestly, from 0.044 to 0.036, prompting the author's own comment on "how hard it is to predict performance the next season" ([sabermiketrics](https://sabermiketrics.blogspot.com/2021/10/how-predictive-are-statcast-metrics.html)). These bounded gains are consistent with the broader finding that Statcast's own flagship "expected stats" — xwOBA, xBA, xSLG, themselves an ML (nearest-neighbor similarity-matching) layer built into Baseball Savant — do **not** clearly outperform traditional metrics as forward predictors: Baseball Prospectus's 2018 comparison found xwOBA (r=.35) performing no better than FIP (r=.36) or DRA (r=.36) at predicting next-season outcomes, concluding "little evidence that metrics like xBA or xwOBA provide a uniquely better measurement of probable...skill" ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/40026/prospectus-feature-siren-song-statcasts-expected-metrics/)), and FanGraphs' own in-season study found first-half xwOBA predicting second-half actual wOBA at only r=.406 for hitters, calling the relationship "feeble" ([FanGraphs](https://fantasy.fangraphs.com/the-in-season-predictiveness-of-xwoba/)). The metrics that do show the most consistent individual predictive value are contact-quality aggregates — barrel rate (r=.609–.775 with future home runs) and max exit velocity, described as "the most stable skill indicator" ([sabermiketrics](https://sabermiketrics.blogspot.com/2021/10/how-predictive-are-statcast-metrics.html)) — and, for pitchers, velocity and spin rate, which dominate SHAP feature-importance rankings in injury-prediction models (accuracy 0.84, AUC 0.66, on 3,808 pitcher-seasons) even more clearly than they do in performance-forecasting models ([PMC11369970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11369970/)). Data access itself is not a meaningful barrier: Statcast pitch-level data is free via Baseball Savant from 2015 onward, with the open-source `pybaseball` package handling extraction and automatic query-chunking around Savant's per-request result caps — meaning the real engineering cost is feature engineering (aggregating pitch-level rows into barrel rate, Hard-Hit%, or "stuff" composites), not data procurement ([pybaseball docs](https://github.com/jldbc/pybaseball/blob/master/docs/statcast.md)).

## Adoption framework: small swap or new pipeline?

Synthesizing architecture, data requirements, and reported accuracy across every method surveyed yields a practical adoption ladder for a team moving beyond a traditional stats-based pipeline, summarized below with publication years and peer-review status noted throughout.

| Method | Core idea | Validation target | Reported accuracy | Peer-reviewed? | Adoption scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random forest / XGBoost / LightGBM on rolling box-score stats (2017–2026, multiple sources) | Tree ensemble on season-level rolling aggregates, same features traditional systems use | WAR, ERA, wOBA, HR | R²≈0.60 (batter WAR, arXiv:1712.05754); R²≈0.27 (pitcher ERA, NCF thesis) | Mixed: arXiv:1712.05754 is a preprint; KBO JICCE paper is peer-reviewed; TDS/dev.to are blogs | **Small** — drop-in model swap on existing data |
| XGBoost/LightGBM + Statcast contact-quality features (2021–2026, dev.to, FanGraphs Community) | Same tree ensemble, add exit velocity/barrel rate/Hard-Hit% | wOBA, xFIP | +12.1% MAE reduction vs. Marcel (batters); +12pt classification accuracy (FanGraphs NN) | No — all non-peer-reviewed blogs/community writeups | **Small-to-moderate** — needs Statcast ingestion (free, public) plus feature engineering |
| Season-sequence LSTM/GRU on standard stats (2019–2022, CS229, arXiv:2206.09654) | Treat player's prior N seasons as a sequence instead of independent rows | HR, OPS+ | LSTM beat ZiPS 10–13% exact-match HR accuracy; disputed by null ablation elsewhere | arXiv:2206.09654 is peer-reviewed (2023 journal version); CS229 is a course project | **Small architecturally**, same data as Marcel/ZiPS — but evidence of benefit is contested |
| Pitch-sequence transformers / contextual event embeddings (2021–2023, Heaton & Mitra, Penn State) | Treat pitch-by-pitch data as language-model token sequence, learn player-form embeddings | Single-game K, hit outcomes | Competitive with major sportsbooks using 10 games of pitch data | Sloan conference (juried, not journal peer-review) | **Large** — requires event-level Statcast ingestion, embedding infrastructure |
| Temporal Fusion Transformer for pitcher ERA (2025, Lee & Kim) | Multi-horizon attention-based forecasting with variable-selection networks | ERA | Reported to "consistently outperform" RNNs and projection systems | Yes — *Computers, Materials & Continua*, 2025 | **Large** — complex architecture; input granularity unconfirmed |
| Bayesian/Dirichlet compositional models (2025, arXiv:2501.09153) and nuclear-penalized multinomial regression (~2017, arXiv:1706.10272) | Classical statistical (non-neural) distributional modeling of outcome composition | Plate-appearance outcome categories | Not fully extractable (PDF parsing failures) | arXiv preprints, not journal-reviewed | **Small** — standard outcome-count data, different statistical paradigm not deep learning |
| Nearest-neighbor comparable-player matching (PECOTA, 2002–2003; k-NN xwOBA, Medium) | Match player to statistically similar historical players/batted balls | Rate stats, total bases | PECOTA undisclosed post-2020; KNN xwOBA claimed 76% accuracy (unverified, 403 on source) | No (PECOTA proprietary; Medium post unverified) | **Moderate** — needs large historical comparable database |

The highest-confidence, lowest-risk move for a traditional pipeline is the first and second rows: swapping Marcel/ZiPS-style regression for gradient boosting on the same or lightly-Statcast-enriched feature set, which is where the only rigorously backtested, peer-review-adjacent accuracy gain in this entire research base was found (the 12.1% MAE improvement). Sequence and transformer architectures remain a frontier with genuine promise but thin, inconsistent, and frequently non-peer-reviewed evidence — and a conspicuous null result exists for the specific claim that sequence architecture alone (without richer data) beats a tabular baseline.

## Conclusion

The weight of evidence inverts the intuitive assumption that fancier models necessarily win: the architectural choice (tree ensemble vs. neural sequence model) matters less than the data-granularity choice (box-score aggregates vs. Statcast contact-quality features vs. raw pitch-by-pitch sequences), and even the best-documented Statcast-plus-ML gains top out around a 12% error reduction over a 2004-vintage baseline that was deliberately built to be beatable. This suggests MLB performance has an intrinsic predictability ceiling — most studies converge on R²≈0.2–0.3 for pitcher stats and ≈0.6 for aggregate hitter value — that neither traditional regression-to-the-mean nor modern ML has broken through, making further gains likely to come from richer event-level data (pitch sequences, biomechanics, bat-tracking) rather than from swapping algorithms on the same inputs. For a research or production team already running a traditional stats pipeline, the evidence supports a two-speed roadmap: adopt gradient boosting with Statcast contact-quality features now as a low-risk, modestly-accretive upgrade, while treating pitch-sequence transformers and contextual embeddings as a genuinely separate, higher-cost research track whose payoff depends entirely on committing to event-level data infrastructure — a track where the strongest results (Heaton and Mitra's Sloan-presented work, the Lee and Kim 2025 TFT paper) remain conference-juried or recent-journal results rather than an established, widely-replicated consensus.
