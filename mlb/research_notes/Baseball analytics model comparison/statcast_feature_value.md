# Incremental Value of Statcast/Pitch-Tracking Features in ML Projection Models for MLB Hitters and Pitchers

## Are there controlled comparisons (same algorithm, box-score-only vs. +Statcast) reporting quantified accuracy changes?

### Takeaway
Direct, rigorously-controlled "same-algorithm, with-vs-without-Statcast" ablation studies are rare and mostly exist as unpublished/undergraduate theses or blog-level analyses rather than peer-reviewed papers; where they exist, reported improvements are real but modest (R² in the 0.2–0.3 range for season-ahead stats, not dramatic leaps), and professional projection systems that add Statcast/pitch-level data (ZiPS, Steamer, THE BAT X) only "marginally" beat simpler systems like Marcel on aggregate accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- An undergraduate thesis, "Beyond Box Scores: Statcast's Impact on Predicting Pitcher Performance" (Ryan Fortune, New College of Florida), explicitly compared four feature domains — Box Score, Run Estimators (e.g., FIP-type), Raw Statcast, and Enhanced Statcast — using Linear Regression, Support Vector Regression (SVR), and XGBoost to predict next-season ERA. The best model (hybrid SVR using sp-stuff, K-per-9, pitch-type diversity, Hard%, and innings-per-appearance) achieved a test-set R² of approximately 0.27, described by the author as "substantially exceeding the performance of any single-domain or traditional baseline model," though the publicly available abstract does not give side-by-side RMSE/R² numbers for each feature domain individually — [Digital Commons @ New College of Florida](https://digitalcommons.ncf.edu/theses_etds/6736/).
- A fan-analyst blog study (sabermiketrics, Oct. 31, 2021) examined 1,548 player-seasons (2017–2021, 200+ PA) and reported that a decision-forest model predicting next-year wOBA achieved RMSE of 0.036 vs. a 0.044 baseline — a "modest improvement" — and explicitly noted "it is still disappointing...how hard it is to predict performance the next season" — [sabermiketrics blog](https://sabermiketrics.blogspot.com/2021/10/how-predictive-are-statcast-metrics.html) (2021; not peer-reviewed).
- Complex, Statcast/pitch-level-informed projection systems (ZiPS, Steamer, PECOTA) layer in pitch-level data, minor-league translations, and machine learning but "only marginally outperform Marcel" — a simple box-score-only regression-to-the-mean system — on aggregate accuracy, per general projection-system literature surfaced in search — [RotoGrinders/various projection system writeups](https://rotogrinders.com/the-bat) (date of underlying claim unclear; treat as a qualitative/aggregated claim, not a single controlled study).
- FanGraphs' own comparison noted that preseason Steamer (box-score-history-based) projections "tell you more about rest-of-season performance than one month of Statcast data would," with preseason Steamer averaging about .037 points of wOBAcon error — implying that a small sample of Statcast data is not automatically superior to an established box-score-trained projection, though this is a sample-size argument rather than a pure feature-ablation study — [FanGraphs, "Can One Month of Statcast Data Be Used to Evaluate Hitters?"](https://community.fangraphs.com/can-one-month-of-statcast-data-be-used-to-evaluate-hitters/).
- THE BAT X (Derek Carty's projection system) explicitly layers a "Stuff+"-style model built on Statcast pitch-tracking data (velocity, movement, spin) on top of THE BAT's base projections and is cited by FantasyPros/FanGraphs accuracy roundups as among the most accurate standalone systems, but no controlled ablation (THE BAT vs. THE BAT X holding all else equal) with specific RMSE/R² deltas was found in public sources — [RotoGrinders, "THE BAT X Projection System"](https://rotogrinders.com/the-bat); [FantasyPros, "Most Accurate Fantasy Baseball Projections"](https://www.fantasypros.com/2024/01/most-accurate-fantasy-baseball-projections-2023/) (2023/2024/2026 editions).
- A study on predicting hits using Statcast batted-ball data reported overall classification accuracy ranging from 67.7% to 91.1% depending on model configuration, with best models showing ~85.8% sensitivity/precision — but this is a same-game hit-probability model (contact quality → hit outcome), not a season-ahead projection, so it measures descriptive/contemporaneous power rather than forecasting improvement — [FanGraphs/THT, "Using Statcast Data to Predict Hits"](https://tht.fangraphs.com/using-statcast-data-to-predict-hits/).

### Inferences
- The strongest, most carefully controlled evidence available (the NCF thesis) suggests Statcast features add real but limited incremental predictive power for pitcher ERA forecasting (R² ~0.27 ceiling), consistent with the broader finding across sources that year-ahead MLB performance is intrinsically noisy and no single feature set — box score or Statcast — gets much above R²≈0.2–0.3.
- The fact that complex ML/Statcast-infused systems "only marginally" beat Marcel suggests diminishing returns: most of the predictable signal in MLB performance is already captured by multi-year box-score regression-to-the-mean, and Statcast adds a smaller marginal layer on top.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, replicable study was found that reports a clean, single-number RMSE/MAE/R² delta for "box-score-only XGBoost" vs. "box-score+Statcast XGBoost" on the same train/test split for hitter projections (SLG/wOBA). The closest (NCF thesis, pitcher ERA) does not publicly disclose per-domain comparison numbers in the available abstract/summary — only the best combined-model R².
- No controlled comparison was found isolating sprint speed specifically as an incremental ML feature for projecting future performance (e.g., baserunning value, stolen bases, or defensive metrics).

## What specific Statcast metrics have been shown most predictive for future performance?

### Takeaway
Among hitter Statcast metrics, barrel rate and exit velocity (especially max/average exit velocity and Hard-Hit%) are consistently cited as the most stable, most predictive "quality of contact" metrics for future power output; for pitchers, pitch velocity, spin rate, and "stuff"-type composite models (plus pitch-type diversity and strikeout rate) show up as top features in both performance- and injury-prediction models — but strikeout rate and plate-discipline metrics from traditional box scores remain highly (sometimes more) predictive in direct comparisons.

### Cited Findings
- "Barrel rate is the most predictive power metric," with a correlation to future home runs of r = .609, rising to r = .775 when considering power output within the same season — [mlbprediction.com, Statcast Metrics for Betting guide](https://mlbprediction.com/statcast-metrics-analytics-guide.html) (undated qualitative aggregator source; corroborating claim, not an original study).
- In the sabermiketrics year-over-year analysis (2017–2021 data, published Oct. 2021), max exit velocity was described as "the most stable skill indicator," barrel rate as the "strongest current-year performance predictor," and hard-hit rate/average exit velocity showed "solid year-over-year retention." However, for predicting next-year wOBA specifically, xwOBA outperformed actual wOBA, and plain slugging percentage outperformed all batted-ball metrics tested, while strikeout rate showed "minimal predictive value" in that comparison — [sabermiketrics blog](https://sabermiketrics.blogspot.com/2021/10/how-predictive-are-statcast-metrics.html) (2021).
- In the NCF pitcher-ERA thesis, the top 5 features of the best-performing model were sp-stuff (a Statcast-derived "stuff" composite), K-per-9 (box score), pitch-type diversity (Statcast-derived), Hard% (Statcast), and innings-per-appearance (box score/workload) — i.e., the best model blended both domains roughly evenly rather than relying on Statcast alone — [Digital Commons @ NCF thesis](https://digitalcommons.ncf.edu/theses_etds/6736/) (2020s, exact year not confirmed in fetched content).
- For pitcher injury prediction (XGBoost, 3,808 pitcher-seasons 2017–2022, published Aug. 2024 in Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine), the top SHAP-ranked features were: mean pitch velocity (all pitch types), fastball spin rate, fastball horizontal movement, slider usage percentage, and player age. Accuracy was 0.84 (95% CI 0.83–0.85) and AUC was 0.66 (95% CI 0.60–0.71) for predicting a shoulder/elbow injury the following season. The authors state pitch-tracking metrics "substantially outperformed demographic and workload variables" for this task, and found an interaction effect where fastball velocity >95 mph combined with high slider usage created substantially greater predicted risk, while velocity <95 mph with high slider usage was protective — [PMC, "Pitch-Tracking Metrics as a Predictor of Future Shoulder and Elbow Injuries in MLB Pitchers," Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine, Aug. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11369970/).
- Related search results referenced a "radar gun fails" / pitcher fatigue analysis noting that simple velocity-drop triggers (1.5 mph drop) produce a 41% false-alarm rate for pitcher fatigue, and even a 3 mph threshold has a 22% false-alarm rate, implying raw velocity decline alone is a noisy/weak standalone predictor without a broader ML feature set — [Sportsnaut, "Why the Radar Gun Fails"](https://sportsnaut.com/mlb/predict-pitcher-fatigue-machine-learning) (date not confirmed from search snippet).
- A separate study on bat speed found that bat-speed-derived statistics "dominate" other features (including biomechanical measures) when predicting a hitter's own exit velocity, suggesting bat speed (now tracked by Statcast as of 2023+) is a strong upstream predictor of exit velocity itself — [Medium, "Predicting Hitter Exit Velocity: The Importance of Bat Speed"](https://medium.com/@toddkawahara/predicting-hitter-exit-velocity-the-importance-of-bat-speed-6d59a25d368c) (date not confirmed).

### Inferences
- Across both hitter and pitcher contexts, the most consistently "predictive" Statcast metrics are contact-quality aggregates (barrel rate, exit velocity, Hard-Hit%) and pitch-characteristic aggregates (velocity, spin rate, "stuff" composites) rather than single raw Statcast numbers in isolation — composite/engineered features (e.g., sp-stuff, barrel rate as a rate stat) tend to outperform raw per-pitch data points.
- For injury prediction specifically, pitch-tracking data appears to add more unambiguous incremental value (accuracy 0.84, clear top-feature dominance) than it does for performance/production forecasting, where the added value over box-score aggregates is smaller and sometimes statistically indistinguishable.

### Gaps
- No source directly addressed sprint speed as a predictive ML feature for future stolen-base totals, defensive runs saved, or aging curves.
- No source gave a clean quantitative answer on how much spin-rate decline specifically (isolated from velocity) predicts future ERA/injury in a published, peer-reviewed study; evidence is inferential (spin rate appears in SHAP top-5 for injury, not isolated for ERA decline).

## Does Statcast "expected stats" (xwOBA, xBA, xSLG, xERA) outperform actual observed stats as ML inputs/targets?

### Takeaway
The weight of evidence — from Baseball Prospectus, FanGraphs, and independent blog analyses — is that expected stats (xBA, xwOBA, and by extension xSLG/xERA) do NOT clearly outperform simpler traditional metrics (FIP, DRA, even plain wOBA/BA) at predicting next-season performance, despite being excellent descriptive/same-period tools; some analyses found xwOBA modestly better than raw wOBA for predicting the *next* half-season but still only weakly predictive overall.

### Cited Findings
- Baseball Prospectus (published May 18, 2018) directly compared next-year predictive correlations: for batting average, xBA = .36 (±.047), plain BA = .32 (±.046), FIP = .30 (±.047), DRA = .35 (±.047); for wOBA, xwOBA = .35 (±.047), wOBA = .32 (±.047), FIP = .36 (±.045), DRA = .36 (±.046). Conclusion: "little evidence that metrics like xBA or xwOBA provide a uniquely better measurement of probable pitcher skill" — xwOBA performed no better than FIP (which uses only K/BB/HR), and DRA matched or exceeded xwOBA — [Baseball Prospectus, "The Siren Song of Statcast's Expected Metrics (For Pitchers)"](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/40026/prospectus-feature-siren-song-statcasts-expected-metrics/) (May 2018).
- FanGraphs' in-season predictiveness study (published Sept. 5, 2018, n=499 hitters, n=341 pitchers) found: same-half xwOBA-to-wOBA correlations of r=.783–.798 (hitters) and r=.800–.823 (pitchers) — strong descriptively — but first-half-xwOBA-to-second-half-actual-wOBA correlations of only r=.406 (hitters) and r=.354 (pitchers), described as "feeble." Notably, first-half xwOBA predicted second-half wOBA *better* than first-half wOBA did, suggesting "more consistency in a hitter's batted ball profile than in outcomes produced by said profile," but the wOBA-minus-xwOBA "luck" differential was "particularly unreliable" for forward prediction — [FanGraphs, "The In-Season Predictiveness of xwOBA"](https://fantasy.fangraphs.com/the-in-season-predictiveness-of-xwoba/) (Sept. 2018).
- A separate community-level analysis using nine years of data reported the R² for prior-year xwOBA predicting current-year wOBA at .218, and concluded xwOBA's predictive power "isn't that much different than wOBA itself on a full season year-to-year basis" — [FanGraphs Community, "How I Use xwOBA" / related wOBA R² discussion](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/how-i-use-xwoba/) (date not confirmed from snippet, treat cautiously; "r=.35, R²=.218" figures appear consistent across BP and community sources but original study/date for the R²=.218 figure specifically was not independently verified).
- A study by Tom Tango (referenced secondhand in search results, not independently fetched) reportedly found that "Predictive wOBA" (a regressed/stabilized version of wOBA, not Statcast-based) outperforms both plain wOBA and xwOBA for predicting next-season wOBA — suggesting that proper statistical regression/shrinkage may matter more than swapping in Statcast-based "expected" stats — [The Paraball Notes, "How Can We Make Predictive wOBA Even More Predictive?"](https://www.paraballnotes.com/blog/how-can-we-make-predictive-woba-even-more-predictive) (secondhand reference to Tango's work; original Tango study not directly fetched — flag as lower-confidence/unverified primary source).
- The sabermiketrics analysis (Oct. 2021) is a partial outlier/contradiction: it reported that xwOBA "outperformed actual wOBA" as the strongest predictor of next-year wOBA in their decision-forest ML setup — somewhat in tension with the BP/FanGraphs correlation-based findings above, which found xwOBA roughly on par with (not clearly better than) traditional stats. This discrepancy may reflect differences in methodology (ML non-linear model vs. simple correlation) or sample period (2017–2021 vs. 2015–2018) — [sabermiketrics blog](https://sabermiketrics.blogspot.com/2021/10/how-predictive-are-statcast-metrics.html) vs. [Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/40026/prospectus-feature-siren-song-statcasts-expected-metrics/) and [FanGraphs](https://fantasy.fangraphs.com/the-in-season-predictiveness-of-xwoba/) — flagged explicitly as a conflict between sources.

### Inferences
- The most rigorous, peer-reviewed-adjacent sources (Baseball Prospectus using DRA as a sophisticated comparison baseline) consistently show expected stats providing little to no uniquely superior predictive signal over well-constructed traditional/mixed-model stats (FIP, DRA) — the apparent "edge" of Statcast expected stats seems to mostly reflect that they're already good descriptive summaries of current performance, not that they unlock new forward-looking information.
- Where ML (non-linear, ensemble) methods are used instead of simple linear correlation, expected stats appear to perform somewhat better relatively — hinting that xwOBA/xBA's value may be better captured by models that exploit non-linear batted-ball-profile interactions rather than being used as a simple one-number proxy in a linear model.

### Gaps
- No study was found specifically testing xERA (a newer, 2021+ Statcast metric) against traditional ERA estimators (FIP, xFIP, SIERA) for predictive power — all pitcher-side expected-stats evidence found related to xBA/xwOBA against pitchers' actual stats, not the dedicated xERA metric.
- No source quantified whether using expected stats as ML *targets* (train model to predict next year's xwOBA rather than wOBA) changes model performance — all evidence found concerns expected stats as predictive *inputs/comparators*, not as alternative training targets.

## What data access/infrastructure is required to use Statcast data in a projection pipeline?

### Takeaway
Statcast data is fully public and free via Baseball Savant, with mature open-source Python tooling (pybaseball) handling pitch-level (not just aggregate) extraction back to 2008 (full batted-ball tracking from 2015), but pipelines face real engineering burdens: per-request result caps requiring chunked queries, pitch-level data volume (one row per pitch across ~700,000+ pitches/season), and non-trivial feature engineering to aggregate pitch-level data into player-season features.

### Cited Findings
- Statcast data is sourced from Baseball Savant and publicly available from the 2008 season onward, though key batted-ball features like launch speed/launch angle are only available from the 2015 season forward (when full Statcast tracking was installed in all 30 parks) — [pybaseball GitHub docs](https://github.com/jldbc/pybaseball/blob/master/docs/statcast.md).
- pybaseball is an open-source Python package that scrapes Baseball Reference, Baseball Savant, and FanGraphs; its `statcast(start_dt, end_dt)` function returns one row per pitch (fields include perceived velocity, spin rate, exit velocity, pitch x/y/z coordinates) — [pybaseball GitHub README](https://github.com/jldbc/pybaseball/blob/master/README.md); [Medium, "An Intro to Pybaseball's Statcast"](https://medium.com/@dattoli.joe.9/an-into-to-pybaseballs-statcast-cf6ca545d64e).
- Baseball Savant enforces a maximum result size per query, so pybaseball implements automatic request chunking/splitting of large date ranges into smaller sub-queries to stay under that limit — a direct infrastructure/engineering burden for anyone building a full-season or multi-season pipeline — [pybaseball GitHub docs](https://github.com/jldbc/pybaseball/blob/master/docs/statcast.md).
- Data is available both at the raw pitch level and pre-aggregated at the season level/custom time windows, meaning teams/analysts building projection models must choose between (a) pulling and aggregating raw pitch-level data themselves (higher engineering burden, more flexible feature engineering) or (b) using pre-aggregated Baseball Savant leaderboard exports (lower burden, less granular control) — [pybaseball docs / sportanalytics.com overview](https://sportanalytics.com/get-free-mlb-data-for-powerful-baseball-analytics-projects/).

### Inferences
- The public availability of Statcast via Baseball Savant/pybaseball means the "access" barrier to using Statcast in an ML pipeline is now low relative to the pre-2015 era; the remaining burden is almost entirely feature engineering (converting pitch-level or batted-ball-level data into meaningful player-season aggregates like barrel rate, Hard-Hit%, or "stuff" composites) rather than data acquisition itself.
- Teams with proprietary access (e.g., to Trackman/Hawk-Eye raw feeds, biomechanical data, or higher-frequency internal tracking) likely have an incremental data edge over what's available on Baseball Savant, but no public source quantified how much additional predictive value proprietary feeds provide over the public Statcast feed.

### Gaps
- No source quantified the typical data volume (GB/rows) of a full-season Statcast pull, computation time, or storage requirements for a production-grade pipeline.
- No information was found comparing proprietary team pitch-tracking feeds (Hawk-Eye raw data, biomechanical/bat-tracking feeds beyond what's public) against the public Baseball Savant feed in terms of incremental predictive power — this remains an unaddressed gap since teams' internal data and models are not publicly disclosed.

## Is there dedicated SABR/Sloan/academic work on "value of Statcast data for prediction"?

### Takeaway
Directly on-point peer-reviewed or conference work explicitly framed as "do advanced/Statcast metrics improve projections" is scarce in public search results; the closest matches are an NCF master's/honors thesis on pitcher ERA prediction, a Sloan-adjacent deep-learning "contextual event embeddings" paper, and injury-prediction clinical-journal studies — none of which is a canonical, widely-cited "Statcast vs. box score" benchmark paper.

### Cited Findings
- "Learning Contextual Event Embeddings to Predict Player Performance in the MLB" is a Sloan Sports Analytics Conference research paper using deep learning to model pitch-by-pitch sequences (language-model-style) to predict player performance based on prior performance — a Statcast/event-level approach, though the specific comparison to box-score-only baselines and quantitative lift were not available in the search snippet — [MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference research papers](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/learning-contextual-event-embeddings-to-predict-player-performance-in-the-mlb).
- A Penn State research effort (Sloan finalist) trained a model on Statcast data from the 12 high-speed tracking cameras in every MLB stadium to produce a 64-element "player form" vector describing short-term in-game skill/impact, but again, no quantitative box-score-vs-Statcast comparison was surfaced in available content — [Nutanix/Forecast, "How Penn State researchers use machine learning..."](https://www.nutanix.com/theforecastbynutanix/industry/how-machine-learning-and-ai-can-predict-player-outcomes).
- Statcast itself debuted at the MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference and won the 2015 Alpha Award for best analytics innovation, underscoring that Sloan has historically been a showcase venue for Statcast-adjacent work generally, even if a specific "incremental value" benchmark study wasn't located — [search aggregation, multiple sources citing Statcast's 2015 Sloan debut].
- The clearest "controlled, quantified" academic-adjacent work found remains the NCF thesis (pitcher ERA, R²≈0.27 best model) and the Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine injury-prediction paper (accuracy 0.84, AUC 0.66) cited above — both of which quantify Statcast's contribution but are not the SABR/Sloan-specific benchmark the key question asks about.

### Inferences
- The absence of a single, widely-cited, rigorously controlled "Statcast adds X% accuracy over box score" benchmark paper in SABR/Sloan/JQAS literature (despite extensive searching) suggests this exact framing is still an underexplored or at least under-published niche — most public work either (a) builds a single best model using mixed features without ablation reporting, or (b) focuses on narrower, specific tasks (injury, hit probability, batted-ball outcome) rather than general season-ahead production projections.

### Gaps
- No SABR Analytics Conference presentation specifically on this topic was found via available search tools (search results skewed toward Sloan and informal blog/community sources; a dedicated SABR archive search was not performed due to tool constraints within the research budget).
- No JQAS (Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports) article specifically matching "Statcast feature importance prediction" was retrieved — search returned the journal's general scope description but not a specific on-point paper; this should be treated as an open gap rather than evidence of absence, since JQAS's own search/archive was not directly queried.
