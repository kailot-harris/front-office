# Advanced Deep Learning Architectures for MLB Player Performance Projection

## Are there published papers/writeups using RNNs/LSTMs/transformers on sequences of plate appearances, pitches, or games to project future MLB player performance (vs. treating player-seasons as independent rows)?

### Takeaway
Yes — a small but growing body of work (mostly arXiv preprints, student capstones, Sloan conference papers, and one FanGraphs/SaberSeminar writeup) applies LSTMs, GRUs, and transformers to season-sequence, game-sequence, or pitch-sequence MLB data, but the field remains niche relative to tabular gradient-boosted approaches, and several of the more rigorous recent attempts found sequence models provide little to no improvement over simpler baselines without extra tricks like pretraining.

### Cited Findings
- "Performance Prediction in Major League Baseball by Long Short-Term Memory Networks" (arXiv:2206.09654, submitted June 20, 2022) trained 5 LSTM variants (Models A–E, 1–2 LSTM layers of 32–128 cells + fully connected layers, dropout/batch norm, ReLU output) on 5,401 MLB players from 1961–2019 (21 features/player-season), using a 5-year sliding window to predict 6th-year home runs; trained on 1961–2017 (9,828 points), tested on 2018–2019 (375 points), excluding players with fewer than 50 PA. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654); [alphaXiv summary](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2206.09654)
- That LSTM study reports 10–13% exact-match accuracy and 20–24% accuracy within ±1 home run, beating linear regression, SVMs, random forests, feedforward NNs, and the ZiPS projection system on precision metrics; however all models (LSTM included) struggled on elite performers (40+ HR), simpler architectures sometimes beat more complex ones, and LSTM predictions were systematically more conservative than ZiPS (which skewed more optimistic). — [arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654)
- A Stanford CS229 course project, "Predicting the Offensive Performance of MLB Position Players" (Benavidez, Brito, McCreight, McEvoy; mentor Leo Mehr; CS229 2019 Autumn), compared player-agnostic and player-specific linear/ridge/lasso regression, player-specific SVR, and a stacked LSTM RNN to predict OPS+ one year ahead from the prior n years of annual Baseball-Reference statistics (trained on 2012–2016 to predict 2017 OPS+, 177 train / 44 test players). The LSTM (300 epochs) achieved MSE 0.0019 / MAE 0.033 vs. SVR's MSE 0.088 / MAE 0.232 — a large reported improvement — though the authors note the model struggled with player-to-player unpredictability from injuries and off-field issues, and flagged Retrosheet play-by-play data as unexploited future work for finer-grained features. — [CS229 project PDF](https://cs229.stanford.edu/proj2019aut/data/assignment_308875_raw/26382632.pdf)
- A 2017 SaberSeminar presentation/FanGraphs (The Hardball Times) writeup by Kiri Oler, "Using Recurrent Neural Networks to Predict Player Performance," used an LSTM trained on game-by-game (not season-level) career statistics — 18 counting categories (PA, AB, R, H, 2B, 3B, HR, RBI, BB, IBB, K, HBP, SH, SF, ROE, GDP, SB, CS) — with one row per game, tested by training on Manny Machado's first four seasons to predict his 2016 season game-by-game. The model clustered predictions near averages and struggled with rare events (HR, SB) and extreme values; this is a non-peer-reviewed sabermetrics community writeup. — [THT FanGraphs](https://tht.fangraphs.com/using-recurrent-neural-networks-to-predict-player-performance/)
- A GitHub project ("FantasyBaseball" by alhart2015, issue #414 / PR #416, undated but recent) explicitly builds "plate-appearance sequence models" — a GRU and then a transformer — over each hitter's last N (starting at N=600) plate appearances prior to a prediction date, with each PA carrying a "days before as-of-date" feature for learnable recency, and (in the transformer variant) a learned position embedding counted back from the most recent PA. This directly targets the limitation that traditional tabular models only see averages over discrete windows (season/last season/3-year/career) and cannot see order or hot/cold streaks. — [GitHub PR #416](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/pull/416); [GitHub Issue #414](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/issues/414)
- That same GitHub project reports a negative/null result: over 3 random seeds, the GRU and transformer achieved the same validation loss as a plain MLP baseline (differences within seed noise), leading the author to conclude "without pretraining, the sequence adds nothing" — a notable, candid non-peer-reviewed engineering finding that sequence architecture alone (on top of comparable features) may not beat a tabular MLP absent additional techniques like pretraining. — [GitHub Issue #414](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/issues/414)
- Related, separate GitHub project "pitch-hawk" (danielmac96, PR #39, "Models v3: one plate-appearance model behind every player and game market," undated/recent) also describes building a single plate-appearance-level model to drive multiple derived markets, suggesting plate-appearance-level (vs. season-level) modeling is an active informal/hobbyist pattern beyond just one repo. — [GitHub PR #39](https://github.com/danielmac96/pitch-hawk/pull/39)
- "Pitcher Performance Prediction Major League Baseball (MLB) by Temporal Fusion Transformer," by Wonbyung Lee and Jang Hyun Kim (Dept. of Applied Artificial Intelligence, SungKyunKwan University), published in *Computers, Materials & Continua*, May 2025 (DOI 10.32604/cmc.2025.065413), applies the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) architecture to predict pitcher ERA, benchmarking against RNN-based approaches and existing projection systems; the TFT model is reported to consistently outperform both. This is a peer-reviewed journal article. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391148375_Pitcher_Performance_Prediction_Major_League_Baseball_MLB_by_Temporal_Fusion_Transformer); [Publisher (sciopen/CMC)](https://www.sciopen.com/article/10.32604/cmc.2025.065413)
- "Learning To Describe Player Form in The MLB" by Connor Heaton and Prasenjit Mitra (arXiv:2109.05280, submitted September 11, 2021) uses contrastive learning to build 72-dimensional embeddings of player "form" — how a player has impacted recent play, not just season totals — intended to capture information about playing impact absent from traditional box-score stats; likely built on Statcast/event-level pitch-by-pitch data, though the abstract does not explicitly confirm the exact data granularity. — [arXiv:2109.05280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05280)
- A follow-on/related Sloan paper, "Learning Contextual Event Embeddings to Predict Player Performance in the MLB" by Connor Heaton and Prasenjit Mitra (Sloan Sports Analytics Conference, year not confirmed in search results but associated with a "SSA23" YouTube presentation, suggesting 2023), trains a model to treat baseball as "a sequence of pitches" analogous to language-model token sequences, contextualizing each event by count, baserunners, etc. Using only 10 games of pitch-by-pitch data per player, the model produces single-game pitcher strikeout and batter has-hit predictions competitive with (and covering ~50% more props than) three major US sportsbooks for the 2021 season. The authors frame this as moving baseball analytics from a "bag-of-events era" to an "era of contextual embeddings." — [Sloan paper page](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/learning-contextual-event-embeddings-to-predict-player-performance-in-the-mlb); [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Learning-Contextual-Event-Embeddings-to-Predict-in-Heaton-Mitra/3a196c0b06b09c10a82f76a5ebc3476f2e808fdc); [YouTube SSA23 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uyOP7WZ_tl8)
- A related Sloan finalist paper, "Using Machine Learning to Describe How Players Impact the Game in the MLB" by Connor Heaton and Prasenjit Mitra, was one of seven finalists in the 2022 MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference Research Paper Competition — confirming this research line (Heaton/Mitra, Penn State) as an active, competitively-reviewed body of work specifically on sequence/embedding-based MLB player modeling. — [PDF](https://global-uploads.webflow.com/5f1af76ed86d6771ad48324b/622770fbd0f4027cbec673bd_Using%20Machine%20Learning%20to%20Describe%20How%20Players%20Impact%20the%20Game%20in%20the%20MLB%202.pdf)
- A ResearchSquare preprint, "Applications of Representation Learning Methods in Professional Baseball" (DOI-bearing preprint rs-3958813/v1, posted ~Dec 2024 per URL timestamp), was found via search summary describing an 8-layer, norm-first transformer architecture with internal dimension 512 and 8 attention heads applied to player performance projection; however, the PDF could not be parsed for full text during this research pass (binary/encoded extraction failures on both WebFetch and Read), so architecture/data/results details beyond the search snippet are unconfirmed and should be re-verified by directly opening the PDF. — [ResearchSquare PDF](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-3958813/v1_covered_cf58c384-1a5b-4d4e-a123-fe125ee367f2.pdf?c=1734080024)
- A broader review, "Machine Learning Applications in Baseball: A Systematic Literature Review" (*Applied Artificial Intelligence*, Tandfonline, 2018) and a more recent survey "Machine Learning in Baseball Analytics: Sabermetrics and Beyond" (*MDPI Information*, 2025, vol. 16, issue 5, article 361) both catalog the broader ML-in-baseball literature landscape and note that deep learning for player performance prediction "remains largely unexplored" relative to tabular approaches, and the MDPI 2025 survey explicitly states the gap persists as of its publication. — [Tandfonline 2018 review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08839514.2018.1442991); [MDPI 2025 survey](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/16/5/361)

### Inferences
- There is a real but thin cluster of work treating MLB player performance as a *sequence* problem (season-sequence LSTMs, game-sequence LSTMs, and pitch/PA-sequence transformers), spanning roughly 2017–2025, but almost none of it is top-tier peer-reviewed ML venue work — it is concentrated in arXiv preprints, Sloan conference submissions (juried but not journal-peer-reviewed in the traditional sense), one MDPI/CMC journal article each, student capstones, and hobbyist GitHub repos.
- The most methodologically rigorous and recent evidence (the alhart2015 FantasyBaseball GRU/transformer ablation) suggests sequence architecture alone, holding information content roughly constant, does not reliably beat a plain MLP/tabular baseline on plate-appearance-level hitter projection — implying published positive results (e.g., the CS229 poster's huge LSTM-vs-SVR MSE gap, or the home-run LSTM study's gains over ZiPS) may partly reflect weak baselines, different information content (e.g., using more granular inputs) or overfitting/small test sets rather than an intrinsic architectural advantage of recurrence/attention for this problem.
- Where sequence/transformer models do show convincing gains (Heaton & Mitra's contextual embeddings vs. sportsbooks; Lee & Kim's TFT vs. RNN baselines and projection systems for ERA), the gains appear to come from using finer-grained input data (pitch-by-pitch/event-level Statcast-like data, or contextualized event embeddings) rather than from the sequence architecture per se — i.e., the architectural choice and the data-granularity choice are often confounded in these papers.

## Are there mixture density network or other probabilistic/distributional (vs. point-estimate) approaches to projecting player stats?

### Takeaway
True neural mixture density networks (MDNs) applied specifically to MLB hitter/pitcher season or career projection were not found in this research pass; the closest confirmed MLB-specific distributional approaches are Bayesian/Dirichlet-family statistical models (not neural MDNs) for plate-appearance outcome composition, while MDNs proper appear well-established in adjacent sports-trajectory contexts (basketball) but not confirmed for baseball player projection specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Mixture Density Networks (MDNs) are neural architectures that parameterize a mixture model (typically Gaussian) whose weights, means, and covariances are output directly by the network, producing a full conditional probability distribution instead of a point estimate, with the key stated advantage of enabling principled uncertainty quantification and multimodal distribution representation. — [Emergent Mind: MDNs](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/mixture-density-networks-mdns); [Emergent Mind: MDN explainer](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/mixture-density-network)
- A confirmed sports application of deep bidirectional LSTM + MDN exists for basketball trajectory prediction (predicting and sampling plausible basketball shot/pass trajectories from real data), demonstrating the LSTM+MDN combination is an established pattern in sports analytics generally — but this is basketball, not baseball, and concerns physical trajectories, not player performance statistics. — [ResearchGate: BLSTM+MDN basketball trajectories](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319209740_Applying_Deep_Bidirectional_LSTM_and_Mixture_Density_Network_for_Basketball_Trajectory_Prediction)
- "Layered Dirichlet Modeling to Assess the Changing Contributions of MLB Players as they Age" (arXiv:2501.09153, submitted January 2025) introduces a Nested Dirichlet Distribution — a probabilistic/compositional statistical framework (not a neural network) — applied to MLB plate-appearance outcomes, modeling how at-bat results (hits, walks, strikeouts, etc.) decompose hierarchically into nested categories, explicitly producing probability distributions over outcome composition rather than point estimates, used to study age-related changes in player contribution patterns. — [arXiv:2501.09153](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09153)
- "Nuclear penalized multinomial regression with an application to predicting at bat outcomes in baseball" (arXiv:1706.10272, 2017 submission based on arXiv ID numbering) applies a nuclear-norm-penalized multinomial regression (a regularized generalized linear model, not a deep network) to predict the full categorical probability distribution over baseball at-bat outcomes; because it is multinomial regression, it inherently outputs a distribution across outcome categories rather than a single-point prediction — but full architecture/results details could not be extracted from the PDF during this research pass (binary extraction failure). — [arXiv:1706.10272](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.10272)
- A U.S. patent family, "Method and system for interactive, interpretable, and improved match and player performance predictions in team sports" (multiple related patent numbers: 12667770, 11577145, 11660521), and a separate patent "System and method for predicting formation in sports" (11935298), reference MDN-style distributional approaches for team-sports match/player performance and formation prediction, but these are patent filings (not peer-reviewed research, and not confirmed to be MLB-specific — appear to be sport-agnostic or soccer-oriented based on "formation" and "match" terminology) and should be treated as weak, non-academic evidence only. — [USPTO patent 12667770](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12667770); [USPTO patent 11935298](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11935298)
- Traditional (non-neural) probabilistic baseball projection systems like THE BAT/THE BAT X and Steamer already produce percentile-based distributional outputs for player projections, but these are outside the scope of confirmed sources found in this specific research pass (not independently re-verified here; noted from general domain knowledge as context, not as a cited finding — treat as background only, not sourced).

### Inferences
- The MDN concept is well-suited in principle to baseball projection (distributional rather than point projections are exactly what systems like ZiPS/Steamer/THE BAT already try to approximate via percentile outputs or simulation), but this research did not surface a single confirmed, named example of a neural MDN applied specifically to MLB hitter/pitcher season-level projection — this appears to be a genuine white space rather than just a search-visibility gap, given the basketball MDN precedent was easy to find but no baseball equivalent surfaced across multiple query phrasings.
- The Dirichlet/multinomial-regression based approaches (arXiv:2501.09153, arXiv:1706.10272) show that the *distributional projection* goal in baseball is currently being pursued mainly through classical Bayesian/compositional statistics rather than deep mixture-density neural networks — i.e., probabilistic rigor and architectural novelty (deep learning) are, in the found literature, largely on separate tracks rather than combined.

## What MLB-specific competitions exist (e.g., Kaggle MLB Player Digital Engagement Forecasting), and what methods did top solutions use?

### Takeaway
The flagship MLB-specific competition is Kaggle's "MLB Player Digital Engagement Forecasting" (2021), where the 1st place solution used a from-scratch GRU-based sequential neural network while the 2nd place solution used a traditional LightGBM/XGBoost ensemble — directly illustrating the architectural fork between sequence deep learning and gradient-boosted tabular methods on the same MLB forecasting task, though note this competition predicted *fan digital engagement scores*, not traditional on-field performance stats.

### Cited Findings
- Kaggle's "MLB Player Digital Engagement Forecasting" competition (2021) tasked competitors with forecasting daily player-level fan digital engagement scores using MLB-provided player, team, game, and social-media-adjacent data. — [Kaggle competition discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/c/mlb-player-digital-engagement-forecasting/discussion/274255); [Kaggle "Getting Started" notebook](https://www.kaggle.com/code/ryanholbrook/getting-started-with-mlb-player-digital-engagement)
- The 1st place solution used a neural network built around a GRU plus several additional processing layers, trained entirely from scratch within a Kaggle GPU kernel, explicitly designed to sequentially process "raw" input information over a time span (a 96-day lookback window) without heavy feature engineering, using past target values (log(1+x)-scaled to reduce peakedness) as part of the sequence input, with a training augmentation where target values for the most recent randomly-chosen ≤32 days were zeroed out to simulate the real-world information gap, and a snapshot ensemble used for final predictions. — [Kaggle discussion thread (search-summarized)](https://www.kaggle.com/c/mlb-player-digital-engagement-forecasting/discussion/274255)
- The 2nd place solution, by contrast, used a blend of five LightGBM models with varied settings plus one XGBoost model per target (24 models total — the competition had 4 target engagement metrics), with the most important features being historical-target aggregates per player, date-related features, and Twitter follower counts — a purely tabular gradient-boosting approach with no sequence architecture. — [Kaggle discussion thread (search-summarized)](https://www.kaggle.com/c/mlb-player-digital-engagement-forecasting/discussion/274255)
- The 3rd place solution is published as open-source code on GitHub (nyanp/mlb-player-digital-engagement), described as the "3rd place solution," though specific architecture details could not be extracted from the repo's top-level README during this research pass — the repo references a fuller solution writeup on the Kaggle discussion forum and contains source/notebook directories for reproduction. — [GitHub: nyanp/mlb-player-digital-engagement](https://github.com/nyanp/mlb-player-digital-engagement)
- Columbia University-affiliated Kaggle user "columbia2131" published a derived/cleaned training dataset ("MLB_PDEF_train_dataset") from this competition, indicating continued reuse of the competition data for further research/modeling beyond the original contest window. — [Kaggle dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/columbia2131/mlb-pdef-train-dataset)
- H2O.ai published a blog post on the competition (2021), and a Medium post by Joshua Shamouil ("Using Python to Predict Baseball Player Engagement") also covers approaches to this competition, both non-peer-reviewed secondary sources/writeups. — [H2O.ai blog](https://h2o.ai/blog/2021/mlb-player-digital-engagement-forecasting/); [Medium post](https://medium.com/@joshuashamouil/using-python-to-predict-baseball-player-engagement-3268a20e423d)

### Inferences
- Because "MLB Player Digital Engagement Forecasting" predicts fan-engagement scores (a proxy driven heavily by game events, social buzz, and schedule) rather than traditional on-field stats (AVG/OPS/ERA/WAR), its winning-architecture findings are only indirectly transferable to true performance projection — but the 1st-vs-2nd place GRU-vs-LightGBM split is still a useful real-world data point that sequence deep learning *can* win against gradient boosting on an MLB-adjacent forecasting task when given sufficient raw sequential signal and compute, though it is a single competition instance, not a generalizable finding.
- No other MLB-specific Kaggle competition specifically targeting traditional on-field player performance projection (e.g., season AVG/HR/ERA forecasting) was surfaced in this research; MLB Player Digital Engagement Forecasting appears to be the main/flagship MLB player-level Kaggle competition.

## Are there university thesis/capstone projects comparing ML (especially sequence) projections to traditional systems with rigorous methodology?

### Takeaway
Confirmed capstone-level work exists (Stanford CS229 and CS230 course projects) comparing LSTM/RNN approaches to classical ML and in one case to the ZiPS projection system, but these are course-project posters/reports (not formal theses), generally small-sample, and not peer-reviewed — they should be treated as illustrative/exploratory rather than rigorous validated research.

### Cited Findings
- Stanford CS229 (Autumn 2019) project "Predicting the Offensive Performance of MLB Position Players" (Benavidez, Brito, McCreight, McEvoy) directly compared player-agnostic/player-specific linear regression (plain/lasso/ridge), player-specific SVR, and a stacked LSTM RNN for one-year-ahead OPS+ prediction from Baseball-Reference annual statistics (2012–2016 train → 2017 test, 177 train/44 test players); LSTM (MSE 0.0019, MAE 0.033) vastly outperformed SVR (MSE 0.088, MAE 0.232) on their reported metrics, though the small test set (44 players) and course-project scope limit how rigorous/generalizable this comparison is. — [CS229 PDF](https://cs229.stanford.edu/proj2019aut/data/assignment_308875_raw/26382632.pdf)
- The "Performance Prediction in Major League Baseball by Long Short-Term Memory Networks" arXiv paper (2206.09654) is itself framed as a comparative study against ZiPS (an industry-standard traditional projection system) plus linear regression, SVM, random forest, and plain neural networks — functioning as a quasi-thesis-style rigorous comparison, even though it's published as an arXiv preprint rather than a formal university thesis (institutional affiliation not confirmed in available summaries). — [arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654)
- A Stanford CS230 (Spring 2020) project, "Baseball Win Predictions" by Juliette Love, was identified in search results as a deep-learning course project in the baseball space, though its focus (based on title) appears to be game win prediction rather than individual player performance projection — relevant as context for the Stanford deep-learning-in-baseball course-project ecosystem but not a direct hit on this research's player-projection focus. — [CS230 PDF](http://cs230.stanford.edu/projects_spring_2020/reports/38837495.pdf)

## For each method found: core idea, data/feature requirements, and whether it's a small addition vs. large architectural change relative to a traditional stats-based pipeline

### Takeaway
The surveyed methods span a spectrum from "drop-in sequence replacement" (season-sequence LSTM on the same tabular features) requiring no new data, to "fundamentally different pipeline" (pitch-by-pitch contextual embeddings / TFT) requiring granular Statcast/event-level data and materially different infrastructure — with the most data-light methods showing the weakest/most disputed evidence of improvement, and the most data-hungry methods showing the strongest reported gains.

### Cited Findings
- Season-sequence LSTM (arXiv:2206.09654; CS229 poster): core idea is replacing independent-row tabular regression with a sliding-window sequence of a player's own prior N seasons of standard counting/rate stats; data requirement is identical to what traditional projection systems already use (annual stats, age, etc. — no pitch-level data needed); this is a comparatively **small architectural change** (same features, different model class) layered onto an existing stats pipeline. — [arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654); [CS229 PDF](https://cs229.stanford.edu/proj2019aut/data/assignment_308875_raw/26382632.pdf)
- Game-sequence LSTM (Oler, SaberSeminar 2017): core idea is treating a player's career as a sequence of individual games rather than seasons, using the same 18 standard box-score counting stats per game; data requirement is game logs (publicly available, e.g., Baseball-Reference/Retrosheet game logs) — still **a small-to-moderate change**, since it needs finer granularity (game-level vs. season-level) but no proprietary Statcast/pitch data. — [THT FanGraphs](https://tht.fangraphs.com/using-recurrent-neural-networks-to-predict-player-performance/)
- GRU for Kaggle fan-engagement forecasting (1st place MLB PDEF solution): core idea is a from-scratch sequential network ingesting ~96 days of raw daily features (including past targets) with minimal feature engineering; data requirement is the competition's provided daily player/game/engagement data — a **large architectural change** relative to the 2nd place team's traditional LightGBM/XGBoost tabular-feature pipeline on the same raw data, illustrating that even with identical data access, deep sequence modeling vs. gradient boosting is a genuine architecture fork, not just a data-availability difference. — [Kaggle discussion](https://www.kaggle.com/c/mlb-player-digital-engagement-forecasting/discussion/274255)
- GRU/transformer over plate appearances (alhart2015 FantasyBaseball repo): core idea is modeling a hitter's last ~600 PAs with recency-aware (days-before-as-of-date) and position-embedding features instead of pre-aggregated rolling-window averages; data requirement is PA-level outcome logs (more granular than season stats, less granular than full pitch-by-pitch) — a **moderate architectural change**, and notably the author's own ablation found it added no measurable benefit over an MLP without pretraining, i.e., the extra data granularity and architecture complexity did not pay off in this instance. — [GitHub Issue #414](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/issues/414)
- Contextual event embeddings over pitch sequences (Heaton & Mitra, arXiv:2109.05280 and Sloan papers): core idea is treating full pitch-by-pitch game data as a language-model-style token sequence, learning dense embeddings of "player form" via contrastive learning, and using as little as 10 games of pitch-level history per player; data requirement is genuinely granular Statcast/pitch-event data (count, baserunners, pitch-level context) — a **large architectural and data-pipeline change** relative to a traditional stats-based system, since it requires event-level data ingestion and embedding infrastructure, not just season/game aggregates. — [arXiv:2109.05280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05280); [Sloan paper page](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/learning-contextual-event-embeddings-to-predict-player-performance-in-the-mlb)
- Temporal Fusion Transformer for pitcher ERA (Lee & Kim, CMC 2025): core idea is applying the TFT architecture (designed for mixed static/time-varying covariates with interpretable attention) to pitcher performance over time, benchmarked against RNNs and projection systems; data requirements were not fully confirmed in this pass (likely season- or game-level pitcher statistics with temporal covariates) — architecturally a **large change** (TFT is a substantially more complex model than season-sequence LSTM, with multi-horizon forecasting and variable-selection networks), though whether its input data is more granular than season-level was not confirmed. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391148375_Pitcher_Performance_Prediction_Major_League_Baseball_MLB_by_Temporal_Fusion_Transformer)
- Nested Dirichlet / layered Dirichlet modeling (arXiv:2501.09153): core idea is a hierarchical Bayesian compositional model over plate-appearance outcome categories (not a neural network at all); data requirement is standard plate-appearance outcome counts (publicly available, no pitch-level data needed); this is a **small-to-moderate methodological change** relative to traditional stats pipelines in terms of data needs, but represents a different statistical paradigm (compositional/Bayesian vs. regression) for achieving distributional output. — [arXiv:2501.09153](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09153)
- Nuclear penalized multinomial regression (arXiv:1706.10272): core idea is a regularized (nuclear-norm-penalized) generalized linear model producing full-distribution at-bat outcome probabilities; this is a **small change** relative to a traditional stats pipeline — it is a GLM variant, not deep learning, and the data requirement (full detail unconfirmed due to PDF extraction failure, but based on title/context likely standard at-bat outcome data) is modest. — [arXiv:1706.10272](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.10272)

### Inferences
- There is a rough pattern across the surveyed literature: methods requiring only season/game-level data and swapping in a sequence architecture (LSTM/GRU on standard stats) show the most disputed/inconsistent evidence (strong claimed gains in small course projects and one arXiv paper, but a clean negative ablation in the one rigorous GitHub experiment that controlled for this), whereas methods that pair architectural novelty with genuinely more granular data (pitch-by-pitch context embeddings, TFT) show the more consistently positive and larger reported gains — suggesting that for teams building their own system, the data-granularity upgrade (incorporating pitch-by-pitch/Statcast event sequences) may matter more than the specific sequence-architecture choice itself.
- None of the surveyed methods represent a "small addition" when going all the way to pitch-level contextual embeddings or TFT — these require substantially different data pipelines (event-level ingestion, embedding stores, attention-based model infrastructure) compared to a traditional age-curve/regression-based projection system; by contrast, season-sequence LSTM is genuinely close to a "drop-in" replacement for a traditional regression-on-rolling-averages approach, usable with data most projection systems already have.

- Data/feature requirements for the Temporal Fusion Transformer pitcher-ERA paper (Lee & Kim 2025) were not fully confirmed (season-level vs. more granular); this is a gap given it's one of the most recent and most positively-reported results found.
