# MLB markets are efficient, but not perfectly

MLB moneyline and totals markets are, by the weight of the academic evidence, **close to efficient by closing time** — large-sample strategy-backtesting finds profitable-strategy rates no better than chance ([Tandfonline 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115)) — but "close to efficient" is not "perfectly efficient," and the gaps that remain are exactly the ones a risk-management engine should care about: documented negative autocorrelation and overreaction in MLB line movement ([Simon 2024, *Management Science*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html)), a contested reverse favorite-longshot bias that is strong at the open and fades by the close ([Woodland & Woodland 1994](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1994.tb04429.x); [Liu 2025](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/81e73570-084b-4e39-84d3-ccb4649c61ab/download)), and a markedly inefficient season win-totals derivative market ([Woodland & Woodland 2015/2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x)). Closing Line Value (CLV) turns out to be industry terminology rather than a validated academic construct — no peer-reviewed paper regresses bettor ROI on captured CLV, and the one rigorous independent check of the hypothesis (Karl Whelan's NBA decile analysis) found positive CLV does **not** map monotonically onto realized profit ([Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/sportsbetting/the-truth-about-closing-line-value/)). Devigging methodology beyond simple proportional normalization is a genuinely separate, well-developed literature: Shin's method and the power method both reliably beat plain proportional devigging on calibration accuracy in peer-reviewed comparisons ([Štrumbelj 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533)), but neither has been tested specifically on MLB odds. Kelly-criterion staking has the deepest and most directly actionable extension literature of the four topics: fixed-fraction Kelly is theoretically arbitrary ("no theoretical underpinnings," per [Chu, Wu & Swartz 2018](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jqas-2017-0122/html?lang=en)), parameter-uncertainty-adjusted shrinkage Kelly and drawdown-constrained dynamic Kelly both have formal derivations and favorable sports-betting simulation evidence, and the literature on simultaneous/correlated multi-bet Kelly is directly relevant to a same-day MLB slate but not baseball-specific. None of the four literatures has a large, directly-verified MLB-specific empirical base for its most advanced methods — baseball is well-studied for market-efficiency questions but essentially unstudied for devigging-method comparison and Kelly-variant validation — so adopting any of the more sophisticated methods below means importing evidence from tennis, horse racing, European football, NBA, or NFL and treating MLB applicability as a reasonable but unverified extrapolation.

## MLB moneyline and totals look efficient; win-totals and the early season don't

The academic literature on MLB market efficiency spans three decades and a methodological arc: early studies found inefficiencies, and later re-examinations with better data (full vig-adjusted pricing instead of binary outcomes) and larger samples tended to shrink or overturn them. Woodland & Woodland's 1994 *Journal of Finance* paper first reported a **reverse favorite-longshot bias** in MLB moneylines — bettors undervalue favorites relative to true win probability, the opposite of the classic racetrack pattern — and reaffirmed it in a 2003 follow-up ([Woodland & Woodland 1994](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1994.tb04429.x); [2003](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255726080_The_Implications_of_a_Reverse_Favourite-Longshot_Bias_in_a_Prediction_Market)), but Gandar, Zuber, Johnson & Dare (2002) failed to replicate it, calling MLB betting "unremarkable" ([summarized in Vogel et al. 2021, *Risks*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jrisks/v9y2021i1p22-d478780.html)) — a direct, unresolved contradiction in the literature. The most recent entrant, a Harvard undergraduate thesis by Liu (2025), reconciles the dispute somewhat: the bias is **present in opening odds but disappears by closing odds**, with stronger effects in home games, implying the market largely corrects the mispricing between open and close ([Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/81e73570-084b-4e39-84d3-ccb4649c61ab/download) — note this thesis could not be fetched in full; findings are drawn from search-engine summaries). For totals, Paul & Weinbach (2014, *Applied Financial Economics*) corrected a methodological flaw in Brown & Abraham (2002) — who had wrongly assumed equal returns on over/under wagers — and, using correct vig-adjusted pricing, found **little evidence the MLB totals market is inefficient** ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09603107.2014.925052)). The strongest and most recent confirmation of overall efficiency is a 2024/2025 *Applied Economics* paper that brute-force-tested **1,547 simple MLB moneyline wagering strategies from 1999–2016**: only 2.46% were profitable at 5% significance and 0.45% at 1%, rates consistent with pure chance after family-wise error correction ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115)).

Where real, better-replicated inefficiency survives, it clusters in lower-liquidity or lower-attention corners of the market rather than in the core single-game lines. Woodland & Woodland's follow-up work found the MLB **season win-totals market** — traded far less frequently than single-game lines — shows bettors overreacting to a team's prior-season record (a representativeness-heuristic bias), producing a genuinely profitable wagering opportunity, a pattern the same authors documented in NFL and NBA season win-totals markets too ([Springer, 2015/2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x)). Ryan, Gramm & McKinney (2012) found early-season MLB underdog bets — placed when public information on team quality is still thin — produced significant positive returns, but Ryan & Celestin (2018) showed this specific edge **dissipated once the strategy was publicized**, with up to half the return decline attributable to bettors adjusting their own behavior rather than bookmakers repricing ([Applied Economics, 50(37)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036846.2018.1430349)) — direct evidence that MLB markets self-correct once an inefficiency becomes known. The most directly relevant finding for a live risk-management engine comes from Jay Simon's 2024 *Management Science* paper analyzing **3,681 MLB games across four sportsbooks**: closing lines are "mostly reliable" forecasts, but line changes show **statistically significant negative autocorrelation** — overreaction that partially reverses and "could be exploited by sophisticated bettors" — plus weaker forecast accuracy for weekend day-games priced close to first pitch ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html)). A companion 2025 paper (Simon, *International Journal of Sport Finance*) extended a similar autocorrelation/weekend-effect finding to NFL, NBA, and NHL moneylines, framing it as "extending inefficiencies recently revealed for MLB" — implying overreaction in line movement is a shared, not baseball-unique, phenomenon across the four major leagues ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports)). Practitioner claims about **weather/wind effects on totals** and **starting-pitcher information lag** circulate widely in betting media but have no peer-reviewed MLB-specific confirmation located in this research; the one rigorous academic weather-totals study found is NFL-specific, not baseball ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24071150_Weather_Biases_in_the_NFL_Totals_Market)), and should not be imported as baseball evidence. Cross-sport comparison is itself contested: older literature held that NFL and MLB show more documented inefficiency than NBA/NHL, but Simon's 2025 cross-sport paper found similar overreaction patterns in all three other leagues, suggesting the gap may partly reflect which sports were studied first rather than a structural difference ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports)).

## Shin's method and the power method both beat proportional devigging, with no MLB test of either

Beyond simple proportional (multiplicative) devigging — which assumes the bookmaker spreads margin evenly regardless of each outcome's likelihood, an assumption the literature rejects — three conceptually distinct corrective families exist. The first is **Shin's method** (Shin 1991–1993), which models the bookmaker as pricing against a mix of uninformed bettors and a small proportion *z* of informed "insiders," and inverts that model to recover zero-margin probabilities that redistribute more of the overround onto longshots ([Shin, *Economic Journal*](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article-abstract/103/420/1141/5157258)). For a two-outcome market (like most MLB moneylines) *z* can be solved analytically and Shin's method reduces to the simple additive-devigging formula; for 3+ outcome markets it requires iterative numerical solving, typically via the Jullien & Salanié (1994) algorithm ([GitHub: mberk/shin](https://github.com/mberk/shin)). The second is the **power method**, a purely statistical curve-fit with no behavioral model: each implied probability is raised to a common exponent *1/k*, with *k* solved numerically so the probabilities sum to 1 ([CRAN `implied` package](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)). A third family — the **odds-ratio/logarithmic method** (Cheung, popularized by Buchdahl's "Wisdom of the Crowd") and **Margin Weights Proportional to the Odds (MPTO)** — applies a single constant shift on the log-odds scale or distributes margin proportional to each outcome's odds, respectively ([CRAN `implied` vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html); [Buchdahl PDF](https://football-data.co.uk/The_Wisdom_of_the_Crowd_updated.pdf)).

The clearest peer-reviewed empirical comparison is **Štrumbelj (2014)**, *International Journal of Forecasting*, which tested basic normalization, Shin's procedure, and regression-based approaches across tennis, horse racing, and greyhound racing, finding **Shin's probabilities more accurate than basic normalization or regression models for all bookmaker/sport pairs tested**, though the Shin advantage shrinks as market size (liquidity, number of competitors) increases ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533)). A more recent comparison (Clarke et al., cited via [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326510904_Adjusting_Bookmaker's_Odds_to_Allow_for_Overround)) reports the power method "universally outperforms the multiplicative method and outperforms or is comparable to Shin's method," and a 2025 practitioner critique explicitly argues Shin's method is dated and should be retired in favor of power-method variants ([Algorithmic Sports Betting Substack](https://algorithmicsportsbetting.substack.com/p/its-time-to-retire-shins-method)) — this is a practitioner, not peer-reviewed, claim. A 2026 arXiv working paper comparing Numerical Shin, Analytical Shin, Power, and proportional normalization on European football odds via log-loss found the specialized methods generally beat plain proportional normalization, with the specific winner (Shin vs. power) varying by bookmaker ([arXiv:2604.17194](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17194)). Separately, Berkowitz, Depken & Gandar (2017, *Journal of Sports Economics*) showed that seven commonly cited money-line-to-probability conversion formulas actually collapse to only **three mathematically distinct estimators**, one of which is biased for very heavy favorites, yet still recommended standard proportional normalization for its simplicity and literature compatibility — a sport-general (not MLB-specific) finding that cuts against treating devigging-method choice as a solved problem ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2658335)).

The MLB-specific gap here is stark and should be stated plainly: **no peer-reviewed study was located that compares Shin's method, the power method, and proportional devigging head-to-head on MLB odds for calibration accuracy.** Existing MLB market-efficiency papers (the favorite-longshot-bias literature, the Harvard theses) almost uniformly use simple proportional normalization as their input probability, not because it was shown superior for baseball but because it is the field's historical default. It is plausible — but unverified — that because MLB moneylines are two-outcome, heavily bet, and liquid, the Shin/power advantage over proportional normalization (which Štrumbelj found shrinks with market size) would be smaller for MLB than for the thinner tennis, greyhound, and horse-racing markets where the method was validated; no source directly tests this. From a complexity standpoint, proportional and additive devigging are closed-form, one-line formulas; power and odds-ratio require a single-parameter numerical root-find per market; Shin's method and the structurally similar Balanced Book method require an iterative joint solve for an unobserved parameter — the most complex tier, but all are available as off-the-shelf library calls (R's `implied`, Python's `shin`) requiring no external training data beyond a single market's own odds ([CRAN `implied` vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html); [GitHub: mberk/shin](https://github.com/mberk/shin)).

## CLV is industry language for a hypothesis academia has not tested

Closing Line Value — the gap between a bettor's captured price and the market's final pre-game price — turns out to be **a practitioner concept with no peer-reviewed academic definition or validation**, not an established construct borrowed loosely from finance. No paper regresses bettor-level or cohort-level realized ROI on measured CLV; the industry claim that sustained positive CLV predicts long-run profitability is repeated as received wisdom across tracking-tool marketing (Pikkit, Unabated, SharpROI) without a cited academic source. What does exist is a large, adjacent **market-efficiency** literature testing whether the closing line itself is a good estimator of true probability — a related but distinct question from whether an individual bettor's CLV predicts their own future returns. Foundational work (Zuber, Gandar & Bowers 1985; Gandar, Zuber, O'Brien & Russo 1988) established the closing line as the standard efficiency benchmark and generally failed to reject market rationality, while still finding scattered profitable rules ([UNLV Gaming Research & Review](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?httpsredir=1&article=1123&context=grrj)). Steven Levitt's 2004 *Economic Journal* paper supplies an important theoretical counterweight: bookmakers may set prices to **exploit known, predictable bettor biases rather than to balance action**, meaning the closing line is not necessarily a pure aggregation of informed money and can deliberately depart from true probability ([Levitt 2004](https://pricetheory.uchicago.edu/levitt/Papers/LevittWhyAreGamblingMarkets2004.pdf)) — a finding that undercuts the implicit CLV assumption that "closing line ≈ true probability, so beating it ≈ positive expected value."

Multiple lines of evidence suggest the closing line carries real, documented biases of its own, which matters because CLV only measures a market-relative edge against that closing line, not an absolute edge against true probability. The favorite-longshot bias is "the most widely documented empirical regularity" in parimutuel betting, and Snowberg & Wolfers (2010, NBER/*Journal of Political Economy*) attribute it to genuine bettor probability-misperception rather than rational risk preference ([NBER](http://www.nber.org/papers/w15923.pdf)). A peer-reviewed sentiment-bias study using Facebook "Likes" as a popularity proxy across seven leagues found bookmakers price point spreads **0.6 points more favorable per percentage point of NFL popularity** for fan-favorite teams, and critically that "favorable prices do not translate into a higher probability of winning a bet" — a clean mechanism by which a closing line encodes non-probabilistic sentiment distortion ([Economic Inquiry](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ecinqu/v55y2017i2p1119-1129.html)). The single sharpest empirical check on the core CLV hypothesis — important to flag as **not peer-reviewed** but the most methodologically transparent counter-evidence located — is economist Karl Whelan's independent analysis of **3,670 NBA games (34,944 quotes, ~10 sportsbooks, 2022/23–2024/25 seasons)**: three of five positive-CLV deciles lost money on average, and only the top decile achieved a meaningful average return, directly contradicting the strong form of "positive CLV predicts profitability" ([Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/sportsbetting/the-truth-about-closing-line-value/)). There is also a documented moral-hazard problem: sportsbooks are reported to limit accounts specifically for consistently beating the closing line, with the Massachusetts Gaming Commission reportedly finding a correlation between CLV and account limiting ([closingline.substack.com](https://closingline.substack.com/p/sportsbook-limits-get-a-closer-look)) — meaning real-world datasets of sustained-high-CLV bettors are likely survivorship-biased.

No peer-reviewed paper anywhere uses the literal term "closing line value" in an MLB context — a clean, unambiguous gap — but baseball has one of the deepest market-efficiency literatures of any sport that functions as the closest substitute. Woodland & Woodland (1994) using MLB closing lines from 1979–1989 found the reverse favorite-longshot bias noted above ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:49:y:1994:i:1:p:269-79)), and the single best MLB analogue to a CLV-validation study, Simon's 2024 *Management Science* paper on 3,681 games, found MLB closing lines are "mostly reliable" but show statistically significant negatively autocorrelated overreaction exploitable by sophisticated bettors ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html)) — peer-reviewed evidence that an MLB CLV benchmark inherits a small, real amount of exploitable noise rather than measuring a pristine probability. No MLB paper studies line movement triggered by weather, lineups, or pitcher scratches specifically, and the widely repeated claim that popular teams (Yankees, Dodgers) are systematically overpriced due to public bias has no peer-reviewed MLB confirmation, surviving only as industry/betting-media assertion.

## Fixed-fraction Kelly is "theoretically unprincipled"; sports-betting-adjacent research offers two concrete upgrades

The classical Kelly criterion (Kelly 1956; popularized by Thorp from the 1960s) gives the bet fraction f* = (bp − q)/b that maximizes long-run expected log-wealth growth, and fractional Kelly — betting a fixed multiple like 0.5 or 0.25 of that stake — is standard practice that trades growth for variance reduction: **half-Kelly captures roughly 75% of full-Kelly growth at half the variance; quarter-Kelly captures roughly 44% of growth at a quarter the variance** ([topendsports.com](https://www.topendsports.com/sports-betting/tools/kelly-criterion-calculator/)). An experimental review across horse racing, NBA, and football data found that when a bettor's model is actually worse than the market's (negative edge), both full Kelly and a maximum-Sharpe strategy produced a **100% ruin rate**, while fractional Kelly avoided this and was judged "the most suitable option out of the strategies reviewed" ([arXiv:2107.08827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.08827)) — a direct caution for any MLB bettor who overestimates their model's edge against an efficient MLB market. But Chu, Wu & Swartz (2018, *Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports*) explicitly criticize ad-hoc fixed fractions like half- or quarter-Kelly as having "no theoretical underpinnings," and instead derive a decision-theoretic "modified Kelly" that produces a systematically smaller, principled fraction, tested via simulation on horse racing, basketball, and soccer ([DeGruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jqas-2017-0122/html?lang=en)). Baker & McHale (2013) independently derive a formal **shrinkage factor** that discounts the naive Kelly stake specifically as a function of estimation uncertainty in the win-probability input, validated via simulation and real tennis-betting data, showing shrunken-Kelly "offers an improvement over the raw Kelly criterion" ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262425087_Optimal_Betting_Under_Parameter_Uncertainty_Improving_the_Kelly_Criterion)). The key distinction from fixed fractional Kelly: these methods derive the discount from the *measured* uncertainty of each individual probability estimate, rather than applying the same constant multiplier regardless of how confident the model is in a given game.

A separate, architecturally distinct strand formally embeds a drawdown or ruin-probability constraint *inside* the Kelly optimization itself, producing a dynamic fraction that varies with current drawdown state, rather than a constant fraction plus an independently-chosen stop-loss rule bolted on afterward. Busseti, Ryu & Boyd's "Risk-Constrained Kelly Gambling" converts the chance-constrained drawdown problem into a convex optimization with one risk-aversion parameter, and report that risk-constrained bets **"out-perform fractional-Kelly bets for the same drawdown risk level or growth rate"** ([arXiv:1603.06183](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06183)). A related finance paper shows that under a formal drawdown constraint, the growth-optimal strategy is itself a *dynamic* fractional Kelly whose fraction depends on the currently realized drawdown — a state-dependent function of path history, not a fixed constant ([arXiv:1206.2305](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.2305); exact functional form not retrievable — abstract-level only). The sports-betting experimental review found a "drawdown-modified Kelly" achieved **0% ruin** across horse racing, basketball, and football while retaining strong growth (10.25x median final wealth in the football dataset) — the closest empirical sports-betting evidence that a principled drawdown-aware Kelly variant beats naive fixed-fraction-plus-stop-loss on ruin risk without materially sacrificing growth ([arXiv:2107.08827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.08827)). Architecturally, this is a genuinely different design than "quarter-Kelly plus a separate stop-loss": a fixed fraction with a bolted-on stop-loss is a two-stage heuristic where the bet-sizing rule and the loss-threshold rule are chosen independently, whereas the risk-constrained approaches solve one joint optimization where bet size continuously and endogenously responds to current wealth state, governed by a single tunable risk parameter instead of two separately-chosen heuristics.

Finally, a distinct and directly relevant literature addresses **simultaneous/correlated multi-bet Kelly** — exactly the situation of placing several bets across a roughly 15-game MLB slate on the same day. Naively applying single-bet Kelly independently to each game overstates safe total exposure when outcomes are correlated, and the mathematically correct joint-optimal strategy requires optimizing over the full joint outcome space, which scales as O(2^N) and becomes computationally intractable beyond a handful of simultaneous bets ([arXiv:0803.1364](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.1364)). Grant & Buchen (2013), using Premier League data, found that Kelly strategies using "multibets of all levels" (jointly optimized combination bets alongside singles) outperform a portfolio approach restricted to single-outcome bets ([Journal of Gambling Business and Economics](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/bucjgbeco/v_3a6_3ay_3a2012_3ai_3a2_3ap_3a1-28.htm)), building on Grant, Johnstone & Buchen's foundational "Optimal Betting Strategies for Simultaneous Games" (*Decision Analysis*, INFORMS) ([pubsonline.informs.org](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/deca.1080.0106)). No source directly quantifies typical outcome correlation across different, simultaneously-played MLB games (as opposed to correlation within a single game's bet legs); most MLB games on a given slate — different stadiums, different starting pitchers — are plausibly closer to independent than same-game parlay legs, which would make the correlation-correction more tractable for MLB than for sports/markets with more inherent same-slate correlation, but this is an unverified inference, not a cited finding. **No MLB-specific Kelly paper of any kind was found** — the uncertainty-shrinkage and drawdown-constrained literature is validated on tennis, horse racing, basketball, soccer, and football, and the simultaneous-bet literature is validated on English Premier League soccer; all MLB applicability is extrapolation.

## From literature to engine: what's a drop-in swap versus a rebuild

The table below maps each notable method or finding against the stated baseline — single-book proportional devigging, fixed-fraction (quarter/eighth) Kelly staking, and an independent drawdown-based stop-loss — and estimates implementation effort if adopted.

| Method / finding | Core idea | How it differs from baseline | Effort if adopted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shin's method (devig) | Models bookmaker pricing against informed "insider" bettors; redistributes more margin to longshots than proportional | Baseline spreads margin evenly; Shin reallocates based on an estimated insider proportion *z*. For MLB's typically two-outcome moneylines, *z* is solvable analytically (equivalent to additive devig) | **Small addition** — drop-in swap of the devig function; packaged libraries exist (R `implied`, Python `shin`); no new data inputs required |
| Power method (devig) | Raises each implied probability to a common exponent *k*, solved numerically so probabilities sum to 1 | Same motivation as Shin (correct favorite-longshot-style skew) but purely statistical, no economic model; requires a 1-D numerical root-find instead of closed-form division | **Small addition** — same swap-in profile as Shin; single-parameter numerical solve per market, near-instantaneous |
| Odds-ratio / MPTO (devig) | Shifts implied probabilities by a constant log-odds offset or margin proportional to each outcome's odds | Alternative single-parameter correction family; conceptually and implementation-wise similar complexity to power method | **Small addition** — same tier as power method |
| Multi-book consensus devigging | Use several books' lines (not just one) to form a consensus fair price before devigging | Baseline uses a single book; this requires ingesting and reconciling odds feeds from multiple sportsbooks | **Moderate** — not purely a formula swap; requires new data plumbing (multi-book odds ingestion, staleness/alignment handling) even though the devig math itself stays simple |
| Parameter-uncertainty shrinkage Kelly (Baker & McHale 2013; Chu/Wu/Swartz 2018) | Discount the Kelly stake by a factor derived from the *measured* uncertainty of each probability estimate, rather than a constant multiplier | Baseline's quarter/eighth fraction is fixed regardless of how confident the model is in a given game; shrinkage Kelly varies the discount bet-by-bet based on estimated variance of *p* | **Moderate** — requires the model to output (or the engine to estimate) a variance/confidence interval around each win-probability estimate, then a per-bet shrinkage calculation; not a one-line swap, but doesn't require re-architecting bet execution |
| Risk-constrained / drawdown-dynamic Kelly (Busseti, Ryu & Boyd; drawdown-constrained growth-optimal portfolios) | Embed a drawdown-probability constraint directly inside the sizing optimization, so the bet fraction is a continuous function of current wealth/drawdown state | Baseline treats sizing (fixed fraction) and risk control (separate stop-loss trigger) as two independently-chosen heuristics; this jointly optimizes both under one risk parameter | **Large architectural change** — requires replacing the sizing+stop-loss logic with a convex optimization solved per bet (or per period) as a function of path-dependent state, plus choosing/calibrating a risk-aversion parameter; changes the control flow of the staking engine, not just a formula |
| Simultaneous/correlated multi-bet Kelly (Grant, Johnstone & Buchen; Grant & Buchen 2013) | Jointly size bets across a same-day slate accounting for outcome correlation, instead of sizing each bet as if it were the only exposure | Baseline sizes each MLB game's bet independently; this requires a joint optimization over the slate's outcome space (intractable in full for N games, needs approximation/independence shortcuts) | **Large architectural change** — needs a correlation/covariance model across same-day bets, a tractable approximation to the O(2^N) joint problem, and a slate-level (not bet-level) sizing pass before any individual stake is finalized |
| CLV as a bet-quality signal | Track the gap between captured price and closing price as a performance proxy | Baseline staking/devig engine doesn't reference CLV at all; adding it is a monitoring/diagnostic layer, not a change to sizing or devigging logic itself | **Small addition** — logging and reporting feature; given the academic evidence that CLV is unvalidated and non-monotonic with ROI (Whelan), it should be treated as a soft diagnostic, not wired into automated sizing decisions |
| MLB-specific line-overreaction correction (Simon 2024 negative autocorrelation finding) | Line changes partially reverse; a sophisticated bettor could exploit the overreaction pattern | Baseline devigging/staking takes the current line at face value; this would require a timing/line-movement signal layered on top of the probability model | **Moderate to large** — requires new real-time line-movement tracking and a validated signal-generation step before it can feed into existing devig/Kelly logic; the underlying MLB evidence (one paper, 3,681 games) is suggestive but not yet a mature, replicated strategy |

## What is baseball-specific, what is borrowed, and what could not be verified

Several distinctions matter more than any single finding above. First, on **peer-review status**: the market-efficiency literature (Woodland & Woodland, Paul & Weinbach, Simon 2024, the 2024/2025 *Applied Economics* strategy-backtest paper) and the devigging-method comparison literature (Štrumbelj 2014, Berkowitz/Depken/Gandar 2017) are peer-reviewed; CLV as a concept, the Whelan NBA decile study, the Outlier.bet bookmaker-by-bookmaker devig comparison, and most weather/pitcher-information-lag claims are practitioner or industry sources and are flagged as such throughout. Second, on **sport specificity**: MLB has unusually deep market-efficiency literature (arguably the deepest of any sport given the Woodland & Woodland lineage running from 1994 to 2016 and Simon's 2024 *Management Science* paper), but has essentially **zero** direct literature on devigging-method comparison or any Kelly-criterion variant — every devigging and Kelly finding above that is not purely about market efficiency is borrowed from tennis (Baker & McHale), horse racing (Chu/Wu/Swartz; the robust-optimization line), European football/soccer (Grant & Buchen; Štrumbelj's comparison set partially), or NBA/NFL/finance (Busseti/Ryu/Boyd; Davis & Lleo; Whelan). Third, several specific items could not be verified during this research and should not be treated as settled: the exact closed-form shrinkage factor in Baker & McHale could not be extracted (full text blocked); Clarke (2017)'s exact power-method formulas and results could not be parsed from the hosted PDF; the precise numerical magnitude of Grant & Buchen's multibet-vs-single-bet performance advantage was unavailable beyond a qualitative "outperforms"; several key papers (both Harvard theses, the Woodland & Woodland 2015/2016 season win-totals paper, the MDPI 2023 prospect-theory paper) were only accessible via search-engine summaries due to 403/429 errors or identity-provider redirects, not direct full-text extraction. Readers relying on this report for implementation decisions involving exact coefficients, confidence intervals, or sample sizes for any of these specific items should treat the qualitative direction of the finding as reliable but the precise numbers as unverified pending institutional journal access.

## Conclusion

The four literatures converge on an unexpected asymmetry: the topic with the strongest, most MLB-specific peer-reviewed evidence (market efficiency) offers the least actionable guidance for immediate engineering changes, since "the market is mostly efficient" mainly tells you *not* to expect easy wins rather than how to build a better engine — while the topic with almost no MLB-specific evidence at all (Kelly-criterion variants) offers the clearest, best-theorized upgrade path, because parameter-uncertainty shrinkage and risk-constrained dynamic sizing are general mathematical results that don't actually require baseball-specific validation to apply correctly, only a baseball-specific probability model to feed them. The practical sequencing this suggests is to treat devigging-method upgrades (Shin, power) as low-risk, low-cost swaps worth making regardless of MLB-specific validation gaps, since both methods are theoretically well-motivated and empirically favored over proportional devigging in every sport where they've been tested; to treat CLV purely as a diagnostic overlay given the Whelan non-monotonicity finding, not as an input to automated decisions; and to reserve the genuinely large architectural investments — risk-constrained dynamic Kelly and correlated slate-level sizing — for a point where the underlying probability model's edge and uncertainty are well-characterized enough that the sophistication of the sizing rule is no longer the binding constraint on performance.
