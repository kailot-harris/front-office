# Machine Learning Applied to MLB Hitter Performance Projection

## What published academic papers or rigorous technical writeups use ML to project MLB hitting stats, and what algorithms do they use?

### Takeaway
The literature splits into three tiers: (1) a handful of arXiv/academic papers predicting career-level value (WAR) or counting stats (home runs) with classical ML (random forest, ridge regression, neural nets, SVR) and LSTM/RNN deep learning; (2) non-peer-reviewed but methodologically rigorous community writeups (FanGraphs Community Blog, independent Medium/dev.to analysts) that build neural nets, KNN, or gradient-boosted (LightGBM) models specifically to project or classify rate stats like wOBA; and (3) a 2025 MDPI systematic review surveying the whole field. Random forest and gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) are the most common tabular-data algorithms; LSTM/RNN deep learning appears in at least one arXiv paper for sequential home-run forecasting; shallow feed-forward neural nets and KNN appear in community research on wOBA classification/regression.

### Cited Findings
- Bierig, Hollenbeck & Stroud (Stanford), "Understanding Career Progression in Baseball Through Machine Learning," arXiv:1712.05754 (Dec 2017, stat.ML) — trained four ML models (ridge/L2 linear regression, multi-layer perceptron neural network, random forest/bagging ensemble of 100 trees, epsilon-SVR with Gaussian kernel) on batters and pitchers separately, using players with ≥7 MLB seasons since 1970 (Lahman/Baseball-Reference data), to predict future-season WAR from first-6-season features (plus age, handedness, position, one-hot encoded). Feature selection via scikit-learn Recursive Feature Elimination narrowed ~200 correlated features to 15–20. Hyperparameters tuned via grid search with 3-fold CV on an 80% train split; evaluated on a held-out 20% test set. — [arXiv:1712.05754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.05754)
- For batters, all four ML models explained ~60% of variance in future WAR (R²≈0.60, dipping to ~0.55 in the 3rd prediction year); pitcher models only reached R²≈0.30–0.40, attributed to injury variance. No single ML algorithm (ridge, NN, RF, SVR) clearly dominated the others for batters, but all four "soundly" and "handily" beat a naive Delta Method baseline (Tom Tango's approach) and produced visibly tighter prediction distributions. Cumulative first-6-season WAR and 6th-season WAR were the single most predictive features for years 7–8; debut age became more important by years 9–11. — [arXiv:1712.05754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.05754)
- Sun, Lin & Tsai (Claremont Graduate University / National Taiwan University / National Chengchi University), "Performance Prediction in Major League Baseball by Long Short-Term Memory Networks," arXiv:2206.09654 (June 2022, cs.LG) — target variable is home runs; dataset of 5,401 MLB players 1961–2019 with 21 features (performance + physical attributes), filtered to ≥50 PA/season; trained on 9,828 player-seasons (1961–2017), tested on 375 player-seasons (2018–2019). Compared five LSTM architecture variants (incl. GRU, BiLSTM, Attention-LSTM) against linear regression, SVM, random forest, and the sZymborski/ZiPS projection system. — [arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654)
- LSTM models reported 10–13% exact-match accuracy (predicted HR total matching actual exactly) and 20–24% accuracy within ±1 home run, "substantially higher" than ZiPS and linear regression, with LSTM outperforming other methods particularly for players hitting under 30 HR; all methods, including LSTM, struggled with elite power hitters (40+ HR seasons). Precise MAE/RMSE values for each model were not extractable from available summaries — flagged as a gap below. — [arXiv:2206.09654 via alphaxiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2206.09654)
- A 2025 MDPI systematic review, "Machine Learning in Baseball Analytics: Sabermetrics and Beyond" (Information journal, vol. 16, no. 5, article 361, 2025), surveys ML applications across baseball analytics, including player-performance prediction and player valuation; it identifies random forest and support vector machine as the most popular ML models in the field overall, and Baseball Savant and Baseball Reference as the most popular underlying data repositories. Full text was inaccessible (403 errors on direct and ResearchGate mirrors) so specific hitter-projection accuracy figures from this review could not be extracted. — [MDPI 2078-2489/16/5/361](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/16/5/361)
- Shibata et al., "Consistency verification and interpretation of explainable AI for predicting annual home runs of professional baseball players from sensor data," Scientific Reports, 2025 — used bat-mounted IMU sensor data (not Statcast) with five features (bat speed, bat mass, attack angle, on-plane efficiency, rotational acceleration) to predict annual home run totals via Random Forest (compared against Gradient Boosting and multiple linear regression). Random Forest: test R² = 0.56 ± 0.08, RMSE = 5.30 ± 0.51 home runs, beating Gradient Boosting (R² = 0.49) and linear regression (R² = 0.52). Trained on 295 swings from 31 players (2023), tested on 30 swings from 10 different players (2024), a 90/10-style cross-player holdout. SHAP, partial dependence, and ICE plots used for interpretability. — [PMC12464232 / Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12464232/)

### Inferences
- Academic/arXiv-level work on hitters skews toward predicting aggregate value (WAR) or a single counting stat (home runs) rather than directly projecting rate stats like wOBA/OBP/SLG/AVG — the more granular rate-stat projection work (below) is concentrated in non-peer-reviewed but methodologically transparent community writeups rather than formal papers.
- Ensemble tree methods (random forest, gradient boosting) and neural networks perform comparably on these problems in the papers reviewed; no architecture shows a decisive, consistent edge across all studies — LSTM's edge in the Sun/Lin/Tsai paper is specific to home-run forecasting, a highly skewed/rare-event target, where sequence modeling of year-over-year power trends may matter more than for rate stats.

## What features are used — traditional box-score rolling stats only, or Statcast features (exit velocity, launch angle, barrel rate, hard-hit rate, sprint speed)?

### Takeaway
Across the sources found, there is a clear and fairly consistent pattern: models using only traditional/box-score and plate-discipline features achieve moderate accuracy, and every source that tested adding Statcast features (exit velocity, barrel rate, hard-hit%, launch angle) found it measurably improved predictive/classification accuracy, sometimes substantially.

### Cited Findings
- FanGraphs Community Blog, Michal Kokta, "Predicting wOBA Using Process-Based Statistics" (July 19, 2021) — built a shallow neural network (3 hidden layers, 8 units each) to binary-classify whether a batter-season's wOBA exceeds .320 (~league average), using 989 qualified batter-seasons (2015–June 2021), 80/20 train-test split (791/198). Plate-discipline-only features (zone/out-of-zone contact rate, swing rate, swing-and-miss rate — 6 features): 69.19% test accuracy. Adding average exit velocity: 75.25%. Adding barrel rate instead: 71.72%. All features combined (plate discipline + exit velocity + barrel rate): 81.31% test accuracy — a 12-point accuracy gain over discipline-only. — [FanGraphs Community](https://community.fangraphs.com/predicting-woba-using-process-based-statistics/)
- YMori (dev.to), "Can Statcast Data Improve MLB Player Performance Predictions? — Beating Marcel with LightGBM" (posted ~March 2026, per article dating) — built a LightGBM gradient-boosting model with Optuna hyperparameter tuning (1,000 trials) to project batter wOBA and pitcher xFIP, benchmarked against the Marcel projection system plus an ElasticNet Bayesian correction layer. Features compared: box-score aggregates (K%, BB%, BABIP, ERA) vs. Statcast tracking data (exit velocity, barrel rate, launch angle, hard-hit%, contact rate, sprint speed, whiff rate, Stuff+/Pitching+ for pitchers). Validation: strict time-series expanding-window backtest — trained 2015–2019, cross-validated 2020–2024, held out 2025 as fully unseen test year. — [dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)
- Same source: on the 2025 holdout year, Marcel's batter wOBA MAE was 0.0331 vs. the LightGBM/Statcast model's 0.0291 — a 12.1% MAE reduction. (Pitcher xFIP improved 4.0%, from 0.5038 to 0.4837 MAE.) The ML+Statcast model beat Marcel in all 6 backtested years for batters (5 of 6 years for pitchers). The author attributes the batter improvement specifically to exit velocity and contact-quality metrics carrying predictive signal independent of traditional aggregate stats (identified via an ElasticNet residual-model analysis). — [dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)
- Dan Szymborski (FanGraphs/ESPN analyst), "Yes, Hitter xStats Are Useful," FanGraphs (Aug 29, 2023) — non-ML but rigorous statistical analysis of Statcast's "expected" stats (xBA, xOBP, xSLG, xwOBA, which are themselves derived from a Statcast ML model based on exit velocity/launch angle similarity matching) as year-over-year predictors. Analyzed >1,800 consecutive season-pairs (200+ PA) since 2015, computing r² of year-1 stat vs. year-2 actual performance. xStats modestly outperformed same-named actual stats as single predictors: xBA r²=0.173 vs actual BA r²=0.163; xOBP 0.236 vs 0.210; xSLG 0.226 vs 0.189; xwOBA 0.221 vs actual wOBA 0.179. Optimal blended-prediction weighting was roughly 59–73% xStat / 27–41% actual stat, i.e., xStats add value in combination with, not as a full replacement for, actual results. — [FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/yes-hitter-xstats-are-useful/)
- Statcast's own xwOBA/xBA/xSLG "expected" stat family is itself produced by a machine learning model: it assigns outcome probabilities to batted balls based on similarity (nearest-neighbor-style) in exit velocity and launch angle to historically similar batted balls — meaning Statcast's core "expected stats" infrastructure is itself an ML projection layer that downstream hitter-projection work (above) often builds on top of. — [MLB Technology Blog: An Introduction to xwOBA](https://technology.mlblogs.com/an-introduction-to-expected-weighted-on-base-average-xwoba-29d6070ba52b)
- A WebSearch-surfaced summary (full article not independently fetchable/403) describes a KNN-based model ("Modelling xwOBA (With KNN)" by Thomas James Nestico, Medium) achieving 76% accuracy predicting total bases of test batted balls from launch speed and launch angle alone — consistent with Statcast's own methodology of neighbor-similarity matching on exit velocity/launch angle. Treat this single figure with caution since the full article could not be verified directly (403 error on fetch). — [Medium (unverified detail)](https://medium.com/@thomasjamesnestico/modelling-xwoba-with-knn-9b004e93861a)

### Inferences
- The magnitude of Statcast's incremental value appears context-dependent: it is large for season-level classification tasks on a relatively small, curated feature set (FanGraphs community NN: +12pts accuracy) and moderate-but-consistent for full-season rate-stat regression against a strong existing baseline like Marcel (LightGBM: +12.1% MAE reduction for batters). In both cases, Statcast features like exit velocity/barrel rate were the single most impactful addition beyond basic plate-discipline/box-score inputs.
- None of the academic/arXiv-level papers found (WAR career progression, LSTM home run paper) used Statcast features at all — those used only traditional box-score-era statistics (even the 2022 LSTM paper's data ran only through 2019 and used classic counting/rate stats, not exit velocity/launch angle), suggesting a gap between peer-reviewed academic work (still box-score-only) and more recent community/independent technical work (incorporating Statcast).

### Gaps
- Could not verify exact methodology or validate accuracy claims for the KNN xwOBA Medium post (403 error on direct fetch; details above come only from search snippets) — flagged as lower-confidence.
- No source found that isolates sprint speed's specific marginal contribution to hitter rate-stat projection accuracy (sprint speed is mentioned as an available Statcast feature in the LightGBM writeup's feature list but its individual effect size is not broken out).

## What is each paper/system validated against, and what accuracy is reported?

### Takeaway
Validation approaches range from simple train/test splits (academic papers) to rigorous time-series expanding-window backtests against the Marcel projection system (community LightGBM work) to large-sample retrospective correlation analysis against actual outcomes (Szymborski/FanGraphs). Reported accuracy is heterogeneous by target: WAR career-value R²≈0.60 for batters; wOBA classification accuracy 69–81% depending on feature set; wOBA regression MAE improves ~12% over Marcel when adding Statcast+ML; home run forecasting LSTM gets 10–13% exact-match / 20–24% within-one-HR accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- arXiv:1712.05754 (Bierig et al.) validated via 80/20 train-test split with 3-fold CV for hyperparameter tuning; reported R² on held-out test set (~0.60 for batters across 4 ML models and 5 prediction horizons, years 7–11 of career). Baseline comparator: the "Delta Method" (Tom Tango's simple aging-curve approach), which all four ML models "handily beat." — [arXiv:1712.05754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.05754)
- arXiv:2206.09654 (Sun, Lin & Tsai) validated on a true out-of-sample holdout (2018–2019 seasons, 375 player-seasons) after training on 1961–2017 data (9,828 player-seasons); baseline comparator was the ZiPS ("sZymborski") projection system plus linear regression, SVM, and random forest. LSTM reported as "substantially" outperforming ZiPS in accuracy-within-range metrics (10–13% exact, 20–24% within ±1 HR), though precise ZiPS-specific error numbers were not extractable. — [arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654)
- dev.to LightGBM/Statcast study used strict chronological backtesting (train 2015–2019, validate/tune 2020–2024, fully-unseen holdout 2025) against the Marcel projection system, widely regarded as a transparent, conservative public baseline in sabermetrics. Reported MAE improvement for batter wOBA: 12.1% (0.0331 → 0.0291), with the ML+Statcast model beating Marcel in 6 of 6 backtested years for batters. — [dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)
- FanGraphs Community (Kokta) validated via single 80/20 split (no cross-validation or multi-year holdout mentioned) on 989 batter-seasons 2015–June 2021; this is a less rigorous validation design than the time-series backtests used elsewhere, and the classification task (wOBA above/below .320) is coarser than a continuous rate-stat projection. — [FanGraphs Community](https://community.fangraphs.com/predicting-woba-using-process-based-statistics/)
- Szymborski/FanGraphs xStats analysis is not a predictive model per se but a retrospective correlation study (r² of year 1 → year 2 performance) across >1,800 season-pairs since 2015 — useful as a baseline accuracy benchmark for "how predictive is any single-year rate stat of itself next year," against which ML-based systems can be compared (e.g., xwOBA's raw r²=0.221 is a useful anchor point for interpreting the stronger backtested accuracy gains reported by the LightGBM/Marcel study, which uses multi-year regression-to-mean and additional features rather than raw single-year correlation). — [FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/yes-hitter-xstats-are-useful/)

### Inferences
- The most rigorous and most directly comparable validation methodology among sources found is the dev.to LightGBM-vs-Marcel backtest, because it uses genuine expanding-window, no-leakage, multi-year chronological validation against a well-known industry-standard baseline (Marcel) — this makes its reported +12.1% MAE improvement figure the strongest available quantification in these notes of "does adding Statcast data measurably improve projection accuracy."
- None of the sources found validate against the more sophisticated proprietary systems named in the original brief (Steamer, ZiPS, PECOTA, THE BAT) with a head-to-head ML comparison — ZiPS is used only as a loose baseline in the LSTM home-run paper (not a full rate-stat projection comparison), and Marcel (not Steamer/PECOTA/THE BAT) is the ML-era benchmark of choice in the dev.to study, likely because Marcel's formula is fully public/replicable while Steamer/ZiPS/PECOTA/THE BAT are proprietary black boxes unsuitable for exact backtesting.

### Gaps
- No source found directly benchmarks an ML/Statcast hitter projection system against Steamer, ZiPS, PECOTA, or THE BAT specifically (as opposed to Marcel or an internal ML baseline) — this is a significant open gap relative to the original key question, likely because those systems' internals and historical year-by-year projections are not fully public/replicable for independent researchers to backtest against.
- Could not find a source directly reporting RMSE (as opposed to MAE, r², or classification accuracy) for a full wOBA/OBP/SLG/AVG rate-stat regression model, limiting cross-study comparability on a single consistent metric.
