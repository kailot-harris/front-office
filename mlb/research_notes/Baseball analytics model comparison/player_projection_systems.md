# Fancier models barely beat a 2004 baseline

The research record on MLB projection systems delivers a counterintuitive verdict: two decades of methodological escalation — from Marcel's bare-bones three-year weighted average through PECOTA's comparable-player matching, ZiPS's aging curves, Steamer's calibrated regression, THE BAT X's Statcast "stuff" layer, and now gradient-boosted trees and neural sequence models — has produced only **modest, inconsistent accuracy gains**, most on the order of a few percent of RMSE/MAE and some outright noise. The single most-cited quantitative comparison (Dan Meyer, The Hardball Times, 2014) found hitter wOBA MAE ranging from .0274 (ZiPS, best) to .0289 (Marcel, worst) — a spread Meyer himself called "subtle" ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/evaluating-the-2014-projection-systems/)). The strongest documented machine-learning improvement over a Marcel baseline — a Statcast-enriched LightGBM model beating Marcel's batter wOBA MAE by 12.1% (.0331 → .0291) in a rigorous chronological backtest — still leaves the ceiling for explainable variance stuck around R²≈0.2–0.3 for pitcher outcomes and ≈0.6 for aggregate hitter value, the same ceiling every method in this review runs into ([dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)). For a pipeline already running closed-form empirical Bayes shrinkage on wOBA/ISO/xFIP/K%/BB% with park-factor adjustment, nearly every published alternative — PECOTA, ZiPS, Steamer, THE BAT, hierarchical Bayesian models, random forests, gradient boosting, and most deep learning — sits on a spectrum from "small formula swap" to "genuinely new data-engineering project," and almost none of them has been shown, in a rigorous peer-reviewed benchmark, to decisively outperform that baseline on classic rate stats. The one place the literature shows outsized, reproducible gains is pitch-level Statcast event modeling (Stuff+-family, pitch-sequence transformers) — but that requires standing up pitch-by-pitch ingestion the baseline doesn't have, a genuinely large architectural change, not an incremental one.

## No peer-reviewed benchmark actually settles Marcel vs. PECOTA vs. ZiPS vs. Steamer vs. THE BAT

Despite being the most commercially visible comparison in sabermetrics, there is **no JQAS-caliber, peer-reviewed paper** that benchmarks all five named systems against each other on RMSE/MAE/correlation for wOBA, ERA, or FIP. Every rigorous head-to-head found in this research is a sabermetric blog or trade-press piece. Matt Swartz's 2012 FanGraphs test (2011 season) found, for 200+ PA hitters: PECOTA RMSE .0317/corr .6136 (best), ZiPS RMSE .0318/corr .6139, Oliver RMSE .0321, Steamer RMSE .0322, Marcel RMSE .0330/corr .5614 (worst), CAIRO RMSE .0333 ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/testing-projections-for-2011/)). For pitchers (40+ IP), Steamer led on all three metrics (RMSE .8324), attributed to its integration of velocity data. Dan Meyer's 2014 THT study (177,967 PA) ranked ZiPS best on MAE (.0274) and R² (.330), Marcel worst (.0289, R²=.240) ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/evaluating-the-2014-projection-systems/)). Colin Wyers' 1950–2010 Baseball Prospectus test found simplified PECOTA and Marcel in a "dead heat" on offense RMSE, with Marcel marginally ahead on pitching ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/12884/reintroducing-pecota-theyre-here/)). More recently, FantasyPros' annual z-score-based accuracy study — not a classic RMSE framework, but the longest-running continuous comparison — found **THE BAT X** the most accurate *original* (non-consensus) system for six straight years through 2024, the first original system ever to beat consensus/blended projections (score −1.874 vs. Steamer's −0.649 and ZiPS's −0.138) ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/2025/02/most-accurate-fantasy-baseball-projections-2024-results/)). The one genuine academic paper engaging any of these systems, Gerber & Craig's 2021 JQAS mixed-effects multinomial logistic-normal model, explicitly benchmarks against Marcel as "the most established publicly accessible prediction methodology," but its full quantitative results could not be retrieved in this research ([JQAS](https://doi.org/10.1515/jqas-2020-0007)). Tom Tango himself — Marcel's creator — frames the entire comparison exercise bluntly: more elaborate systems "have only added a small increase in accuracy" over Marcel "despite doing substantially more work," and he treats any forecaster who doesn't publish after-the-fact accuracy results as "Marcel-equivalent until proven superior" ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/tangotigers-projection-tests/)). Methodologically, every system shares the same skeleton — multi-year weighted history, regression to the mean, an age adjustment — and the differences are refinements layered on top: PECOTA's defining feature is k-nearest-neighbor comparable-player matching across a historical database rather than parametric regression, rebuilt entirely in 2020 into a proprietary engine now integrated with BP's "Deserved" metrics (DRA/DRC+) ([SABR](https://sabr.org/latest/judge-introducing-pecota-2020/)); ZiPS adds DIPS-theory pitcher regression, comparable-player aging curves, and million-simulation Monte Carlo team projections; Steamer derives its weighting and regression strength from historical regression analysis rather than fixed weights; and **THE BAT X is the only system with a confirmed, disclosed ML/Statcast component** — a proprietary "stuff model" (THE BATcast) built on pitch-tracking data plus machine-learning-built DFS ownership projections — though creator Derek Carty has never published a technical methodology paper, leaving the core hitter/pitcher projection internals a partial black box ([RotoGrinders](https://rotogrinders.com/pages/the-bat-x-projections-the-bat-plus-statcast-3361569)). For a Marcel-plus-closed-form-EB-shrinkage pipeline, replicating PECOTA's k-NN comp-matching or ZiPS's Monte Carlo aging-curve machinery would be a **moderate addition** (new algorithm family, same data), while replicating THE BAT X would require standing up Statcast ingestion — a larger lift addressed below.

## Hierarchical Bayesian models beat naive baselines by 2–3x, but barely edge closed-form shrinkage

The Bayesian shrinkage literature traces a clean methodological ladder, and the accuracy gains compress sharply as the ladder gets more sophisticated. At the bottom, Efron & Morris's 1975 James-Stein estimator reduced total squared prediction error for batting average from 0.086 (raw sample mean) to 0.027 — roughly a **3.2x improvement** — on Roberto Clemente's and 17 other players' 1970 at-bats ([Efron & Morris](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios602/MultilevelData/EfronMorrisJASA1975.pdf)). That gain — shrinkage beats no-shrinkage — is universal and large across every study reviewed. The gain from *simple* closed-form shrinkage to *fully hierarchical* Bayesian models is far smaller and context-dependent. Jensen, McShane & Wyner's 2009 "Bayesball" model — a three-level hierarchy combining position-specific intercepts, ballpark effects, cubic-B-spline age curves, and a hidden Markov "elite vs. non-elite" mixture, estimated via Gibbs sampling — achieved internal validation RMSE of 5.30 home runs versus 8.24 for a naive "repeat last year" baseline (a 36% reduction), but externally, against PECOTA (RMSE 7.11) and Marcel (RMSE 7.82) on 118 elite hitters, the hierarchical model's RMSE of 7.33 was **worse than PECOTA and only modestly better than Marcel** ([arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)). Its one clear edge was among young players (age ≤26), where it won 62% of head-to-head comparisons against both commercial systems — precisely where a recency-weighted baseline like Marcel is structurally weakest due to limited track record. Removing just the elite/non-elite mixture component degraded the model's RMSE by roughly 30%, more than removing the position/park covariates, indicating the heterogeneous-mixture shrinkage (letting the amount of shrinkage vary by latent subgroup rather than smoothly by sample size alone) is the single highest-leverage structural addition. Brown's 2008 *Annals of Applied Statistics* field test of six batting-average shrinkage variants found an even more sobering pattern: a nonparametric empirical Bayes procedure won on the full heterogeneous player population, but **lost to simpler, more familiar parametric EB methods once the population was split into more homogeneous subgroups** (by position or playing time) — direct evidence that estimator sophistication matters less than whether the shrinkage target population is homogeneous ([arXiv:0803.3697](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3697)). A 2025 "Clemente Problem" robust-Bayes paper revisited the original Efron-Morris dataset and showed the classic estimator over-shrinks genuinely exceptional players (Clemente's .400 was pulled to a predicted .290 when his true rest-of-season average was .346), fixing this with heavy-tailed (Cauchy/double-exponential) priors instead of the conjugate light-tailed prior ([arXiv:2506.10114](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10114)).

Translating this ladder into adoption cost for a pipeline already doing closed-form shrinkage toward a league/positional mean: covariate-adjusted priors (shrinking toward a park- or position-specific mean instead of one global mean) and a two-component mixture to avoid over-shrinking elite performers are achievable **without MCMC** — Brown's regression-EB variants and David Robinson's beta-binomial-regression extension (varianceexplained.org) remain closed-form/empirical-Bayes-estimated ([varianceexplained.org](http://varianceexplained.org/r/empirical_bayes_baseball/)). That is a **small addition**. Full Jensen-et-al.-style hierarchical modeling — B-spline age curves, hidden Markov elite-status transitions, Gibbs sampling, posterior predictive distributions — or Gu & Koenker's nonparametric longitudinal EB (jointly estimating player location *and* volatility via convex-optimization NPMLE) is a **large architectural change**: new estimation infrastructure (MCMC or convex optimization), new covariate data, and — per the Jensen et al. results — a payoff that is real but modest and concentrated in specific subpopulations (young players) rather than a uniform win. Notably, the pitching side of this literature is thin: no peer-reviewed hierarchical Bayes paper was found targeting ERA, FIP, K%, or BB% directly as the outcome (the closest, Brill, Deshpande & Wyner's 2022/2023 JQAS paper on the "time through the order" penalty, uses hierarchical random effects for a different question — isolating pitcher/batter quality confounds, not building a general projection system) ([arXiv:2210.06724](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06724)). Any extension of EB shrinkage to pitching rate stats needs separately-calibrated prior variance per stat regardless of architecture — K% is markedly more "ability-driven" (lower year-to-year volatility relative to signal) than batting average, so reusing batting-calibrated shrinkage weights would over-shrink pitchers' K%, a parameter-tuning issue rather than evidence that a bigger model is needed.

## Tree ensembles are a drop-in swap; sequence and transformer architectures are a new department

Machine learning's relationship to traditional systems inverts the intuitive assumption that newer, fancier models win outright. **Random forest, gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost), ridge regression, and shallow neural networks perform comparably** on tabular season-level MLB prediction — no algorithm family shows a decisive edge. The 2017 Stanford paper on career-WAR progression trained four parallel models (ridge, MLP, 100-tree random forest, SVR) and all four explained roughly **R²≈0.60** of future batter WAR variance, with none clearly beating the others, though all four "handily beat" Tom Tango's Delta Method baseline; pitcher models in the same study only reached R²≈0.30–0.40 ([arXiv:1712.05754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.05754)). The most rigorous and directly relevant backtest found in this research — a time-series expanding-window test (train 2015–2019, holdout 2025) of Statcast-enriched LightGBM against the Marcel projection system — found the ML model beat Marcel's batter wOBA MAE by **12.1%** (.0331→.0291) and pitcher xFIP MAE by **4.0%** (.5038→.4837), winning in 6 of 6 backtested batter-years and 5 of 6 pitcher-years ([dev.to](https://dev.to/yasumorishima/can-statcast-data-improve-mlb-player-performance-predictions-beating-marcel-with-lightgbm-1lb5)). This is the single strongest, most methodologically credible piece of evidence in the entire research base that a tree-ensemble-plus-Statcast upgrade measurably beats the exact baseline architecture (Marcel) under discussion here. Because tree ensembles consume the same tabular, season-level feature rows that a closed-form shrinkage pipeline already produces, swapping the shrinkage/regression step for XGBoost or LightGBM on rolling box-score aggregates is a **small, low-risk addition** — a model-library change, not a new data pipeline. Adding Statcast contact-quality features (exit velocity, barrel rate, Hard-Hit%) on top is **small-to-moderate**, since the data is free and public via Baseball Savant/`pybaseball` from 2015 onward and the main cost is feature engineering, not acquisition ([pybaseball docs](https://github.com/jldbc/pybaseball/blob/master/docs/statcast.md)).

Sequence and transformer architectures are a different proposition entirely, and the evidence for them is both thinner and more contested. The clearest peer-reviewed deep-learning win is narrow: Sun, Lin & Tsai's 2022/2023 LSTM paper (*International Journal of Data Science and Analytics*) trained five LSTM variants on 5,401 players (1961–2019) to predict home runs, beating ZiPS and classical ML baselines with 10–13% exact-match accuracy and 20–24% accuracy within ±1 HR on a 2018–2019 holdout, with gains concentrated among sub-30-HR players ([arXiv:2206.09654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.09654)). But this is a single-stat, skewed-target result (home run counts, not wOBA/ERA/FIP), and precise MAE/RMSE deltas versus ZiPS could not be extracted even from the paper's own supplementary materials. Beyond home runs, evidence for sequence architectures on standard rate stats is genuinely mixed and includes an explicit null result: an open-source project building GRU and transformer models over a hitter's last 600 plate appearances found, across three random seeds, that the sequence models matched a plain MLP baseline within noise — the author's own conclusion was "without pretraining, the sequence adds nothing" ([GitHub Issue #414](https://github.com/alhart2015/FantasyBaseball/issues/414)). Where sequence/transformer architectures *do* show convincing, reproducible gains — Heaton & Mitra's Penn State "contextual event embeddings" (treating pitch-by-pitch data as language-model token sequences, producing single-game strikeout/hit predictions competitive with major sportsbooks from just 10 games of history) and Lee & Kim's 2025 Temporal Fusion Transformer for pitcher ERA (peer-reviewed, *Computers, Materials & Continua*) — the gain traces to **finer-grained input data** (pitch-level Statcast event context), not the architecture itself ([Sloan](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/learning-contextual-event-embeddings-to-predict-player-performance-in-the-mlb)). Architecture and data granularity are confounded throughout this literature: season-sequence LSTMs on the same box-score stats show disputed benefit, while pitch-sequence transformers on genuinely granular event data show the strongest, most reproducible gains. That distinction is the crux of the adoption question: swapping algorithms on the baseline's existing tabular features is cheap and modestly accretive; building pitch-sequence infrastructure to unlock the bigger gains is a **large architectural change** requiring new data ingestion, embedding infrastructure, and a materially different engineering skill set.

## Statcast-derived "Stuff+" and pitch-quality models require a new data department, not a new formula

Separately from general ML-vs-traditional-system comparisons, a distinct and more mature body of work covers Statcast-based pitch-quality modeling — Stuff+, Location+, Pitching+, and public variants (tjStuff+, aStuff+, PitchingBot) — which are themselves supervised ML models (typically gradient-boosted/decision-tree regressors) trained on per-pitch physical characteristics (velocity, spin rate/axis, release point, horizontal/vertical movement) to predict run value per pitch, then aggregated and index-scaled ([FanGraphs Library](https://library.fangraphs.com/pitching/stuff-location-and-pitching-primer/)). Baseball Prospectus's most rigorous head-to-head of ERA estimators found context-adjusted mixed-model metrics (DRA, cFIP) outperforming both the FIP-family and raw pitch-quality metrics on year-ahead predictive correlation: DRA 0.26, cFIP 0.25, xwOBA/xERA 0.23, xFIP 0.19, FIP 0.19, SIERA 0.18 ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/82426/prospectus-feature-an-updated-evaluation-of-hitting-and-pitching-including-stuff-metrics/)). Critically, that same analysis found raw Stuff+ correlates with next-year run prevention at 0.41 for pitchers who stayed on the same team but only 0.14 for pitchers who switched teams — strong evidence that Stuff+ as typically computed **conflates team/park/catcher context with portable pitcher skill**, a caveat any team adopting it as a standalone predictor needs to internalize. Relative to the baseline pipeline under discussion, this reveals a clean three-tier cost structure. SIERA and xFIP require no new data at all — they are closed-form formulas over the same FanGraphs-leaderboard rate stats (K%, BB%, GB%/FB%) the baseline already computes, so adding either is a **trivial, small addition**. DRA/cFIP-style mixed-effects estimators are a **moderate addition**: they need play-by-play participant identity and context (batter, catcher, umpire, park) per plate appearance — a step up from leaderboard rates but still not pitch-level tracking data. Stuff+-family models and the pitch-sequencing literature (directed-graph embeddings over 3.5 million pitches finding "setup" and "knockout" pitch patterns; a 2026 Transformer-based counterfactual-optimization paper estimating sequencing effects could shift K/9 by more than 1.0) are a **large architectural change**: every one of them requires full pitch-by-pitch Statcast ingestion — velocity, spin, movement, release point, location, pitch type per pitch — which the formula-based baseline simply does not have ([Sloan graph-embeddings paper](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/decoding-mlb-pitch-sequencing-strategies-via-directed-graph-embeddings); [arXiv:2606.17345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17345)). On park-factor methodology specifically, the academic trajectory runs a similar path: simple multiplicative ratios → Acharya et al.'s 2008 JQAS ANOVA-weighted fixed-effects model with a schedule-aware Neutral Park Adjustment (a **small-to-moderate** upgrade using the same run/event data, just re-estimated via regression) → newer personnel-adjusted GLMM/Poisson models controlling for which batters/pitchers actually played in each park (a **larger** addition requiring "elsewhere" ability measures for every player) ([Acharya et al. 2008](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/jqsprt/v4y2008i2n4.html); [arXiv:2506.22350](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22350)).

## Statcast's own "expected stats" are a cautionary tale about assumed superiority

One finding cuts against the instinct that more granular data always helps: Statcast's flagship "expected stats" (xwOBA, xBA, xSLG, xERA) — themselves an ML layer, built via nearest-neighbor similarity-matching on exit velocity/launch angle — do **not** clearly outperform traditional metrics as forward predictors, despite excellent descriptive/same-period correlation. Baseball Prospectus's 2018 comparison found xwOBA's next-year predictive correlation (r=.35) essentially tied with FIP (r=.36) and DRA (r=.36), concluding there is "little evidence that metrics like xBA or xwOBA provide a uniquely better measurement of probable...skill" ([Baseball Prospectus](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/news/article/40026/prospectus-feature-siren-song-statcasts-expected-metrics/)). FanGraphs' own in-season study found first-half xwOBA predicting second-half actual wOBA at only r=.406 for hitters — called "feeble" — even though xwOBA did modestly outpredict raw wOBA, implying batted-ball *profile* is more stable than batted-ball *outcomes* but still not a strong forward signal on its own ([FanGraphs](https://fantasy.fangraphs.com/the-in-season-predictiveness-of-xwoba/)). This matters directly for the baseline pipeline's design: if the plan is to bolt xwOBA/xERA onto the existing wOBA/xFIP targets expecting a dramatic accuracy jump, the controlled evidence says don't expect one from the expected-stat substitution alone — the real lift found across this research comes from combining Statcast contact-quality features (barrel rate, exit velocity) as *additional inputs* to a regression/ML model, not from swapping the target or baseline formula to an expected-stat equivalent. A FanGraphs community neural-network study illustrates the right-shaped gain: classifying whether a batter-season's wOBA exceeds .320 improved from 69.19% accuracy with plate-discipline features alone to 81.31% once exit velocity and barrel rate were added — a genuine 12-point gain, but one that required adding features to an existing model, not changing architectures ([FanGraphs Community](https://community.fangraphs.com/predicting-woba-using-process-based-statistics/)).

## Conclusion

The literature converges on an uncomfortable but useful conclusion for anyone extending a Marcel-plus-closed-form-EB-shrinkage pipeline: the *architecture* of the model (closed-form shrinkage vs. hierarchical Bayes vs. tree ensemble vs. neural sequence model) matters far less than the *granularity of the input data* (box-score aggregates vs. Statcast contact-quality features vs. raw pitch-by-pitch event sequences), and every documented gain — Statcast-plus-ML's 12.1% MAE reduction, the hierarchical model's edge concentrated in young players, Stuff+'s predictive power once team/park context is controlled for — is bounded by an intrinsic predictability ceiling (R²≈0.2–0.3 for pitchers, ≈0.6 for aggregate hitter value) that two decades of methodological elaboration, including systems costing far more engineering effort than the baseline, has not meaningfully broken. The practical sequencing this implies is a two-speed roadmap: swap the regression/shrinkage step for gradient boosting on the same rolling box-score features now (small, low-risk, backed by the strongest controlled evidence in this research), add Statcast contact-quality and pitch-characteristic features as additional inputs next (small-to-moderate, data is free, engineering cost is feature pipelines not acquisition), and treat pitch-sequence transformers, Stuff+-family pitch-quality models, and full hierarchical Bayesian mixture/HMM structures as a genuinely separate, large-architecture research track — one whose strongest results all depend on committing to pitch-by-pitch event-level infrastructure the baseline doesn't currently have, and whose payoff, even when real, has nowhere in this literature been shown to be transformative rather than incremental.
