# MLB Betting Market Efficiency — Academic Literature Review

## What empirical studies test weak-form/semi-strong-form efficiency in MLB moneyline, run line, and totals markets?

### Takeaway
There is a substantial, decades-spanning academic literature (Journal of Finance, Applied Economics, Applied Financial Economics, Journal of Sports Economics, Journal of Prediction Markets, International Journal of Sport Finance) testing MLB betting-market efficiency across moneyline, run line, totals, and season win-total markets. The consensus across the most rigorous and recent studies is that the MLB moneyline and totals markets are "efficient" in the economically meaningful sense (deviations exist but are too small or too inconsistent to beat the vig after transaction costs), while narrower sub-markets (season win totals, specific heuristic-driven strategies, early-season situations) show more persistent, exploitable inefficiency.

### Cited Findings
- Woodland & Woodland (1994), "Market Efficiency and the Favorite-Longshot Bias: The Baseball Betting Market," *The Journal of Finance* (Mar. 1994) — performed weak-form tests of market efficiency within and across MLB odds lines and found the classic racetrack favorite-longshot bias exists in **reverse** for baseball (bettors undervalue favorites / overvalue longshots relative to true win probability), but concluded deviations from efficiency are too small to generate profitable strategies once bookmaker commission (vig) is included — [Wiley/Journal of Finance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1994.tb04429.x); abstract via [DeepDyve](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/wiley/market-efficiency-and-the-favorite-longshot-bias-the-baseball-betting-pJSckk99cx).
- Woodland & Woodland published a follow-up, "The reverse favourite-longshot bias and market efficiency in major league baseball," *Bulletin of Economic Research* (2003), reaffirming the reverse-bias finding with updated data — cited in [reverse-FLB literature review search](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255726080_The_Implications_of_a_Reverse_Favourite-Longshot_Bias_in_a_Prediction_Market).
- Gandar, Zuber, Johnson & Dare (2002), re-examining MLB moneyline data, **failed to replicate** the reverse favorite-longshot bias reported by Woodland & Woodland (1994), concluding MLB betting may be "unremarkable" — bettors favor longshots similarly to patterns seen in other sports, not in reverse — summarized in [literature review, Risks journal (Vogel et al. 2021)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jrisks/v9y2021i1p22-d478780.html). This is a direct contradiction between the two strands of research and should be treated as unresolved/contested rather than settled.
- Lahvička (2014) (or related re-examination; exact citation via Tandfonline), "Re-examining the betting market on Major League Baseball games: is there a reverse favourite-longshot bias?", *Applied Economics*, Vol. 34, No. 10 — a direct re-test of the Woodland & Woodland claim — [Tandfonline abstract page](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036840110095427) (full abstract text could not be retrieved — site returned 403; see Gaps).
- Paul & Weinbach, "Re-examining the efficiency of the Major League Baseball over-under betting market," *Applied Financial Economics*, Vol. 24, No. 18 (2014) — using full price data (including vig-adjusted odds, not just whether the line was crossed), this study corrected a key methodological flaw in Brown & Abraham (2002), who had assumed over and under wagers offered equal returns; Paul & Weinbach found **little evidence that the MLB over-under (totals) market is inefficient** once the correct pricing data is used — [Tandfonline abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09603107.2014.925052); summary of the Brown & Abraham correction via [search results on Paul & Weinbach totals methodology](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263511852_Re-examining_the_efficiency_of_the_Major_League_Baseball_over-under_betting_market).
- Paul & Weinbach, "Baseball," *Journal of Sports Economics* (2013; DOI 10.1177/1527002511417630) — examined MLB moneyline market line movements and found **offshore sportsbooks highly efficient**: no profitable wagering strategy exists based on general line movement direction. However, betting *against* first-movements-toward-favorites was found profitable at a smaller Las Vegas sportsbook, implying some efficiency variation by book size/liquidity — [SAGE/Tandfonline links](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1527002511417630).
- Ryan, Gramm & McKinney (2012) found that MLB moneyline underdog bets placed **early in the season**, when public information on team quality is limited, generated significant positive returns — a seasonal/informational inefficiency — cited in [search summary of MLB moneyline literature](https://www.sportsinsights.com/academic-research-sports-betting-mlb-the-nhl-the-racetrack/).
- Ryan & Celestin (2018), "Game over? Assessing the impact of the emergence of a profitable wagering strategy in major league baseball wagering markets," *Applied Economics*, Vol. 50, No. 37, pp. 3979–3991 — studied what happened once the Ryan-Gramm-McKinney early-season-underdog strategy was published. Overall market efficiency (by standard tests) was not disturbed, but the specific profitable opportunity **dissipated after publication**; up to half of the drop in strategy returns was attributable to bettor-behavior changes (i.e., the market self-corrected once the inefficiency became public) — [Tandfonline abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036846.2018.1430349).
- A 2024/2025 *Applied Economics* study (Vol. 57, No. 34), "Do profitable wagering strategies indicate an inefficient market? An investigation into simple wagering strategies in Major League Baseball moneyline markets," tested **1,547 simple wagering strategies** on MLB moneylines from **1999–2016**. Results: 38 strategies (2.46%) profitable at 5% significance; 7 strategies (0.45%) profitable at 1% significance — both rates are consistent with what pure chance would produce under multiple-testing, and family-wise error correction confirmed the market's overall efficiency was not disturbed — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115).
- Bouchard (2019), Harvard College undergraduate thesis, "Information and Market Efficiency: Evidence From the Major League Baseball Betting Market," used moneyline data from **over 88,000 MLB games spanning 1977–2018** to track the evolution of market efficiency over four decades. Finding: forecast accuracy (i.e., convergence between implied probabilities and outcomes) improved over time, with the biggest jump occurring from the **late 1990s to early 2000s** — attributed to improvements in information quality, quantity, and accessibility (early internet/data era) — [Harvard DASH repository](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/24950429-b1b7-4372-a029-1b68de1872e3/download) (full text could not be fetched directly — server returned 429 rate-limit; findings drawn from search-engine summary of the document, not full read).
- Liu (2025), Harvard undergraduate thesis, "Swing and a Miss: Uncovering Behavioral Biases in MLB Betting Markets" (advisors: Prof. Michael Brenner, Prof. Christopher Avery) — examined favorite-longshot bias and momentum effects using **moneyline and run-line odds**. Finding: the favorite-longshot bias is **clearly present in opening odds but disappears by closing odds**, with stronger effects in home games — indicating the market corrects most of the mispricing between line-open and line-close. Momentum/streak information affects which teams become favorites/underdogs but is efficiently priced once that categorization occurs. Similar patterns were found to emerge in the run-line market specifically — [Harvard DASH repository](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/81e73570-084b-4e39-84d3-ccb4649c61ab/download) (full text not retrievable directly — 429 rate limit; findings from search-engine summary only, see Gaps).
- Woodland & Woodland, "Additional evidence of heuristic-based inefficiency in season wins total betting markets: Major League Baseball," *Journal of Economics and Finance* (Springer, 2015/2016, DOI 10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x) — found the MLB **season win-totals market (not single-game markets) is inefficient** and offers profitable wagering opportunities. Bettors overreact to a team's prior-season performance (representativeness heuristic — expecting small-sample results to generalize), particularly for teams with winning records the prior year. This echoes the same authors' earlier findings of significant inefficiency in NFL and NBA season win-totals markets (Woodland & Woodland 2013, 2015 per the same research program) — [Springer link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x) (full text behind an identity-provider redirect wall; findings drawn from search summaries, not full text).
- Simon (2025), "Autocorrelation and Weekend Effects: Inefficiencies in Moneyline Movement for Three Major Sports," *International Journal of Sport Finance* — examined pre-game moneyline movement (open to close) at three sportsbooks for **NFL, NBA, and NHL, 2019–2023**, extending prior MLB-specific findings to other sports. Found significant **negative autocorrelation** in line changes across all three sports (a violation of weak-form efficiency — if a line moved one direction, the next move tends to reverse), suggesting sportsbook overreaction is broad across sports, not unique to baseball. A "weekend effect" similar to one previously documented in MLB was also found in the NBA: forecast quality in the hours before game time improves less on weekends than on weekdays. The paper frames itself explicitly as extending "inefficiencies recently revealed for MLB" to other leagues, implying a companion/prior MLB-specific autocorrelation and weekend-effect finding exists in the literature — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports); [Figshare](https://figshare.com/ndownloader/files/59330717).

### Inferences
- The literature shows a methodological arc: early studies (Woodland & Woodland 1994, Brown & Abraham 2002) found inefficiencies, but later re-examinations using better data (full vig-adjusted pricing rather than binary win/loss of the bet) and larger/more recent samples (Paul & Weinbach 2014; Gandar et al. 2002; the 2024 1,547-strategy study) tend to overturn or substantially shrink those findings — a pattern consistent with markets becoming more efficient over time and/or earlier studies suffering from data/methodology limitations.
- Where real inefficiency is most reliably documented, it clusters in markets with less liquidity or less attention: season win-totals (a market traded far less frequently and with less continuous price discovery than single-game lines) and early-season single games (when public information about team quality is sparse). Deep, frequently-traded single-game lines (moneyline, totals) at major books appear close to efficient by closing time.
- The Woodland & Woodland vs. Gandar et al. dispute over the "reverse favorite-longshot bias" in MLB, still unresolved in the literature as surveyed, suggests any favorite-longshot-type bias in baseball specifically is fragile and sample/period-dependent rather than a robust structural feature.

### Gaps
- Full text of several key papers (Harvard theses, the Woodland & Woodland 2015/2016 season win-totals paper, the Applied Economics run-line/reverse-FLB re-examination) could not be directly fetched (429 rate-limits, paywalls, identity-provider redirects); findings above are drawn from search-engine-generated summaries of these papers rather than verified primary-text extraction. A follow-up pass with direct journal/library access (e.g., via a university library proxy) would be needed to confirm exact effect sizes, p-values, and sample periods precisely.
- No study specifically isolating the **run line** as its own efficiency test (independent of moneyline/totals) was found with a full abstract — the Liu (2025) Harvard thesis appears to be the main source touching run-line pricing specifically, and only secondhand summary was available.
- Could not confirm the exact citation/author/year for the standalone MLB-specific "weekend effect" and "autocorrelation" finding that the Simon (2025) paper explicitly says it is extending — this appears to be a distinct, not-yet-identified prior MLB paper.

## Do documented inefficiencies exist (favorite-longshot bias, home bias, public betting bias, day-of-week/rest effects, pitcher information lag, weather effects on totals)? With effect sizes.

### Takeaway
Several specific, named inefficiencies are documented in the literature — a (disputed) reverse favorite-longshot bias, a home-game interaction effect, early-season underdog value, weekend/day-of-week forecast degradation, and a behavioral bias toward betting the Over in totals markets — but most are either small, inconsistently replicated, or insufficient to beat the bookmaker's vig; the clearer, better-replicated effects are time-of-season (early season) and heuristic/anchoring-based (season win totals, overreaction to prior-season record), not simple favorite/longshot or pure home-field bias.

### Cited Findings
- **Favorite-longshot bias (reverse direction):** Documented by Woodland & Woodland (1994, 2003) in MLB moneylines but not replicated by Gandar et al. (2002); more recent work (Liu 2025 Harvard thesis) finds it present in **opening** odds only, disappearing by **closing** odds, and stronger in **home games** — [Journal of Finance abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1994.tb04429.x); [Harvard thesis summary](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/81e73570-084b-4e39-84d3-ccb4649c61ab/download); [literature review noting the Gandar et al. non-replication](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jrisks/v9y2021i1p22-d478780.html).
- **Home-game interaction:** The favorite-longshot-type mispricing identified by Liu (2025) was found to be stronger specifically in home games, suggesting a home/road interaction with other biases rather than a standalone home-team bias with its own documented effect size — [Harvard thesis summary](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/81e73570-084b-4e39-84d3-ccb4649c61ab/download).
- **Early-season / information-availability effect:** Ryan, Gramm & McKinney (2012) found MLB underdog moneyline bets placed early in the season (when there is limited public information on current team strength) produced statistically significant positive returns — [search summary citing Ryan Gramm McKinney 2012](https://www.sportsinsights.com/academic-research-sports-betting-mlb-the-nhl-the-racetrack/). This specific exploitable edge was later shown by Ryan & Celestin (2018) to have **dissipated after the strategy was publicized**, with up to 50% of the return decline attributable to bettor behavior adjusting to the known strategy — [Applied Economics 2018 abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036846.2018.1430349).
- **Totals / Over bias:** Paul & Weinbach research (cited in search summaries of their totals-market methodology papers) found bettors exhibit a **behavioral bias toward wagering the Over** in MLB totals, and that using correct full-price (vig-inclusive) data, simple betting strategies could be constructed that reject the efficient-markets null for some sub-periods, though their later 2014 re-examination found the broader totals market largely efficient once pricing data issues were corrected — [search summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263511852_Re-examining_the_efficiency_of_the_Major_League_Baseball_over-under_betting_market).
- **Weather/wind effects on totals:** Industry/practitioner analysis (not peer-reviewed) states wind is the dominant weather factor for MLB totals (e.g., wind blowing in at Wrigley Field can reduce the total by roughly a full run) and that sportsbooks adjust posted totals for wind direction/speed, but allegedly sometimes **under-adjust** relative to the true effect on scoring — this claim comes from betting-industry commentary (ProComputerGambler, OddsIndex), not an academic/peer-reviewed source, and should be treated as a practitioner claim rather than a verified academic finding — [ProComputerGambler](https://www.procomputergambler.com/mlb-totals-trends-over-under-market-value/); [OddsIndex](https://oddsindex.com/guides/weather-impact-totals). By contrast, peer-reviewed work on weather effects in totals markets that was identified in search results is specific to the **NFL**, not MLB — "Weather Biases in the NFL Totals Market" ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24071150_Weather_Biases_in_the_NFL_Totals_Market)) — and should not be treated as MLB evidence.
- **Starting-pitcher information and line movement:** Practitioner/industry sources (not peer-reviewed) report that confirmation of a previously-reported starting pitcher can move a line by roughly 5–10 cents, while a scratch/downgrade can move it 20–30 cents or cause the book to pull the market until a replacement is named, and that bookmaker pricing engines may lag on in-season strikeout-rate (K%) trend shifts for individual pitchers — [thelines.com-style industry summary via search](https://baseballbetsystem.com/starting-pitcher-mlb-betting/). No peer-reviewed academic paper specifically quantifying a "starting pitcher information lag" effect with a rigorous statistical test was located in this research pass; this is a gap (see below), and the only related academic-adjacent finding is the general "line movement accuracy" result that larger relative line movements tend to be less accurate on average (suggesting overreaction to new information broadly, not pitcher-news specifically) — cited from the "weak form efficiency" / "inefficient forecasts at the sportsbook" search summaries.
- **Season win-totals heuristic bias:** Woodland & Woodland (Journal of Economics and Finance, 2015/2016) documented that MLB season win-total bettors overreact to the prior season's win-loss record (representativeness heuristic), especially for teams coming off winning seasons, producing a profitable wagering opportunity — the clearest, most specifically quantified non-single-game inefficiency in the set of findings gathered — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x) (exact effect size/ROI not retrievable from search summary alone; see Gaps).
- **Weekend/day-of-week effect:** A "weekend effect" has been documented for MLB (referenced as prior literature by Simon 2025) and replicated in the NBA: market forecast accuracy in the hours leading up to a game improves significantly less on weekends than weekdays — implying day-of-week variation in how efficiently new information gets incorporated into lines — [ResearchGate, Simon 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports).

### Inferences
- The best-evidenced, most-replicated MLB-specific inefficiencies are not simple favorite/longshot or home/road biases (which are contested in the academic literature) but rather (a) heuristic-driven overreaction in low-liquidity derivative markets like season win totals, and (b) time-dependent effects (early season, weekends) tied to information availability/processing speed rather than to a fixed structural mispricing of any one bet type.
- Weather and starting-pitcher-lag effects are widely discussed in the betting industry but are not well supported by peer-reviewed MLB-specific academic literature in what was found during this search; claims about these effects currently rest on practitioner/industry sources rather than rigorous academic tests, and should be flagged as unverified if used in a model-building context.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, MLB-specific academic study quantifying a starting-pitcher-news information lag (e.g., via event-study methodology around pitcher announcement/scratch times) was located; what exists is informal industry commentary.
- No peer-reviewed MLB-specific academic study on weather/wind effects on the totals market was located (the one rigorous weather-totals paper found is NFL-specific); this should not be treated as evidence for baseball.
- Precise effect sizes (ROI%, breakeven win-rate deltas, statistical significance thresholds) for the season win-totals heuristic bias and the Over-bias in traditional totals could not be extracted because full text of the underlying papers was not accessible (paywalled/redirect-blocked); only abstract-level/search-summary characterizations were obtainable.
- "Public betting bias" (i.e., square money moving lines in exploitable ways) specifically for MLB was not directly documented in any academic (vs. industry) source found in this pass — this is a notable gap given how often it is discussed in sports-betting media.

## How does MLB market efficiency compare to other major sports (NFL, NBA, NHL) per the literature?

### Takeaway
The literature broadly finds football (NFL and college) and MLB to show more documented statistically-significant inefficiencies than the NBA or NHL, though the most recent cross-sport work (Simon 2025) finds a shared pattern of negative autocorrelation / overreaction in line movement across NFL, NBA, and NHL as well, suggesting the sport-to-sport efficiency gap may be narrowing or was partly an artifact of which sports had been studied longest/first.

### Cited Findings
- A cross-sport synthesis in the search results states: "statistically significant inefficiencies in professional and collegiate football, college basketball, and MLB," but researchers "were unable to demonstrate statistically significant inefficiencies in the NBA or NHL" — [search summary citing comparative sports-betting-efficiency literature](https://myweb.ecu.edu/robbinst/PDFs/Weak%20Form%20Efficiency%20in%20Sports%20Betting%20Markets.pdf) (full-text extraction of this PDF failed — binary/garbled content; this is a search-engine paraphrase of the source, not verified primary text).
- Woodland & Woodland extended their heuristic-based season win-totals inefficiency finding across three sports: NFL and NBA season win-totals markets (2013, 2015 per that research program) were found to have "significant inefficiencies," and the MLB season win-totals market (2015/2016 paper) showed a consistent pattern — implying this particular type of inefficiency (representativeness-heuristic overreaction to prior-season record) generalizes across the NFL, NBA, and MLB, not just baseball — [Springer, Woodland & Woodland MLB paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x).
- Simon (2025) found significant **negative autocorrelation** in moneyline-movement for **NFL, NBA, and NHL** (2019–2023 data, three sportsbooks), explicitly describing this as extending findings "recently revealed for MLB" to the other three major sports — implying this specific type of weak-form inefficiency (overreaction/reversal in line movement) is not unique to baseball but common across all four major North American leagues — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports).
- The same Simon (2025) paper notes that despite the autocorrelation finding, "little evidence is found to suggest unreliability of individual prices; moneylines for NFL, NBA, and NHL games appear to be mostly accurate" — i.e., the markets are efficient in level/accuracy terms even while showing a weak-form inefficiency in how prices move — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports).

### Inferences
- The traditional view in the literature (older studies) that MLB and NFL are "less efficient" than NBA/NHL may partly reflect which sports were studied earliest and most intensively (NFL and MLB have the longest betting-market academic literature), rather than a fundamental structural difference. The most recent comparative work (Simon 2025) suggests overreaction-type inefficiencies are common to all four major sports.
- MLB's long season (162 games) and large annual sample size are often cited informally as reasons to expect more efficient pricing (more data points for calibration), but no study in this search explicitly tested "season length" as a causal driver of relative efficiency across sports — this specific causal claim from the task's key questions is not directly supported by any paper found and should be treated as a plausible but unverified hypothesis.

### Gaps
- No paper directly and quantitatively compares MLB efficiency to NFL/NBA/NHL efficiency using a single consistent methodology across all four sports in the same study (closest is Simon 2025, which covers NFL/NBA/NHL together but treats MLB only by reference to "prior" unspecified work).
- The specific mechanism claim that baseball's long season / large sample size relative to other sports *causes* relatively higher efficiency (vs. NFL's short season) was not directly tested or confirmed by any source found; this remains a plausible inference, not a cited finding.

## What data sources and methodologies have researchers used to test MLB market efficiency?

### Takeaway
Researchers predominantly use historical closing/opening moneyline, run-line, and totals odds (often from offshore or Las Vegas sportsbooks, sometimes multiple books for cross-book comparison) spanning multi-decade samples, and test efficiency via (a) regression/calibration of implied win probabilities against actual outcomes, (b) backtesting large numbers of simple rule-based wagering strategies with significance/multiple-testing correction, and (c) analysis of autocorrelation and line-movement dynamics from opening to closing lines.

### Cited Findings
- Bouchard (2019) used moneyline data from **over 88,000 MLB games, 1977–2018**, to regress/track implied-probability calibration against actual win outcomes over time — a classic semi-strong-form calibration test applied longitudinally — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/24950429-b1b7-4372-a029-1b68de1872e3/download).
- The 2024/2025 Applied Economics paper used a **brute-force strategy-backtesting approach**: 1,547 distinct simple wagering rules tested against MLB moneylines from **1999–2016**, with significance assessed at both 5% and 1% thresholds and corrected for family-wise error across the large number of simultaneous tests — a methodology explicitly designed to guard against false-positive "edges" that are really just multiple-comparisons artifacts — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115).
- Paul & Weinbach's totals-market work emphasized the importance of using **full vig-adjusted price data** (not just binary over/under outcome) — they showed that Brown & Abraham (2002) had reached incorrect efficiency conclusions by assuming equal returns on all over/under wagers, a methodological critique that reshaped how later totals-market studies handle pricing data — [search summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263511852_Re-examining_the_efficiency_of_the_Major_League_Baseball_over-under_betting_market).
- Simon (2025) used **detailed intraday moneyline movement data from three sportsbooks, 2019–2023**, tracking prices from market open to close, to test for autocorrelation in line changes and time-of-day/day-of-week variation in forecast accuracy — a market-microstructure approach distinct from the single-closing-line methodologies used in older MLB papers — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398469716_Autocorrelation_and_Weekend_Effects_Inefficiencies_in_Moneyline_Movement_for_Three_Major_Sports).
- Ryan & Celestin (2018) used an event-study-style design, comparing wagering-strategy returns **before and after** a specific strategy (early-season MLB underdog betting) was publicly disclosed, to test whether publication of an inefficiency causes the market to arbitrage it away — a methodology aimed directly at testing market adaptability/self-correction rather than a one-time efficiency snapshot — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036846.2018.1430349).

### Inferences
- There has been a clear methodological progression in this literature: from single-period regression/calibration tests (1990s–2000s) toward large-scale multiple-strategy backtests with statistical correction (2020s) and market-microstructure/intraday line-movement analysis (2020s) — reflecting both better data availability (more sportsbooks, higher-frequency odds snapshots) and more statistically rigorous standards (guarding against data mining / multiple comparisons).
- The field has moved toward treating "is there a profitable strategy" as a multiple-hypothesis-testing problem rather than a single bet-on-this-signal test, which raises the bar for claiming a true inefficiency (e.g., the 2024/2025 paper's 2.46%/0.45% profitable-strategy rates being judged consistent with pure chance).

### Gaps
- The exact data vendors/sources (e.g., Vegas Insider, Sports Insights, Pinnacle historical odds, Kaggle datasets) used by each paper could not be confirmed from abstract-level search summaries alone; full-text access would be needed to document precise data provenance.

## Are there specific papers quantifying ROI or edge size from identified inefficiencies, and over what time periods/sample sizes?

### Takeaway
A few papers give concrete, quantified figures — most notably the 2024/2025 Applied Economics paper's precise strategy-success rates (2.46% and 0.45% of 1,547 strategies profitable at 5%/1% significance, 1999–2016) and the Ryan & Celestin (2018) finding that up to ~50% of the decline in a previously-profitable strategy's returns was due to bettor-behavior adjustment — but most other papers in this review were only accessible at the abstract/summary level, so specific ROI percentages for the favorite-longshot, season win-totals, and totals/Over biases could not be confirmed.

### Cited Findings
- 2024/2025 *Applied Economics* (Vol. 57, No. 34): of **1,547 simple MLB moneyline wagering strategies tested over 1999–2016**, **38 (2.46%)** were profitable at the 5% significance level and **7 (0.45%)** at the 1% level — rates consistent with chance, supporting overall market efficiency rather than genuine edge — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115).
- Ryan & Celestin (2018), *Applied Economics* Vol. 50, No. 37, pp. 3979–3991: after the early-season-underdog MLB strategy (originally identified by Ryan, Gramm & McKinney 2012) was publicized, its previously-positive returns dissipated, with bettor-behavior changes (not just bookmaker re-pricing) accounting for **up to half of the return decline** — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036846.2018.1430349).

### Inferences
- The scarcity of precisely quantified, independently-replicated ROI figures for MLB-specific betting inefficiencies (beyond the two studies above) is itself informative: it suggests that where rigorous, large-sample, statistically-corrected testing has been applied, documented MLB inefficiencies tend to be small, fragile, and/or short-lived (dissipating once publicized), consistent with a market that is close to (though not perfectly) efficient.

### Gaps
- Exact ROI/edge figures for: the reverse favorite-longshot bias (Woodland & Woodland 1994/2003), the season win-totals heuristic bias (Woodland & Woodland 2015/2016), and the totals/Over behavioral bias (Paul & Weinbach) could not be extracted — these papers were only accessible via search-engine abstract summaries or were paywalled/blocked (HTTP 403, 429, or identity-provider redirects) during this research session. A follow-up with institutional journal access (university library, JSTOR, Taylor & Francis subscription) would be needed to pull precise effect sizes, confidence intervals, and sample sizes for these studies.
- No paper was found that reports a single consolidated "edge size" comparison across moneyline vs. run line vs. totals markets within MLB — the literature tends to study one market type per paper rather than comparing edge magnitude across bet types directly.
