# Closing Line Value (CLV): Formal Definitions and Measurement Methodologies

## What is the formal definition of CLV, and does an academic definition actually exist?

### Takeaway
"Closing line value" as a named term is an **industry/practitioner concept**, not a term with a standalone formal definition in peer-reviewed academic literature. Practitioners define it as the difference between the price/probability a bettor obtained at bet placement and the market's final ("closing") price/probability just before the event starts; the academic literature does not use the phrase "CLV" but supports the underlying premise through research on sports-betting **market efficiency**, which treats the closing price as the market's best, most information-complete estimate of the true outcome probability. No academic paper defining "CLV" per se was found in this research pass — this is a mapping of industry terminology onto academic efficient-markets concepts, and that gap should be treated as a genuine limitation, not an oversight.

### Cited Findings
- Industry definition: "CLV compares the odds you got on a bet to the odds the sportsbook offered right before the game started (the closing line)... If you bet a team at -3 (-110) on Tuesday and the line closed at -5 (-110) on Sunday morning, the market moved 2 full points in your favor." Positive CLV = bettor got a better number than the close; negative CLV = bettor got a worse number. — [Pikkit](https://pikkit.com/blog/what-is-closing-line-value)
- "Closing Line Value is the sports bettor's version of beating the market... measuring how the odds you bet on compare to the final odds (the 'closing line') just before an event starts." Pinnacle frames consistent positive CLV as "one of the most important indicators of a bettor's skill and long-term potential profitability." — [Pinnacle](https://www.pinnacle.com/betting-resources/en/educational/what-is-closing-line-value-clv-in-sports-betting)
- "Pinnacle argues that the closing line should be the most efficient point of the market and therefore a useful proxy for underlying probability," though Pinnacle's own materials caveat that this logic applies most strongly to "high-limit, high-volume events rather than every small derivative market on the board." — [Joe Saumarez Smith](https://joesaumarez.co.uk/sports-betting-market-efficiency-and-the-closing-line)
- Academic market-efficiency research (not using the term CLV) finds "closing odds are more correct than opening odds" as the market absorbs new information between line posting and kickoff; one study found initial postings carry an average margin of 2.81% at lower volume (~€1,000) while closing odds reflect ~€10,000 volume and a tighter 2.03% margin, consistent with the closing price being a more information-complete estimate. — [MPRA working paper, "Chance or Ability? The Efficiency of the Football Betting Market"](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/87230/1/MPRA_paper_87230.pdf)
- General weak-form efficiency literature: markets are "generally efficient" in the sense that "no odds-based betting strategy will yield statistically significant long-term profits," though technical inefficiencies (e.g., favorite-longshot bias, home-favorite bias) persist and vary by sport. — [search synthesis citing Kuypers 2000 and Gross & Rebeggiani 2018 literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2773161824000193)
- Levitt (2004), "Why Are Gambling Markets Organised So Differently From Financial Markets?", *The Economic Journal* 114(495):223–246, is the foundational peer-reviewed paper most relevant to closing-line logic: it argues bookmakers set prices to maximize profit by exploiting known bettor biases rather than to "clear the market" (i.e., rather than balancing action), which complicates a naive equivalence between closing price and true probability. — [Levitt 2004, University of Chicago](https://pricetheory.uchicago.edu/levitt/Papers/LevittWhyAreGamblingMarkets2004.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because Levitt's model shows bookmakers may deliberately set prices away from the "balanced-book"/consensus price to exploit bettor bias, the "closing line = true probability" assumption that underlies industry CLV reasoning is a simplification that holds best at low-margin, high-volume, high-limit markets where bookmakers compete hard on accuracy (this is explicitly how Pinnacle's own practitioner caveat is framed), rather than being a market-universal law validated in a specific peer-reviewed paper.
- The industry CLV framework should be understood as applying the broader academic finding of "closing prices are more informative than opening prices" (supported by efficiency studies) to individual bettor performance tracking — but the specific claim "my positive CLV forecasts my long-run profitability" has not itself been tested as a named hypothesis in the papers surfaced here.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper was found that explicitly defines, names, or empirically validates "closing line value" as a bettor-skill metric. This is a plausible genuine gap in the literature (the concept may exist primarily in grey literature/industry analytics write-ups, e.g. Unabated, Pinnacle, sports-betting blogs) rather than a search failure — flagging this for the report writer to state explicitly.
- Could not verify whether any sports-economics journal (Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports, Journal of Sports Economics, Journal of Gambling Studies, International Journal of Forecasting) has published a paper using "closing line value" terminology directly; this pass's searches surfaced only market-efficiency papers that support the underlying logic, not papers naming the metric.

## What are the different measurement conventions (devigged vs. raw closing price; percentage vs. cents/basis points; single sharp book vs. market consensus)?

### Takeaway
Industry practice measures CLV in several non-equivalent ways: (1) raw/"vigged" closing price vs. no-vig (devigged/fair) closing price, (2) as a percentage of implied probability vs. as "cents"/price units in moneyline terms, and (3) against a single sharp reference book (most commonly Pinnacle) vs. an average/consensus closing line across multiple books — these choices are practitioner conventions, not standardized in any academic source found.

### Cited Findings
- Basic percentage CLV formula cited in practitioner sources: **CLV% = [(X − Y) / Y] × 100**, where X = implied probability at close and Y = implied probability of the bettor's own wager price. For decimal odds, an equivalent shortcut is **CLV = (bettor's decimal odds ÷ closing decimal odds − 1) × 100**. — [search synthesis of CLV calculator guides](https://therundown.io/blog/closing-line-value-api), [thelines.com](https://www.thelines.com/betting/guides/closing-line-value/)
- "No-vig CLV" is distinguished from raw CLV: "No-vig CLV compares your price with the close after its margin is removed: the decimal odds you bet divided by the fair closing decimal odds, minus one." This is presented as the more rigorous of the two conventions because it strips out the bookmaker's hold before comparing. — [gamblingcalc.com CLV calculator](https://gamblingcalc.com/betting/closing-line-value-calculator/)
- In moneyline-heavy sports (e.g., baseball), bettors track CLV in "dollars and cents" terms rather than percentage: "a bet on a team at -120 that closes at -130 generated '10 cents' of CLV." — [search synthesis](https://www.lineups.com/betting/glossary/closing-line-value/)
- American-to-decimal conversion used before CLV math: decimal = 1 + (American/100) for positive American odds; decimal = 1 + (100/|American|) for negative American odds. — [search synthesis of CLV calculator guides](https://bettinglocation.com/400-how-to-calculate-closing-line-value-clv-in-sports-betting)
- Pinnacle is repeatedly cited across practitioner sources as the standard single-book reference for closing-line benchmarking "because its lines are... used by professional bettors worldwide as the benchmark" and "the closing line is the most efficient price" at sharp books specifically (as opposed to retail/recreational books). — [search synthesis](https://www.pinnacle.com/betting-resources/en/educational/what-is-closing-line-value-clv-in-sports-betting); caveated by [Joe Saumarez Smith](https://joesaumarez.co.uk/sports-betting-market-efficiency-and-the-closing-line) that this applies best to high-limit/high-volume markets.
- Alternative convention: comparing against a market-consensus/average closing line (aggregating many books) rather than one sharp book — described by the Joe Saumarez Smith piece as "the comparison between a bettor's entry price and the market's final consensus price at start time," implying consensus-based measurement is also used as a methodology, distinct from single-book (Pinnacle) benchmarking.

### Inferences
- The percentage-vs-cents distinction is largely a sport/market convention (percentage more common for point-spread/totals markets priced near -110; cents more common for moneyline-dominant sports like baseball where price granularity in American odds is more intuitive to bettors) rather than a methodological disagreement about what CLV "is."
- Raw vs. no-vig CLV will diverge most when vig itself changes between bet placement and closing (e.g., limits/markets tightening margin as volume increases, as suggested by the MPRA finding of margin shrinking from opening to close) — meaning no-vig CLV is the more defensible metric when margins are not constant across the line's life, but raw CLV is simpler and more commonly used in casual tracking tools.

### Gaps
- No source quantified how much raw CLV and no-vig CLV typically diverge in practice (i.e., no empirical study decomposing CLV into "vig-change" vs. "true probability-change" components was found).
- Could not find a rigorous practitioner or academic comparison of "single sharp book" vs. "consensus/average closing line" methodologies measuring which better predicts long-run bettor profitability; this is asserted directionally by industry sources (favoring Pinnacle) but not tested against consensus-line alternatives in any source retrieved.

## How is vig/overround removed to compute a "true" implied probability, and which devigging methods are preferred academically?

### Takeaway
Multiple overround-removal ("devigging") methods exist — basic/proportional (multiplicative), additive, power, logarithmic, and Shin's method — and the academic literature (notably Clarke et al. 2017 and Štrumbelj 2014) finds that simple proportional normalization is suboptimal, while the **power method** and **Shin's method** perform better, particularly for markets exhibiting favorite-longshot bias; there is no single universally "preferred" method, and some recent practitioner-level critique argues Shin's method is becoming outdated relative to power/other newer approaches.

### Cited Findings
- **Multiplicative/proportional (basic normalization) method**: formula is `true probability_i = implied probability_i / Σ(implied probabilities)`, i.e., divide each outcome's inverse-odds by the sum of all inverse-odds (the "overround"/booksum) so probabilities sum to 1. Described as the simplest, most "standard practice" approach, but assumes the margin is spread equally/proportionally across outcomes regardless of their likelihood. — [search synthesis, sharkbetting.com devig explainer](https://www.sharkbetting.com/blog/devig-explained)
- **Power method** (Clarke 2016 / Clarke et al. 2017, *Adjusting Bookmaker's Odds to Allow for Overround*, published in American Journal of Sports Science, Science Publishing Group): finds an exponent *k* solving `(1/odds_A)^k + (1/odds_B)^k + ... = 1`, then sets `true probability = (1/odds)^k`. This method "never produces... probabilities outside the 0–1 range" and explicitly "allows for the favourite-longshot bias" by giving outsiders a greater probability adjustment than favorites. Per the cited performance comparison, "the power method universally outperforms the multiplicative method and outperforms or is comparable to the Shin method." — [ResearchGate: Clarke, Adjusting Bookmaker's Odds to Allow for Overround](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326510904_Adjusting_Bookmaker's_Odds_to_Allow_for_Overround), [sciencepublishinggroup.com](https://www.sciencepublishinggroup.com/article/10.11648/j.ajss.20170506.12)
- **Logarithmic method**: in some sources treated as effectively the same technique as the power method (the "Wisdom of the Crowds" document reportedly uses "logarithmic method" to describe the same `p_i = r_i^(1/k)` transform where *k* is chosen so probabilities sum to 1) — i.e., terminology is not fully standardized across sources, and "power" and "logarithmic" are sometimes used interchangeably for this family of methods. — [football-data.co.uk, Wisdom of the Crowd paper](https://football-data.co.uk/The_Wisdom_of_the_Crowd_updated.pdf) as referenced in search synthesis
- **Shin's method**: originates from Hyun Song Shin's early-1990s academic work (an insider-trading model in a 1991 *Economic Journal* paper) modeling bookmaker odds-setting when a proportion *z* of bettors are "insiders" with certain knowledge of the outcome, with the rest uninformed; the method "distributes more of the margin to longshots (where insider information has the most impact)." It is described as "the one favoured in the academic literature on favourite-longshot bias." — [Algorithmic Sports Betting Substack, "It's Time to Retire Shin's Method"](https://algorithmicsportsbetting.substack.com/p/its-time-to-retire-shins-method)
- **Štrumbelj (2014)**, "On determining probability forecasts from betting odds," *International Journal of Forecasting* (peer-reviewed): compares basic normalization, Shin probabilities, and two regression-based variants; finds "probabilities determined from betting odds using Shin's model are more accurate forecasts than those determined using basic normalization or regression models," and that betting odds generally are "the most accurate source of sports forecasts" relative to alternatives, though betting-exchange odds are "not always the best source, especially in smaller markets." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533), [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264349990_On_determining_probability_forecasts_from_betting_odds)
- Critique/practitioner-level pushback: a 2025-era practitioner piece ("It's Time to Retire Shin's Method") argues the most-cited empirical support for Shin's method is now "nearly a decade old and focused on traditional sports rather than modern prediction markets," and that "newer methods have emerged," naming the Clarke power method and a "Whelan's Shin-inspired method... designed to more realistically model insider trading dynamics" as more current alternatives. — [Algorithmic Sports Betting Substack](https://algorithmicsportsbetting.substack.com/p/its-time-to-retire-shins-method)
- Practical use-case guidance from industry sources: Shin's method is positioned as best for "three-way markets and soccer" and markets with strong favorite-longshot bias (horse racing, futures, lopsided two-way markets), given its horse-racing origin; the multiplicative method is positioned as adequate for "even-money markets like NFL spreads." — [search synthesis, sharkbetting.com](https://www.sharkbetting.com/blog/devig-explained)

### Inferences
- There is a meaningful split between academic preference (Štrumbelj 2014 peer-reviewed finding favoring Shin's method over basic normalization) and more recent practitioner-level methodological critique (favoring the power method as newer/better-performing per Clarke et al.'s comparisons) — the report should flag that "preferred method" depends on vintage of source and sport/market type, not a single settled academic consensus.
- No method is literally termed "additive" with a distinct peer-reviewed formula in the sources retrieved; it appears alongside multiplicative as a simpler proportional alternative but detail on its exact formula was not surfaced.

(2017) PDF directly (source returned HTTP 403); the power-method formula and performance claims above are sourced from secondary summaries (ResearchGate abstract page, search-engine synthesis) rather than the full text, so exact wording/formula should be treated as approximately but not verbatim correct pending direct access to the original paper.

## How do practitioners/researchers convert CLV into expected long-run ROI or edge?

### Takeaway
The conversion logic — not itself a peer-reviewed formula but a practitioner heuristic repeatedly asserted across industry sources — treats the closing line's implied (ideally no-vig) probability as an unbiased estimator of the true win probability; a bettor's "edge" is then approximated as the gap between the probability implied by their bet price and the no-vig closing probability, with sustained positive CLV interpreted as evidence of a repeatable long-run edge rather than variance, though no source quantified a precise CLV-to-ROI conversion ratio with statistical backing.

### Cited Findings
- Practitioner framing: "sustained positive CLV signals genuine edge; negative CLV suggests process weakness regardless of immediate results," and CLV is advocated as superior to tracking raw win-loss record because "a bettor can go 8-2 while consistently taking numbers worse than the close, or go 3-7 while repeatedly betting into prices that later move in the same direction" — i.e., win/loss outcomes are noisy over a finite sample while CLV is argued to isolate the "process" signal from variance. — [Joe Saumarez Smith](https://joesaumarez.co.uk/sports-betting-market-efficiency-and-the-closing-line)
- Pinnacle's own framing: consistently beating the closing line is described as "one of the most important indicators of a bettor's skill and long-term potential profitability," implicitly treating the closing line as the market's unbiased efficient estimate against which any persistent, positive deviation at bet time equates to expected future profit. — [Pinnacle](https://www.pinnacle.com/betting-resources/en/educational/what-is-closing-line-value-clv-in-sports-betting)
- Supporting (indirect) academic rationale: weak-form market efficiency findings that "no odds-based betting strategy will yield statistically significant long-term profits" imply that, if closing odds are efficient, deviating favorably from them before closing should on average translate into positive expected value, since the closing price approximates the best available estimate of true probability. — [search synthesis of market efficiency literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2773161824000193)
- Caveat from the same practitioner analysis: the CLV-to-edge mapping is strongest when segmented ("isolate where they actually outperform—certain totals, leagues, or derivative markets—rather than claiming broad advantage"), i.e., aggregate CLV across unrelated markets is treated by this source as a weaker edge signal than CLV computed within a specific, narrow betting strategy. — [Joe Saumarez Smith](https://joesaumarez.co.uk/sports-betting-market-efficiency-and-the-closing-line)

### Inferences
- The CLV→ROI conversion as practiced in industry is essentially an applied corollary of the efficient-closing-line assumption: if closing probability ≈ true probability, then (bettor's implied probability at placement) vs. (no-vig closing probability) approximates the bettor's true edge, and expected long-run ROI can be approximated as that probability gap (scaled by payout odds), analogous to a Kelly-criterion edge estimate — but this inferential chain was not found explicitly spelled out as a formula in any single source retrieved; it is being inferred by this researcher from the combination of practitioner CLV framing and academic efficiency findings, and should be labeled as an inference rather than a directly cited formula.
- Levitt's (2004) finding that bookmakers may price to exploit bettor biases rather than purely to balance/clear the market is a reason for academic caution about over-trusting the "closing line = true probability" assumption that underlies the whole CLV-to-ROI heuristic — this is a legitimate academic counterpoint that should be surfaced in any final report alongside the practitioner claims.

### Gaps
- No source provided a validated statistical formula or empirically estimated conversion rate (e.g., "X% CLV edge translates to Y% long-run ROI") — all industry claims found were qualitative/directional ("positive CLV signals long-term profitability") without a cited regression or model coefficient linking CLV magnitude to realized ROI.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that specifically tests whether individual bettors' measured CLV predicts their realized future ROI out-of-sample (a direct empirical validation of the core CLV premise) — this remains an assumption carried over from market-efficiency literature rather than a directly tested claim about bettor-level CLV tracking.
