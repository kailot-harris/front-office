# Academic Research on Closing Line Value (CLV) as a Predictor of Long-Run Betting Profitability

## Headline caveat (read first)

**No peer-reviewed paper was found that directly tests the specific hypothesis "CLV predicts long-run realized bettor ROI" via a bettor-level regression of ROI on CLV.** The "CLV is the best predictor of long-term profitability" claim that saturates sports-betting industry blogs, tracking-tool marketing pages, and tipster sites (Pikkit, Unabated, Action Network, SharpROI, RebelBetting, etc.) is **not sourced to a specific peer-reviewed study in any of those pages** — it is repeated as received wisdom without citation. Multiple targeted searches for "CLV regression ROI," "sharp bettor closing line value regression," and "Journal of Gambling Studies closing line value" returned only industry content, not academic citations. This should be flagged explicitly in any report: the practitioner concept of CLV is built on a plausible but, to date, **academically under-tested** causal claim.

The closest rigorous analogues are: (1) market-efficiency studies of the "closing line" as the best available forecast of game outcomes (using betting-market data directly, not bettor cohorts), (2) studies of informed/insider trading and price convergence in betting exchanges (mostly horse racing and football), and (3) a few natural-experiment/dataset papers (Moskowitz 2021; Simon 2024) that study line movement predictiveness at the market level. None of these regress individual or cohort-level realized ROI on a CLV metric the way a sportsbook-side CLV product would. This gap itself is a finding worth reporting.

## Key Question 1: What academic papers have studied opening-to-closing line movement, market efficiency of closing lines, or "sharp bettor" identification via something like CLV?

### Takeaway
There is a substantial academic literature (JQAS-adjacent working papers, Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance, Journal of Finance, Management Science, and several economics department theses) on point-spread/line-movement market efficiency and on using the closing line as the benchmark "true" probability — but almost none of it is framed as "CLV causally predicts bettor profitability." Most papers test whether the *market* (not individual bettors) is efficient by closing time, and whether *specific rule-based strategies* applied against the close are profitable, rather than regressing bettor ROI on captured CLV.

### Cited Findings
- Tobias Moskowitz, "Asset Pricing and Sports Betting," forthcoming/working paper for the *Journal of Finance*, uses "the most comprehensive betting dataset" covering NBA, NFL, MLB, and NHL, spanning more than 100,000 contracts across three decades, to study line movement from open to close as an analogue of asset-pricing momentum/reversal and the role of informed ("sharp") money moving the line. — [Moskowitz paper, NYU Stern mirror](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/finance/docs/pdfs/Seminars/moskowitz_seminar_paper.pdf); [Yale SOM mirror](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/08/AssetPricingandSportsBetting_JF.pdf)
- Jay Simon, "Inefficient Forecasts at the Sportsbook: An Analysis of Real-Time Betting Line Movement," *Management Science*, Vol. 70, No. 12 (December 2024), pp. 8583–8611. Dataset: real-time betting-line movement from opening to closing across **four sportsbooks for 3,681 MLB games**. Methodology: forecast-reliability tests at multiple lead times plus variance-ratio/autocorrelation tests for weak-form efficiency. Finding: closing-line forecasts are "mostly reliable," but lines show **significant negatively autocorrelated changes** (overreaction) that simple strategies could exploit for "significant profit"; forecast quality does not monotonically improve as game time approaches, and weekend day-game lines 90 minutes before first pitch are significantly worse forecasts than lines set earlier — directly rejecting strict weak-form efficiency of the closing line in MLB. — [IDEAS/RePEc citation record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html); [ResearchGate preprint](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372441761_Inefficient_Forecasts_at_the_Sportsbook_An_Analysis_of_Real-Time_Betting_Line_Movement)
- Ryan Earl Oswald, "Testing the Efficiency of the NFL Betting Market" (University of Northern Iowa, undergraduate/honors thesis, submitted May 2022). Examines NFL spread, total, and moneyline markets 2007–2022 for efficiency, individually and jointly. Finds the closing market is "generally efficient" but with "consistent exceptions": e.g., underdogs with a spread greater than 7 points in games with a total over 50.5 covered 64.7% of the time against a 52.38% break-even threshold over the sample — a specific profitable closing-line-based rule, flagged as a thesis (not peer-reviewed). — [UNI ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.uni.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1538&context=hpt)
- Corey A. Shank, "Information asymmetry in the NFL gambling market: Inside information versus informed bettors," *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance*, December 2022. Uses a proprietary NFL betting dataset to test whether sportsbooks or bettors hold superior information; finds **no evidence sportsbooks hold more information than bettors**, consistent with informed ("sharp") bettors driving some closing-line movement rather than the book itself. Peer-reviewed journal article. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214635022000806)
- Related Shank-authored NFL-market papers using the same closing-line-as-benchmark framework: "Momentum trading in the NFL gambling market" (*Finance Research Letters*, 2023); "Behavioral biases in the NFL gambling market: Overreaction to news and the recency bias" (*Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance*, 2021); "Anchoring bias in the NFL gambling market" (*Economics Letters*, 2025). All use line-movement-to-close as the dependent/predictive variable for behavioral biases, not bettor-level ROI. — [Finance Research Letters](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v55y2023ipbs1544612323003781.html); [JBEF 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/beexfi/v31y2021ics2214635021000666.html); [Economics Letters 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolet/v250y2025ics0165176525001259.html)
- Classic foundational literature establishing the closing line as the efficiency benchmark: Zuber, Gandar & Bowers (1985) tested market rationality using 1983 NFL season data and could not reject rationality; Gandar, Zuber, O'Brien & Russo (1988) found no statistical evidence of market inefficiency overall but identified 3 of 7 tested betting rules as profitable against the closing line. Both are foundational peer-reviewed studies (cited widely, e.g. in UNLV Gaming Research & Review Journal summaries) establishing the "closing line as efficient-market benchmark" paradigm that underlies modern CLV framing. — [UNLV Gaming Research & Review summary](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?httpsredir=1&article=1123&context=grrj)
- Steven D. Levitt (2004), "Why Are Gambling Markets Organised So Differently From Financial Markets?", *The Economic Journal*. Argues sportsbooks set prices to exploit known bettor biases rather than to balance action, implying the closing line is not a pure aggregation of informed money — a theoretical counterpoint relevant to whether "beating the close" purely reflects informational skill vs. exploiting bookmaker-side biases. — [ResearchGate discussion/citations](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4810045_Why_are_Gambling_Markets_Organized_so_Differently_from_Financial_Markets)
- A follow-on empirical literature (Paul & Weinbach and others) directly tests the Levitt model using actual bet-percentage data from Sportsbook.com; results are mixed — some support price-setting-to-maximize-profit, others find no overwhelming support — again using the closing line as the terminal efficiency benchmark rather than studying bettor-level CLV/ROI correlation. — [Kyle Woodward hosted PDF of Paul & Weinbach, International Journal of Sport Finance 2008](https://kylewoodward.com/blog-data/pdfs/references/paul+weinbach-international-journal-of-sport-finance-2008A.pdf)

### Inferences
- The academic literature's dominant framing is "is the closing line an efficient/unbiased forecast of the outcome" (a market-efficiency question), not "does an individual/cohort bettor's captured CLV predict their realized long-run ROI" (a bettor-skill-persistence question). The practitioner CLV concept appears to be a plausible, intuitively-motivated extrapolation from market-efficiency findings (if the close is close to the true probability, then consistently beating it implies positive expected value) rather than something that has itself been directly estimated and reported with effect sizes/R² in a published paper.
- Several papers (Simon 2024, Oswald 2022) actually complicate the simplistic "closing line = ground truth" assumption CLV relies on, by showing the closing line itself is not perfectly efficient (overreaction, degraded forecasts near some game times) — meaning CLV as a predictor is itself built on an imperfect benchmark.

### Gaps
- No bettor-level (not market-level) dataset study was found that tracks many individual bettors' CLV and then correlates/regresses it against their realized long-run ROI with reported sample size, regression coefficients, R², or significance levels. This exact study design could not be located in peer-reviewed literature, SSRN, arXiv, or thesis repositories searched.
- Could not verify whether JQAS (Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports) specifically has published a CLV-focused paper; searches returned only the journal's general description and unrelated JQAS articles (e.g., randomness-in-sport papers), no CLV-specific hit.

## Key Question 2: Are there papers using large bet-level datasets from sportsbooks/exchanges (e.g., Betfair) regressing realized ROI on CLV across bettor cohorts, or testing whether bettors who beat the close earn positive long-run returns?

### Takeaway
No such bettor-cohort regression study was found for sports betting or Betfair sports/racing markets in the searches conducted. The closest substitute is the informed-trading literature on Betfair horse racing, which studies whether late price movements (not individually identified bettor CLV) reflect informational advantage and predict race outcomes — a market-level, not bettor-level, test.

### Cited Findings
- Alexis Direr (2011) is cited in the favourite-longshot-bias literature for modeling informed vs. noise traders in horse-race betting markets via optimal-stopping/costly-learning theory, explaining how bookmaker odds adjust as informed ("insider") money moves prices toward close — a theoretical/structural model rather than an empirical bettor-ROI regression. Full paper was not independently retrieved (search snippet only); treat as secondary citation pending primary-source verification. — [Referenced in RePEc "Pricing Decisions and Insider Trading in Horse Betting Markets"](https://ideas.repec.org/p/rug/rugwps/12-772.html)
- Reading University Economics Discussion Paper, "Information, Prices and Efficiency in an Online Betting Market" (University of Reading, Department of Economics working paper, no. EM/DP2019/10, 2019) — a working paper directly studying price informativeness in an online/exchange betting market. Content could not be extracted (PDF fetch returned binary/unparseable content); flagged as a promising unverified lead rather than a confirmed finding. — [Reading.ac.uk working paper PDF](https://www.reading.ac.uk/web/files/economics/emdp201910.pdf)
- Vlastakis, Dotsis & Markellos, "How Efficient Is the European Football Betting Market? Evidence from Arbitrage and Trading Strategies," *Journal of Forecasting*, 2009 (peer-reviewed). Dataset: odds from 12 bookmakers across 21 European football championships over 11 years. Methodology: tests whether systematically selecting odds above an implied-probability threshold yields positive returns. Finding: a rate of return of **4.45%** using best-available odds across bookmakers and **2.78%** using mean odds, concentrated in backing strong favourites (win probability >90%); results robust to real-time data and risk-aversion assumptions, though transaction costs erode profitability except for small stakes. This is a market-level arbitrage/strategy-profitability test, not a bettor-cohort CLV regression, but it is one of the few peer-rereviewed papers with quantified ROI figures tied to pre-closing versus closing odds comparisons. — [IDEAS/RePEc citation](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/45y2013i3p343-356.html) (note: a related/overlapping citation also appears dated 2013 in *Applied Economics*; treat the exact venue/year as needing final verification — multiple RePEc records exist for closely related titles by the same or overlapping author sets)

### Inferences
- Betfair-specific, bettor-cohort-level ROI-on-CLV regressions do not appear to be a developed research niche; the exchange-market literature instead favors race-level or match-level price-efficiency tests (does the final exchange price beat naive earlier prices at predicting the winner), which is structurally different from asking whether a given trader's personal "beat-the-close" record predicts their future trading P&L.
- Given CLV tracking tools (Pikkit, SharpROI, Unabated, RebelBetting, NumberEdge) are built for individual bettors and marketed using this claim, it's plausible that any rigorous bettor-cohort CLV-ROI analysis that exists is proprietary (held internally by sportsbooks like Pinnacle, DraftKings, FanDuel, or exchanges like Betfair) and unpublished, rather than absent from reality. This should be stated as a reasonable inference, not a confirmed fact.

- No large bet-level dataset study (CLV vs. realized bettor ROI, with bettor cohorts) was found despite dedicated searches using the exact suggested terms ("sharp bettor closing line value regression ROI", "closing line value predictor profitability sports betting"). All hits were either industry/marketing content or unrelated academic market-efficiency papers.

## Key Question 3: What statistical methodology is used (regression of ROI on CLV, bootstrap/Monte Carlo convergence, signal-to-noise framing, variance decomposition)?

### Takeaway
Across the academic papers actually located, the dominant methodologies are market-efficiency econometrics (variance-ratio tests, autocorrelation/overreaction tests, weak-form efficiency tests, threshold-based trading-rule backtests) rather than bettor-level signal-to-noise or bootstrap-convergence framing. The "CLV converges faster than win/loss record because win/loss has more variance" argument that underlies the practitioner case for CLV is a **statistical argument about sample sizes needed for a binary (win/loss) outcome versus a continuous (price-difference) outcome to achieve significance** — but no paper was found that formally runs this as a Monte Carlo/bootstrap demonstration in a peer-reviewed sports-betting context.

### Cited Findings
- Simon (2024, *Management Science*) uses variance-ratio tests and autocorrelation testing on sequential line changes (opening to closing, across multiple sportsbooks and intermediate time points) to test weak-form efficiency — a standard financial-econometrics toolkit applied to the "signal" (price movement) itself, rather than to downstream bettor returns. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html)
- Vlastakis, Dotsis & Markellos (2009, *Journal of Forecasting*) use threshold-based odds-selection trading-rule simulation (selecting all odds above/below an implied-probability cutoff and computing realized return across the full multi-year, multi-bookmaker sample) as their core method — effectively a backtested-strategy ROI calculation rather than a bettor-cohort regression, but it is the closest methodological analogue found to "regress realized return on a market-signal variable." — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/45y2013i3p343-356.html)
- Oswald (2022 thesis) uses rule-based backtesting (e.g., spread/total threshold combinations) against realized ATS win rates compared to the 52.38%-vigorish breakeven threshold — a simple hit-rate-vs-breakeven significance framing, not a continuous CLV regression. — [UNI ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.uni.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1538&context=hpt)
- Classic Gandar/Zuber-school papers use standard OLS/probit regression of game outcomes (ATS results) on the point spread itself to test for bias/rationality of the closing number — methodologically the ancestor of all later closing-line efficiency work, but again testing the market/line, not individual bettor ROI. — [UNLV GRRJ summary of this literature](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?httpsredir=1&article=1123&context=grrj)
- No paper located performs an explicit bootstrap or Monte Carlo simulation contrasting the sample-size-to-significance requirements of a binary win/loss outcome versus a continuous CLV metric. This specific "signal-to-noise / convergence properties" methodology — frequently asserted qualitatively in industry content (e.g., "ROI aligns with CLV once sample grows to 2,000–5,000 bets") — was not found attached to any citable statistical derivation or published study.

### Inferences
- The absence of a formal bootstrap/variance-decomposition paper on CLV-vs-ROI convergence suggests this specific quantitative claim, while statistically plausible (a continuous spread/price measure has lower sampling variance per observation than a Bernoulli win/loss outcome, so it should in principle require a smaller sample to detect a given true edge), has not been rigorously demonstrated in the academic literature reviewed. It may exist in grey literature (sportsbook/syndicate internal research, conference talks at analytics conferences like MIT Sloan Sports Analytics or OptaPro) not indexed by the searches performed.

## Overall Summary for Report Writer

1. **The specific hypothesis the user asked about — "CLV predicts long-run bettor ROI," tested via bettor-level regression — does not appear to have a direct peer-reviewed academic test.** This should be stated plainly: the practitioner/industry consensus on CLV is not currently backed by a citable academic regression study with reported effect sizes, R², or significance levels relating individual/cohort bettor CLV to realized ROI.
2. The closest rigorous substitute literature is the **market/closing-line efficiency literature** (Gandar & Zuber school; Levitt 2004 and responses; Oswald 2022 thesis; Shank's NFL behavioral-bias series; Simon 2024 in *Management Science*; Vlastakis, Dotsis & Markellos 2009 in *Journal of Forecasting*), which tests whether the closing line is an efficient/unbiased predictor of outcomes and whether specific threshold-based strategies beat it — a necessary but not sufficient foundation for the CLV-predicts-ROI claim.
3. Peer-reviewed status flags: Simon (2024, *Management Science*) — peer-reviewed, top journal. Shank's NFL papers (*Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance*, *Finance Research Letters*, *Economics Letters*) — peer-reviewed. Vlastakis, Dotsis & Markellos (*Journal of Forecasting*, cited here via an *Applied Economics* RePEc record — venue needs final verification) — peer-reviewed. Oswald (2022) and the two Claremont McKenna theses — undergraduate/honors theses, explicitly not peer-reviewed. Moskowitz "Asset Pricing and Sports Betting" — a *Journal of Finance*-track working paper (verify final publication status independently; treat as a rigorous working paper pending confirmation of print publication).
4. Horse-racing/Betfair informed-trading literature (Direr 2011 and the broader favourite-longshot-bias/insider-trading literature) is the nearest analogue outside major US sports but was the hardest to pin down with primary sources in this search; flagged as requiring further retrieval if the final report needs this angle developed further.
