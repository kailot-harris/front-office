# MLB-Specific Evidence on Closing Line Value, Line Movement, and Betting Market Efficiency

**Headline caveat up front:** There is essentially no peer-reviewed academic literature that uses the specific term "closing line value (CLV)" in the context of baseball. The term "CLV" is a practitioner/industry coinage (poker- and sports-betting-community derived) rather than an academic one. However, there IS a substantial, MLB-specific academic literature — larger than for most other sports — on MLB betting *market efficiency*, *opening-to-closing line movement*, and *favorite-longshot/reverse-bias* effects, going back to 1994 and continuing through 2024-2025. These papers test hypotheses functionally equivalent to CLV-based profitability claims (i.e., "does the closing line contain more information than the opening line, and can bettors systematically beat it"), even though they don't use CLV terminology. This note treats that literature as the closest available MLB-specific evidence, and flags where non-baseball CLV literature would need to be imported as a substitute (that import is explicitly NOT done here, per task scope — this note stays baseball-specific).

## Are there academic papers studying MLB betting market efficiency specifically (moneylines, run lines, unders/overs, home underdogs)?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is the best-covered sub-question. A multi-decade academic literature (Journal of Finance, Applied Economics, Journal of Economics and Finance, Management Science) specifically tests MLB moneyline and totals market efficiency. The consensus is that the MLB moneyline market is highly efficient at the game level, with a documented **reverse favorite-longshot bias** (opposite of the racetrack pattern), while **longer-horizon season-total (win total) markets show more persistent inefficiency** tied to behavioral heuristics.

### Cited Findings
- Woodland & Woodland (1994), "Market Efficiency and the Favorite-Longshot Bias: The Baseball Betting Market," *Journal of Finance* 49(1), pp. 269-279: using MLB closing lines from 1979-1989, found the market efficient but documented a **reverse favorite-longshot bias** — bettors favor underdogs relative to what is actuarially justified in baseball, opposite of the classic racetrack favorite-longshot bias — [EconPapers citation](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:49:y:1994:i:1:p:269-79), discussed in [Risks journal literature review](https://doi.org/10.3390/risks9010022).
- Gandar, Zuber, et al., "Information effects in major league baseball betting markets," *Applied Economics* 44(6) (2012): "Previous studies point to a generally efficient baseball betting market with no profitable betting strategies. However, failure to consider the time of year in which the bets are placed neglects differences in available information throughout the season... incorporating the time of year in which the bet is made generates persistent profitable betting strategies" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233229569_Information_effects_in_major_league_baseball_betting_markets), [Tandfonline abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036846.2010.518951).
- Paul & Weinbach (2008-era work, cited via secondary search), "Line Movements and Market Timing in the Baseball Gambling Market": found offshore sportsbooks highly efficient (no profitable strategy from line movements alone), but betting against first line movements toward favorites was profitable specifically at a smaller Las Vegas sportsbook — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/247739673_Line_Movements_and_Market_Timing_in_the_Baseball_Gambling_Market) (full text behind a 403 paywall; this is a secondary-source characterization, not independently verified against primary text).
- Lock (2013), Erasmus University Rotterdam master's thesis, "Testing Efficiency in the Major League of Baseball Sports Betting Market": concluded the MLB market is quite efficient and that in the long run almost no abnormal returns can be made — [full PDF](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/13879/13879-Lock.pdf) (note: this is a master's thesis, not peer-reviewed; flagged as lower rigor than the journal articles below).
- A 2014 *Applied Financial Economics* paper, "Re-examining the efficiency of the Major League Baseball over-under betting market": using data including run lines and moneylines, found little evidence that the MLB totals market is inefficient — [Tandfonline abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09603107.2014.925052) (full text not independently accessed; abstract-level characterization only).
- Woodland & Woodland (2015/2016), "Additional evidence of heuristic-based inefficiency in season wins total betting markets: Major League Baseball," *Journal of Economics and Finance* 40(3), pp. 538-548: tested betting rules exploiting the "representativeness heuristic" (bettors expect small samples of games to generalize to the full season) in the **preseason/in-season win-total over-under market** and found this specific market **inefficient**, with profitable wagering opportunities from bettors overreacting to prior-season performance, especially for teams with winning records — [Springer link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x), [full PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x.pdf).
- "Do profitable wagering strategies indicate an inefficient market? An investigation into simple wagering strategies in Major League Baseball moneyline markets," *Applied Economics* 57(34) (2024/2025), pp. 5096-5102: tested **1,547 simple wagering strategies using MLB moneyline data from 1999 to 2016**; only 38 (2.46%) were profitable at the 5% significance level and 7 (0.45%) at the 1% level — after correcting for multiple-hypothesis/family-wise error, the paper concludes this confirms the "extremely efficient nature of the Major League Baseball moneyline wagering market" over that timeframe — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115), [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/v57y2025i34p5096-5102.html).
- "Game over? Assessing the impact of the emergence of a profitable wagering strategy in major league baseball wagering markets," *Applied Economics* (2018): per secondary search summary, examines whether a previously-identified profitable MLB strategy persisted after publication/discovery (market-adaptation / self-destructing-anomaly framing) — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2018.1430349) (full text returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch; title and framing confirmed via search listing only, specific findings not independently verified — flagged as a gap below).
- Secondary/aggregator characterization (general search synthesis, not a single primary citation): "the over-under betting market for baseball is considered efficient because the profitability of identified strategies was eliminated in the following years" — this is consistent with the "Game over?" paper's apparent thesis (anomalies get arbitraged away once documented) but should be treated as a weak, unverified aggregator claim pending direct access to that paper.

### Inferences
- Taken together, the MLB literature draws a consistent distinction by market type: **single-game moneyline and run-line/totals markets are close to efficient** (Woodland & Woodland 1994; Lock 2013; the 2024/2025 Applied Economics moneyline paper; the 2014 Applied Financial Economics totals paper), while **season-long aggregate markets (preseason win totals) show more persistent, heuristic-driven inefficiency** (Woodland & Woodland 2015/2016). This suggests any CLV-style profitability framework built on MLB data should expect much smaller, more fragile edges in daily game lines than in preseason futures/win-total markets.
- The "Game over?" paper's apparent framing (anomalies disappearing once publicized) is itself indirect evidence consistent with a CLV logic: if the closing line absorbs known public strategies over time, early/opening-line bettors who exploited now-known biases before they were arbitraged away would show ex-post CLV-positive betting histories relative to the (now-adjusted) closing line.

### Gaps
- Could not independently verify the "Game over?" (2018) paper's specific findings/numbers — Tandfonline blocked direct fetch (403); only the title and general framing from search-engine summarization could be used, which is a weaker source and is flagged accordingly.
- Could not independently verify the Paul & Weinbach "Line Movements and Market Timing" paper's full methodology (sample size, years) — ResearchGate blocked direct fetch (403); characterization relies on secondary search-engine synthesis only.
- The Lock (2013) thesis PDF could not be parsed for its sample size/time period/exact methodology (binary/compressed PDF content was not extractable by the fetch tool); its efficiency conclusion is retained from search-summary text only, and as a master's thesis it carries less evidentiary weight than peer-reviewed journal articles.

## Are there papers or rigorous analyses of MLB opening-to-closing line movement specifically?

### Takeaway
Yes — the strongest, most recent, and most rigorous MLB-specific study found is Jay Simon's 2024 *Management Science* paper, which directly analyzes real-time opening-to-closing MLB betting line movement across multiple sportsbooks and finds statistically exploitable inefficiencies in how lines move, including negative autocorrelation in line changes and non-monotonic forecast accuracy as game time approaches.

### Cited Findings
- Jay R. Simon, "Inefficient Forecasts at the Sportsbook: An Analysis of Real-Time Betting Line Movement," *Management Science* 70(12), pp. 8583-8611, published 2024 (DOI: 10.1287/mnsc.2022.00456): tests efficiency using **detailed betting line movement data from opening until closing, from four different sportsbooks, for each of 3,681 MLB games** — [DOI link](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.00456), [publisher record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.2022.00456), [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html).
- Key finding 1 (forecast reliability but exploitability): "the forecasts are mostly reliable, but there are simple betting strategies that would have yielded significant profit" over the sample — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372441761_Inefficient_Forecasts_at_the_Sportsbook_An_Analysis_of_Real-Time_Betting_Line_Movement).
- Key finding 2 (non-monotonic accuracy): forecasts do not always improve monotonically as a game approaches despite more information becoming available; specifically, "forecasts at weekend day games' start times are significantly worse than forecasts 90 minutes earlier" — same source.
- Key finding 3 (overreaction / negative autocorrelation): betting lines exhibit "significant negatively autocorrelated changes" — i.e., line movements tend to overreact and partially reverse — which the paper frames as a pattern "that could be exploited by sophisticated bettors" — same source.
- A related, unverified claim surfaced in search results (not independently confirmed from primary text, attributed loosely to "Weak Form Efficiency in Sports Betting Markets," *American Journal of Management* 23(2), 2023): "larger relative line movements are on average less accurate, suggesting exaggerated market reactions to new information and highlighting the importance of information processing time on efficiency" — [PDF link](https://myweb.ecu.edu/robbinst/PDFs/Weak%20Form%20Efficiency%20in%20Sports%20Betting%20Markets.pdf) (treat as a weaker, secondary-sourced claim; direct text not independently reviewed).

### Inferences
- The Simon (2024) *Management Science* paper is the single closest MLB-specific analogue to a "CLV validation study" in the peer-reviewed literature: it directly operationalizes the idea that the closing line should be the best unbiased forecast, tests that hypothesis against real multi-book opening-to-close line histories, and finds it is *not quite true* — closing lines in MLB still contain some predictable overreaction/mean-reversion pattern, meaning the closing line, while much better than the open, is not perfectly efficient. This provides empirical grounding for a CLV framework (closing lines are a strong but imperfect benchmark) without using CLV terminology.
- The negative-autocorrelation/overreaction finding is the kind of result a quant-driven MLB betting project could plausibly try to independently replicate or exploit using Retrosheet/odds-archive data, since it describes a structural, mechanical pattern (not just a one-off historical anomaly).

### Gaps
- Could not access the full PDF of Simon (2024) directly (ResearchGate link to the manuscript PDF returned HTTP 403), so finer details — e.g., exact profit magnitude of the "simple betting strategies," the specific years of the 3,681-game sample, and whether moneyline/run-line/totals markets were analyzed separately or pooled — could not be confirmed from primary text. The report writer should treat the quantitative/years detail as unconfirmed pending direct journal access (likely requires INFORMS/Management Science subscription access).
- No MLB-specific papers were found studying line movement specifically in response to **weather**, **lineup changes**, or **starting pitcher scratches/news** as the triggering information events — this is a clear, specific gap. The literature found addresses line movement/timing in aggregate (time-of-season, time-to-game-start) but not these specific information-event triggers.
- No MLB-specific papers were found on **bet volume/handle** directly causing line movement (as distinct from new information) — the closest adjacent finding (Paul & Weinbach, unverified detail) only addresses "first movements toward favorites," not volume-driven movement per se.

## Are there papers using MLB-specific data (Retrosheet, Sports Reference, odds archives) to test predictive models against betting markets, discussing CLV or closing odds as a benchmark?

### Takeaway
No papers were found that explicitly frame a predictive model's validation in CLV terms using MLB data; however, several papers use MLB closing lines/odds as an implied "efficient probability benchmark" against which other measures (e.g., Pythagorean win expectancy, uncertainty of outcome) are compared, which is functionally the same exercise as CLV benchmarking without the label.

### Cited Findings
- "Using Betting Market Odds to Measure the Uncertainty of Outcome in Major League Baseball" — uses MLB betting odds as a proxy benchmark for a different research question (competitive balance/uncertainty of outcome), confirming that MLB closing/market odds are treated in the academic literature as a credible "true probability" benchmark distinct from any one analyst's model — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46532390_Using_Betting_Market_Odds_to_Measure_the_Uncertainty_of_Outcome_in_Major_League_Baseball) (abstract/title level only; full methodology not independently reviewed).
- No paper was found that explicitly combines a Retrosheet- or Sports-Reference-sourced predictive/sabermetric model (e.g., a team-strength or win-probability model) with a historical MLB odds archive to compute something equivalent to "model vs. closing line" CLV-style backtests in a peer-reviewed venue.

### Inferences
- The absence of explicit Retrosheet-vs-odds-archive CLV backtesting papers is itself informative: it suggests that if a research/analytics project wants to validate an MLB predictive model using a CLV-style framework, there is no existing peer-reviewed template to lean on specifically for baseball — the methodological template would need to be adapted from the non-baseball literature (e.g., NBA/NFL/soccer market-efficiency papers, or the general asset-pricing-and-sports-betting literature such as Moskowitz's "Asset Pricing and Sports Betting," *Journal of Finance*, which pools multiple sports — [paper PDF](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/08/AssetPricingandSportsBetting_JF.pdf) — flagged explicitly here as NOT baseball-specific, included only to note it as the nearest cross-sport substitute for this particular question).

### Gaps
- This is the weakest-covered key question. No MLB-specific academic paper combining a Retrosheet/Sports-Reference-style structural model with a historical odds archive explicitly for CLV-benchmark validation was located in this search pass. A deeper, more targeted search (e.g., directly searching JQAS tables of contents, or Google Scholar citation chains from Moskowitz 2021) would likely be needed to fully close this gap, but was outside the ~15-tool-call budget for this research pass.

## If no MLB-specific CLV papers exist, what is the closest baseball-adjacent evidence?

### Takeaway
As established above, direct MLB market-efficiency and line-movement papers (not using CLV terminology) are themselves the closest and most relevant evidence — they do not need to be "substituted" from other sports, because MLB-specific literature on the underlying hypothesis (closing line informativeness, line movement predictability) is comparatively rich. The genuinely closest MLB-adjacent (not MLB-specific) academic anchor for an explicit "CLV" framework is Tobias Moskowitz's "Asset Pricing and Sports Betting" (Journal of Finance), which pools NBA/NFL data (and reportedly other sports) using a "steam move"/sharp-money framework conceptually identical to CLV, but is NOT MLB-specific.

### Cited Findings
- Tobias J. Moskowitz, "Asset Pricing and Sports Betting," *Journal of Finance* — studies how sharp/informed money moves point spreads and whether following late line movement ("steam") generates returns, used as the standard modern academic anchor for CLV-adjacent concepts in sports betting broadly — [paper PDF](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/08/AssetPricingandSportsBetting_JF.pdf). **Explicitly flagged: this paper's primary samples are NBA and NFL, not MLB** — it is cross-sport evidence, not baseball-specific, and should be labeled as such in any final report.
- The MLB-specific literature summarized above (Woodland & Woodland 1994/2015-16; Gandar et al. 2012; Simon 2024; the 2024/2025 Applied Economics moneyline paper) collectively functions as the baseball-specific substitute for an explicit "CLV paper," since each tests whether the closing/late line is a better, more-efficient predictor than the opening line or alternative heuristic-based strategies — same citations as above.

### Inferences
- A final report should state plainly: MLB does NOT lack academic attention on line efficiency/movement — it is one of the better-studied sports in this literature, likely because of Retrosheet's long public data history and baseball's long betting-market history (Woodland & Woodland's original paper dates to 1994, earlier than comparable NFL/NBA market-efficiency literature in some respects). What MLB specifically lacks is adoption of the "CLV" vocabulary itself, which is a framing convention from industry/practitioner writing (poker and sports-betting forums, c. 2010s) rather than from the academic finance/sports-economics tradition that produced the MLB papers cited here.

### Gaps
- None beyond what's noted above — this question is adequately answered by cross-referencing the other sections rather than requiring new search.

## MLB-specific public betting bias (popular teams: Yankees/Red Sox/Dodgers) and resulting line inefficiencies

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed academic paper was found that specifically quantifies a Yankees/Red Sox/Dodgers "popular team" bias in MLB lines; the available evidence here is exclusively from industry/practitioner sources (not academic), and should be clearly labeled as lower-rigor.

### Cited Findings
- Industry source (not peer-reviewed): popular teams such as the Red Sox, Yankees, Cubs, and Dodgers are described as "always... overpriced" because retail bettors back them regardless of price, implying sportsbooks shade lines to account for this — [procomputergambler.com style practitioner claim via search synthesis; primary article not independently opened, see search result list].
- Industry data point (2026 season preview, practitioner/sportsbook marketing content, not academic): the Dodgers "lead by far in ticket count" and are "No. 1 in dollars" for 2026 World Series futures, with 14.0% of tickets and 31.9% of handle — [Fox Sports](https://www.foxsports.com/stories/mlb/2026-mlb-betting-report-bettors-backing-dodgers), [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/espn/betting/story/_/id/48294857/mlb-betting-storylines-2026-preview-top-stories-espn). Note: this is futures-market handle concentration, not a demonstrated same-game moneyline mispricing, and is from current-season sportsbook marketing/journalism content, not research.
- General (non-MLB-specific, practitioner) claim found in search synthesis: "public betting on the favorite exceeds the book's implied price by 24-30 percentage points" — this claim's sport scope and original source could not be confirmed as MLB-specific or as academically sourced; treat with caution.
- Academically, the closest *related but distinct* finding is the Woodland & Woodland reverse favorite-longshot bias (1994) and the heuristic-based season win-total overreaction finding (2015/2016, same authors) — both describe systematic bettor misjudgment patterns in MLB, but neither is specifically about team-popularity/market-size bias (e.g., Yankees/Red Sox/Dodgers specifically) as opposed to favorite/underdog status or prior-season-record anchoring.

### Inferences
- There appears to be a genuine gap between the practitioner betting-content world (which routinely asserts a popular-team/"public fade" bias for the Yankees/Red Sox/Dodgers/Cubs) and the peer-reviewed sports-economics literature, which has tested and documented bias along the favorite/underdog and heuristic/recency axes but not, in the sources found here, along a specific "brand popularity" or market-size axis. A report should present the popular-team bias claim as a widely-repeated practitioner belief, explicitly unverified by the academic literature reviewed here.

### Gaps
- No academic (JQAS, Journal of Sports Economics, Applied Economics, or similar) paper specifically testing a "popular team" / market-size / fan-base-size bias in MLB lines was located. This may be a genuine void in the literature, or may require more targeted search specifically within JQAS back issues (not fully exhausted in this pass) — flagged as an open gap rather than asserting non-existence with full confidence.
- The quantitative "24-30 percentage point" figure found in search synthesis could not be traced to a specific primary source or confirmed as MLB-specific; it should not be used in a final report without further sourcing.

**Overall bottom line for the report writer:** MLB has a comparatively deep, decades-spanning academic literature on betting-market efficiency and line movement — richer in some respects than for NBA/NFL — but it is written in the vocabulary of "market efficiency," "favorite-longshot bias," and "information effects" rather than "CLV."
