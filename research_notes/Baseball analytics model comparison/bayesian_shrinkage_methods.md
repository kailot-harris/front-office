# Bayesian Hierarchical / Empirical Bayes Shrinkage for MLB Player Projection

## What specific Bayesian hierarchical / empirical Bayes shrinkage approaches have been published for MLB player projections?

### Takeaway
The literature runs from the 1975 foundational closed-form James-Stein estimator for batting average, through Brown's 2008 field test comparing several closed-form and semi-parametric EB/Bayes variants, to fully hierarchical multi-level Bayesian models (Jensen et al. 2009 "Bayesball" hitting model) and nonparametric longitudinal EB (Gu & Koenker 2017) that add age/position structure, mixture components, and temporal dependence not present in a single-step shrinkage-to-grand-mean estimator.

### Cited Findings
- Efron & Morris (1975), "Data Analysis Using Stein's Estimator and its Generalizations" (JASA), used batting averages of 18 MLB players through their first 45 at-bats of the 1970 season to predict each player's remaining-season batting average with a James-Stein shrinkage estimator. — [Efron & Morris 1975 PDF](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios602/MultilevelData/EfronMorrisJASA1975.pdf)
- The sample deliberately included an outlier (Roberto Clemente, hitting .400 over the 45 at-bats) to stress-test the estimator against a genuinely exceptional player. — [Efron & Morris 1975 PDF](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios602/MultilevelData/EfronMorrisJASA1975.pdf)
- Reported result: total squared prediction error of the raw/usual estimator (sample mean) was 0.086, versus 0.027 for the James-Stein estimator — roughly a 3x reduction in squared error, i.e., James-Stein MSE was less than half that of the raw sample mean. — [IdeaExchange@UAkron summary](https://ideaexchange.uakron.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1722&context=honors_research_projects)
- Brown, L.D. (2008), "In-Season Prediction of Batting Averages: A Field Test of Simple Empirical Bayes and Bayes Methods," Annals of Applied Statistics, Vol. 2, Issue 1, pp. 113–152, used every MLB player's batting record from the 2005 season, splitting performance into an early period (first ~3 months) used to predict the remainder of the season. — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-applied-statistics/volume-2/issue-1/In-season-prediction-of-batting-averages--A-field-test/10.1214/07-AOAS138.full); [arXiv:0803.3697](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.3697)
- Brown (2008) models the batting average as a binomial proportion (hits out of at-bats) representing latent player ability, and compares: a naive estimator (current average), a simple James-Stein-type/parametric empirical Bayes shrinkage, a newly proposed nonparametric empirical Bayes procedure, and hierarchical Bayes models with regression covariates. — [arXiv:0803.3697 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3697)
- Brown's finding: the nonparametric EB procedure performed particularly well on the full, heterogeneous dataset of all players, but on more homogeneous subsets (e.g., players grouped by position or playing time), the simpler, more familiar parametric EB/Bayes methods performed better. The naive estimator underperformed all EB/Bayes variants in every comparison. — [arXiv:0803.3697 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3697)
- Jensen, S.T., McShane, B., and Wyner, A.J. (2009), "Hierarchical Bayesian Modeling of Hitting Performance in Baseball," builds a three-level Bayesian hierarchical model: (1) player-season home-run counts as Binomial(at-bats, rate); (2) logit(rate) = position intercept + ballpark effect + cubic-B-spline age curve (position-specific aging curves); (3) a population-level two-component ("elite" vs. "non-elite") mixture with a hidden Markov model governing year-to-year transitions between elite/non-elite status. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Jensen et al. validated against 559 players (internal, 2006 season): full model RMSE = 5.30 HR with 80% interval coverage of 0.855, versus RMSE = 6.87 (coverage 0.644) for a stripped-down version without position/elite indicators, versus RMSE = 8.24 for a naive "repeat prior year's HR total" baseline. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- External comparison (118 elite hitters) against established industry projection systems: Jensen et al.'s model RMSE = 7.33 (41% "% Best" — fraction of players on which it had the lowest error among compared systems) vs. PECOTA RMSE = 7.11 (28% Best) vs. MARCEL RMSE = 7.82 (31% Best); for young players (age ≤ 26) the hierarchical model won 62% of the time, beating both PECOTA and MARCEL by a larger margin. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Removing the elite/non-elite mixture component degraded the hierarchical model's RMSE by roughly 30%, a larger effect than removing the position-level structure, indicating the heterogeneous-mixture shrinkage component (not just the age/position covariates) was the main driver of accuracy gains over simple shrinkage. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Gu, J. and Koenker, R. (2017), "Empirical Bayesball Remixed: Empirical Bayes Methods for Longitudinal Data," Journal of Applied Econometrics 32(3), pp. 575–599, treats batting-average prediction as a longitudinal Gaussian/binomial compound-decision problem, estimating a joint nonparametric distribution of player-specific location (ability level) and scale (year-to-year volatility) parameters using a convex-optimization reformulation of the Kiefer–Wolfowitz nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator (NPMLE). — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jae.2530); [EconPapers summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:32:y:2017:i:3:p:575-599)
- Gu & Koenker found a weak positive association between players' location (skill level) and scale (career volatility) in the estimated bivariate heterogeneity distribution, and reported that predicting 2012 batting averages from the prior decade of performance using their nonparametric EB approach showed "substantially improved performance over more naive methods" that impose more restrictive (i.e., simpler, homogeneous-variance) treatment of player heterogeneity — though the paper's summary available did not give a specific numeric MSE/accuracy figure. — [EconPapers summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:32:y:2017:i:3:p:575-599)
- Buenestado Pico/robust-Bayes follow-up: "Limiting the Shrinkage for the Exceptional by Objective Robust Bayesian Analysis: the 'Clemente Problem'" (arXiv:2506.10114) revisits the original 18-player Efron-Morris dataset and shows the classic James-Stein (square-loss, conjugate/light-tailed prior) estimator over-shrinks genuinely exceptional players: Clemente's 45-AB average of .400 was shrunk down to a predicted .290, when his actual rest-of-season average was .346 — i.e., simple shrinkage pulled the exceptional performer too far toward the mean. — [arXiv:2506.10114 (html)](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10114)
- The robust-Bayes fix in that paper replaces the square-loss/light-tailed-prior combination with heavy-tailed priors (double-exponential or Cauchy) or robust loss functions that penalize large errors more severely for ordinary players but allow exceptional players to retain more of their observed signal; both the empirical-Bayes and fully-Bayesian robust versions were reported to outperform the plain James-Stein estimator on the original Efron-Morris dataset. — [arXiv:2506.10114 (html)](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10114); [Pith summary](https://pith.science/paper/2506.10114)
- Independent, non-peer-reviewed but widely cited applied treatment: varianceexplained.org (David Robinson)'s blog/e-book series "Introduction to Empirical Bayes" (and its `ebbr` R package) walks through closed-form beta-binomial shrinkage for career batting average, then extends to beta-binomial *regression* (prior parameters depend on covariates like at-bat era/year) and a two-component beta-binomial mixture model (e.g., pitchers vs. hitters) as a step toward hierarchical structure — illustrating the beta-binomial-regression step as the natural "small addition" bridge between simple EB shrinkage and fuller hierarchical models. — [varianceexplained.org EB baseball series index](http://varianceexplained.org/r/empirical_bayes_baseball/)

### Inferences
- There is a clear methodological ladder: (1) closed-form single-prior EB/James-Stein (Efron-Morris 1975) → (2) closed-form EB with covariate-dependent priors / beta-binomial regression (Brown 2008's regression variants; Robinson's beta-binomial regression) → (3) multi-level hierarchical Bayes with mixture components, aging curves, and MCMC estimation (Jensen et al. 2009) → (4) nonparametric nonhierarchical-but-longitudinal EB via NPMLE/convex optimization (Gu & Koenker 2017) → (5) robust/heavy-tailed-prior EB specifically targeting the "exceptional player" failure mode of plain shrinkage (2025 Clemente Problem paper).
- Brown's (2008) central empirical finding — that the simple, more familiar methods actually beat the fancier nonparametric EB procedure once the player population is split into more homogeneous subgroups — is itself evidence that added model complexity does not uniformly pay off; homogeneity of the shrinkage target population matters more than estimator sophistication in some regimes.

- Could not retrieve exact numeric total-squared-error or MSE tables from Brown (2008) itself; the PDF fetch returned binary/compressed content and the HTML abstract page returned empty, so only the qualitative abstract-level conclusions (nonparametric EB best on heterogeneous full data, simpler methods better on homogeneous subsets) could be confirmed from the source. ## How do these multi-level/hierarchical approaches technically differ from a simple one-step closed-form empirical Bayes shrinkage toward a league/positional mean?

### Takeaway
The hierarchical/longitudinal approaches add at least one of four structural features absent from simple closed-form shrinkage: (1) covariates/structure in the prior (position, ballpark, age curves) rather than one global prior mean; (2) heterogeneous/mixture priors so the shrinkage amount itself varies by latent subgroup (elite vs. non-elite) rather than uniformly by sample size; (3) explicit temporal/longitudinal dependence (hidden Markov transitions across seasons, or joint location-scale heterogeneity across years) rather than treating each season independently; (4) MCMC/Gibbs or convex-optimization NPMLE estimation instead of a closed-form posterior mean.

### Cited Findings
- Simple EB (Efron-Morris style) shrinks every player's observed rate toward one global/grand mean, with shrinkage factor depending only on each player's own sample size (at-bats) relative to the prior variance — a single level, single prior, closed-form estimator. — [Efron & Morris 1975 PDF](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios602/MultilevelData/EfronMorrisJASA1975.pdf)
- Jensen et al.'s hierarchical model instead builds the prior itself out of structured components: a position-specific intercept, a ballpark effect, and a cubic-B-spline age curve feeding into the logit of the rate — so the "mean to shrink toward" is itself a regression surface over position/park/age rather than a single scalar. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- On top of that regression-structured prior, Jensen et al. add a two-component "elite"/"non-elite" mixture at the population level, meaning the amount of shrinkage is not a smooth monotone function of sample size alone — a player classified as elite is shrunk toward the elite sub-population's (higher) mean, with much less shrinkage toward the overall grand mean than the simple model would apply, and this distinction alone was responsible for roughly 30% of the model's RMSE improvement over a version lacking it. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Jensen et al.'s model adds a hidden Markov chain governing the probability of transitioning between elite/non-elite status from one year to the next, i.e., it explicitly models season-to-season correlation structure in latent ability class — something a single-season closed-form shrinkage estimator has no mechanism for. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Estimation in Jensen et al. is done via full MCMC/Gibbs sampling over all hierarchical levels, producing complete posterior predictive distributions (and the reported 80% interval coverage statistic), rather than a closed-form point-estimate shrinkage formula. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Gu & Koenker's nonparametric longitudinal EB jointly estimates a bivariate (not univariate) heterogeneity distribution over both each player's location (skill) and scale (year-to-year volatility) parameters, and found these two were weakly positively associated — information a single scalar shrinkage-to-mean estimator cannot represent, since it assumes homogeneous variance (scale) across players. — [EconPapers summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:32:y:2017:i:3:p:575-599)
- Gu & Koenker's method uses a convex-optimization reformulation of the Kiefer-Wolfowitz NPMLE rather than assuming a conjugate (e.g., Beta) prior family — it is nonparametric in the prior shape, whereas simple closed-form EB (Efron-Morris, standard beta-binomial) assumes a specific parametric prior (Normal or Beta) for closed-form tractability. — [EconPapers summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:32:y:2017:i:3:p:575-599)
- The robust-Bayes "Clemente Problem" paper differs from simple shrinkage specifically in the *tail* of the prior: it swaps the light-tailed conjugate prior (which forces strong, nearly-uniform shrinkage regardless of how extreme the observation is) for a heavy-tailed prior (double-exponential/Cauchy), which lets the posterior shrinkage amount itself depend nonlinearly on how extreme the observed data point is — exceptional players get shrunk much less, not just less because of larger sample size. — [arXiv:2506.10114 (html)](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10114)

### Inferences
- The single biggest practical lever distinguishing these hierarchical methods from closed-form shrinkage is *not* using MCMC per se, but rather making the "shrinkage target" and/or "shrinkage amount" depend on more than just (a) each player's own sample size and (b) one global grand mean — i.e., adding covariates to the prior mean (age/position/park) and/or adding a mixture/heavy-tailed structure to the prior shape.
- For a pipeline already doing closed-form Beta-Binomial or James-Stein shrinkage toward a league/positional mean, the "small addition" tier (covariate-adjusted priors, e.g., shrinking toward position-specific or park-adjusted means, or a 2-component mixture to avoid over-shrinking elite performers) is achievable without MCMC — e.g., Brown's covariate-regression EB variants and Robinson's beta-binomial regression are still closed-form/empirical-Bayes-estimated, not full MCMC. The "large architectural change" tier is the full Jensen et al.-style hierarchical model with hidden Markov elite-status transitions and B-spline aging curves estimated via Gibbs sampling, or Gu & Koenker's NPMLE convex-optimization approach — both require materially more modeling and computational infrastructure than a closed-form shrinkage formula.

### Gaps
- Did not find a direct head-to-head comparison in the literature of "simple closed-form shrinkage toward league mean" versus "closed-form shrinkage toward position-adjusted mean" in isolation (i.e., isolating just the covariate-adjustment step without the mixture/HMM additions) with a reported accuracy delta; the 30% RMSE-improvement figure from Jensen et al. is for removing the mixture component specifically, not the position/park covariates in isolation from the mixture.

## What validation/accuracy results are reported for these methods?

### Takeaway
Reported accuracy gains over naive/raw estimators are large and fairly consistent across studies (roughly 2-3x reduction in squared error for James-Stein vs. raw average; ~35% RMSE reduction for Jensen et al.'s full hierarchical model vs. a naive prior-year baseline), but gains of the more complex hierarchical model over simpler EB baselines are more modest and sometimes negative depending on how homogeneous the validation subpopulation is.

### Cited Findings
- Efron-Morris (1975): total squared error of James-Stein (0.027) vs. raw sample mean (0.086) on the 18-player 1970 dataset — a reduction to about 31% of the raw estimator's error (roughly 3.2x improvement). — [IdeaExchange@UAkron](https://ideaexchange.uakron.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1722&context=honors_research_projects)
- Jensen et al. (2009), internal 2006-season validation (n=559): full hierarchical model RMSE 5.30 HR vs. 6.87 HR for a reduced model without position/elite structure (a ~23% RMSE reduction from adding that structure) vs. 8.24 HR for a naive "repeat last year's total" baseline (a ~36% RMSE reduction for the full model vs. naive). — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Jensen et al. (2009), external validation against commercial systems (n=118 elite hitters): their model RMSE 7.33 vs. PECOTA RMSE 7.11 (PECOTA slightly better overall) vs. MARCEL RMSE 7.82 (hierarchical model better than MARCEL); but by the "% Best" metric the hierarchical model won on 41% of players vs. PECOTA's 28% and MARCEL's 31%, and it specifically outperformed both commercial systems among young players (≤26 years old), winning 62% of those cases. — [arXiv:0902.1360](https://arxiv.org/html/0902.1360v1)
- Brown (2008): no estimator strictly dominated — the nonparametric EB procedure won on the full heterogeneous player population, but lost to simpler parametric EB/Bayes methods once the data was split into more homogeneous subgroups; naive (no-shrinkage) estimation underperformed every EB/Bayes variant in all tested configurations. — [arXiv:0803.3697 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3697)
- Gu & Koenker (2017): nonparametric longitudinal EB reported "substantially improved performance" predicting 2012 batting averages from the prior decade versus more naive, homogeneous-heterogeneity treatments, though no specific MSE number was retrievable from the available abstract/summary sources. — [EconPapers summary](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:japmet:v:32:y:2017:i:3:p:575-599)
- "Clemente Problem" robust-Bayes paper (2025): both empirical-Bayes and fully-Bayesian heavy-tailed-prior versions were reported to beat the plain James-Stein estimator on the original 18-player Efron-Morris dataset, though a precise MSE/error number for the robust version vs. James-Stein's 0.027 was not given in the retrieved summary. — [arXiv:2506.10114 (html)](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10114)

### Inferences
- The marginal accuracy gain from "simple shrinkage" to "naive/no shrinkage" is large and essentially universal (shrinkage always wins by a wide margin in every study reviewed). The marginal gain from "simple shrinkage" to "full hierarchical model" is real but much smaller and context-dependent — sometimes the hierarchical model loses to a strong, simpler, closed-form competitor (e.g., Jensen et al.'s model lost by a small margin to PECOTA on aggregate RMSE despite winning on the "% Best" metric and among young players; Brown's nonparametric EB lost to simpler methods on homogeneous subgroups). This suggests diminishing, and situation-dependent, returns from the extra architectural complexity relative to the dramatic initial gain from doing any shrinkage at all.

### Gaps
- No controlled, apples-to-apples study was found that holds everything constant and isolates purely "closed-form shrinkage toward league mean" vs. "closed-form shrinkage toward position/park-adjusted mean" (without the added mixture/HMM machinery) to quantify that specific intermediate step's accuracy gain.

## Are there papers applying these methods specifically to pitching stats (ERA, FIP, K%, BB%) rather than batting average?

### Takeaway
Direct hierarchical-Bayes applications to classic rate-stat pitching metrics (ERA, FIP, K%, BB%) analogous to the batting-average literature are sparse in what was found; the closest located work is Brill, Deshpande & Wyner's (2022/2023) Bayesian multinomial regression model of the "time through the order" pitching penalty, which partially pools/shrinks pitcher-quality and batter-quality random effects within a hierarchical Bayesian framework, and general-purpose applied blog treatments (varianceexplained.org) that mention beta-binomial shrinkage being extendable to pitchers.

### Cited Findings
- Brill, R.S., Deshpande, S.K., and Wyner, A.J., "A Bayesian analysis of the time through the order penalty in baseball" (arXiv:2210.06724; published JQAS 2023, doi:10.1515/jqas-2022-0116), uses a Bayesian multinomial regression model of plate-appearance outcomes that adjusts for (and implicitly partially pools) batter quality, pitcher quality, handedness, and home-field advantage, to estimate whether pitcher performance discontinuously drops off between times through the batting order. — [arXiv:2210.06724](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06724)
- That paper's main finding was that after properly adjusting for confounders via the hierarchical/random-effects structure, there was little evidence of a strong discontinuous effect between times through the order (i.e., continuous within-game fatigue/adjustment explains most of the apparent "TTOP" effect, not a discrete three-times-through-the-order jump). — [arXiv:2210.06724 summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.06724)
- A general applied source (varianceexplained.org, David Robinson's empirical Bayes series) states that empirical Bayes shrinkage is applied in pitcher/batter matchup analysis by shrinking pitchers "toward the mean quality of pitchers rather than toward an overall mean," and separately notes that strikeout rate is "much more ability-driven than batting average," implying a different (typically less aggressive) shrinkage/prior-variance calibration is needed for K%-type pitching rate stats versus batting average. — [varianceexplained.org / search synthesis](http://varianceexplained.org/r/empirical_bayes_baseball/)
- No peer-reviewed paper directly analogous to Efron-Morris, Brown (2008), or Jensen et al. (2009) — i.e., one that builds a hierarchical/EB shrinkage model specifically targeting ERA, FIP, K%, or BB% as the outcome variable, with reported validation accuracy against naive/raw pitching stats — was found in the searches conducted.

### Inferences
- The pitching side of this literature appears considerably less developed than the batting-average/home-run side; most explicit hierarchical-Bayes "player projection" papers found (Efron-Morris, Brown 2008, Jensen et al. 2009, Gu & Koenker 2017) are batting-focused. Pitching-stat hierarchical models that do exist (e.g., Brill et al.) are framed around a specific effect (TTOP) rather than as a general projection/shrinkage system for ERA/FIP/K%/BB%, though the random-effects machinery they use (partial pooling of pitcher- and batter-quality effects) is structurally the same family of tool.
- Given strikeout rate is noted as more ability-driven (lower year-to-year variance relative to signal) than batting average, a naive carryover of batting-average-calibrated shrinkage parameters to K%/BB% would likely over-shrink; any adoption of these methods for pitching stats would need separately-estimated prior variance/shrinkage-weight parameters per stat, which is true even for the simplest closed-form EB approach and is not itself evidence for needing a more complex hierarchical architecture.

### Gaps
- Could not find a published, peer-reviewed hierarchical Bayesian or empirical Bayes paper with ERA, FIP, K%, or BB% as the primary projected outcome (as opposed to batting average/OBP/HR/SLG, or a pitching effect like TTOP used as a secondary application of hierarchical random effects). This is a genuine gap in the literature as surveyed, not just a search-coverage failure — the search terms used ("empirical Bayes pitching statistics," "hierarchical Bayesian ERA FIP JQAS," "Bayesian shrinkage baseball pitching statistics") did not surface a direct hit, suggesting such papers are rare or nonexistent in the peer-reviewed venues searched (JQAS, Annals of Applied Statistics, arXiv stat.AP).
- Could not determine whether any projection systems (academic or industry, e.g., Marcel, PECOTA, Steamer) that do project ERA/FIP/K%/BB% and are sometimes described as "empirical Bayes" have had their specific pitching-stat methodology published and peer-reviewed with reported accuracy numbers; this would be a useful area for a follow-up search focused on industry projection system methodology write-ups rather than academic journals.
