# Devigging Methodologies for Converting Sportsbook Odds to True Probabilities

## What is Shin's method, what problem does it solve, and what is its mathematical formulation?

### Takeaway
Shin's method (Shin 1991, 1992, 1993) models the bookmaker as pricing against a mix of uninformed bettors and a small group of "insiders" with superior information, and inverts that model to recover zero-margin probabilities that redistribute the overround unevenly across outcomes — concentrating more margin on longshots — which directly targets the favourite-longshot bias that simple proportional devigging ignores; it generally requires numerical (iterative) solving rather than a closed form in markets with more than two outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Shin introduced his insider-trading model in a 1991 Economic Journal paper ("Measuring the Incidence of Insider Trading in a Market for State-Contingent Claims" / related 1991-1993 Economic Journal and Applied Financial Economics work) modeling a bookmaker facing bettors with heterogeneous information, including a subset with superior ("insider") information that the bookmaker cannot identify in advance — [Measuring the Incidence of Insider Trading in a Market for State-Contingent Claims (Economic Journal, Shin)](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article-abstract/103/420/1141/5157258); [Measurement of insider trading in wagering markets (Applied Financial Economics)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09603100600675565)
- In Shin's model, the bookmaker's expected payout is z·Σ(pᵢ/πᵢ) to insiders plus (1−z)·Σ(pᵢ²/πᵢ) to outsiders, where πᵢ are the bookmaker's quoted (vig-included) prices/probabilities, pᵢ are the true win probabilities, and z is the proportion of money coming from informed ("insider") bettors; the bookmaker sets πᵢ to maximize/protect profit under this mix, and inverting this relationship for pᵢ given observed πᵢ (and an implied/estimated z) yields the "zero-margin" true probabilities — [GitHub: mberk/shin — Python implementation of Shin's method](https://github.com/mberk/shin)
- For a two-outcome market, z can be solved analytically, and in that two-outcome case Shin's method is algebraically equivalent to the simple "Additive" devigging method (subtracting an equal absolute slice of margin from each side); for markets with more than two outcomes, Shin's method requires an iterative numerical procedure to jointly estimate z and the probabilities — [GitHub: mberk/shin](https://github.com/mberk/shin); [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- The standard computational approach used in most implementations follows Jullien & Salanié (1994), who provided an iterative algorithm for solving Shin's model; the default method in the widely used `shin` Python/R implementations is based on this Jullien & Salanié algorithm, and it can give slightly different answers from "analytical Shin" approximations, especially when the bookmaker margin (and z) is small — [GitHub: mberk/shin](https://github.com/mberk/shin)
- A 2024/2025 paper by Karl Whelan, "On Estimates of Insider Trading in Sports Betting" (The Manchester School, 2025), revisits Shin's estimator and proposes/evaluates a family of related solutions — [On Estimates of Insider Trading in Sports Betting — Whelan (Manchester School, 2025)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/manc.12505); related working paper: [A Family of Solutions Related to Shin's Model For Probability Forecasts (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381565059_A_Family_of_Solutions_Related_to_Shin's_Model_For_Probability_Forecasts)
- The Shin methodology has been shown in practitioner/technical summaries to be more accurate than simpler normalization approaches because it specifically corrects for the favourite-longshot bias (and in some markets a reverse favourite-longshot bias) rather than assuming the vig is spread evenly or proportionally — [Shin De-Vig: How We Strip Bookmaker Margins to Find Fair Value (OddsFlow)](https://www.oddsflow-partners.com/blog/shin-de-vig-how-we-strip-bookmaker-margins); [Devigging Methods: Power, Shin, Additive, Multiplicative (BetheroSports)](https://betherosports.com/blog/devigging-methods-explained)
- Cain, Law, and Peel ("The Favourite-Longshot Bias, Bookmaker Margins and Insider Trading in a Variety of Betting Markets," SSRN/published work) found empirical support for a central prediction of the Shin (1993) model — that bookmaker margins increase with the number of competitors in a race/market — across a variety of betting markets, though the precise pattern of the favourite-longshot bias was not identical across all markets tested — [Cain, Law & Peel — SSRN](https://autopapers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=418822); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4988900_The_Favourite-Longshot_Bias_Bookmaker_Margins_and_Insider_Trading_in_a_Variety_of_Betting_Markets)

### Inferences
- Because Shin's model has a genuine economic rationale (insider trading / informed bettors), it is not just a curve-fitting adjustment like the power method — it provides a mechanism for *why* the favourite-longshot bias exists, which is likely why multiple studies (see below) find it outperforms naive normalization.
- The need for iterative/numerical root-finding for markets with 3+ outcomes (e.g., 3-way soccer markets, horse racing fields) makes Shin's method meaningfully more complex to implement than proportional devigging, though still tractable with standard numerical solvers (bisection/Newton-type methods) and is available in packaged form (R `implied`, Python `shin`).

- I could not retrieve the full original 1991/1992/1993 Shin papers' exact equations (paywalled/abstract-only access); the formulas above are reconstructed from secondary technical sources (GitHub implementation notes, Whelan 2025) rather than quoted directly from Shin's original text.

## What is "power devigging," and how does it differ from proportional (additive/multiplicative) devigging and from Shin's method?

### Takeaway
The power method devigs by raising each vig-included implied probability to a common exponent k (rather than dividing by, or subtracting a share of, the total overround) and choosing k numerically so the resulting probabilities sum to 1; like Shin's method this reallocates more of the margin to longshots than favourites (addressing favourite-longshot bias), but it does so as a purely statistical/curve-fitting transformation with no underlying behavioral/economic model, and it also requires numerical root-finding rather than a closed-form division.

### Cited Findings
- Formal definition (CRAN `implied` package): power method formula is pᵢ = rᵢ^(1/k), where rᵢ are the vig-included implied probabilities (1/decimal odds) and k is solved numerically such that Σpᵢ = 1 — [CRAN `implied` package vignette — introduction](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- By contrast, the basic/multiplicative method is pᵢ = rᵢ / Σr (simplest, most common, but "tend[s] to be the least accurate of the methods" per the package documentation), and the additive method is pᵢ = rᵢ − ((Σr − 1)/n), which subtracts an equal absolute amount of margin from every outcome and "can produce negative probabilities, especially for outcomes with low probabilities" — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- The power method "slightly reduces larger probabilities more than smaller ones, assuming the sportsbook's margin skews toward favorites," with the favorite "absorbing relatively less vig and the underdog absorbing more" — i.e., it is explicitly designed to mirror the favourite-longshot bias pattern, similar in spirit (but not mechanism) to Shin's method — [Devigging Methods: Power, Shin, Additive, Multiplicative (BetheroSports)](https://betherosports.com/blog/devigging-methods-explained); [Power Method Devig — CalculateMyBet.com](https://calculatemybet.com/power-method-devig/)
- An empirical summary (secondary source, exact study not independently verified) reports: "Shin and the power methods give the best results depending on the bookmaker: 10Bet, Betfair, Pinnacle, and bet365 have the best predictive results using the Shin method while the power method has the best results for 1xbet, TitanBet, and WilliamHill," and that "when odds are above 5.3 there is an advantage to use the power or the Shin method over the two others as the predictive power has increased by almost 1.5%" — [How to Devig Odds — Comparing the Methods (Outlier.bet help center)](https://help.outlier.bet/en/articles/8208129-how-to-devig-odds-comparing-the-methods)
- A 2026 arXiv working paper ("Forecast Sports Outcomes under Efficient Market Hypothesis: Theoretical and Experimental Analysis of Odds-Only and Generalised Linear Models") compares Numerical Shin, Analytical Shin, Power, and Proportional/basic normalization using log-loss on European football odds (Bet365, Bet&Win, Interwetten, Pinnacle, William Hill); it reports that Numerical Shin and Power conversions "significantly outperformed" a proposed alternative method for Bet365, and Power conversion "significantly outperformed" for William Hill, with basic normalization generally underperforming the specialized methods — [Forecast Sports Outcomes under Efficient Market Hypothesis (arXiv 2604.17194)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17194)
- The "odds ratio" (sometimes called logarithmic) method is a distinct third family: OR is solved so that pᵢ = rᵢ / (OR + rᵢ − OR·rᵢ) sums to 1 across outcomes, modeling the relationship between true and bookmaker-implied probabilities via a constant odds-ratio shift (equivalent to a constant shift on the log-odds scale) rather than a power/exponent shift — originally attributed to Keith Cheung and popularized in Joseph Buchdahl's "Wisdom of the Crowd" document — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html); [Using the Wisdom of the Crowd to Find Value in a Football Match Betting Market (football-data.co.uk, Buchdahl)](https://football-data.co.uk/The_Wisdom_of_the_Crowd_updated.pdf)

### Inferences
- The power and odds-ratio methods are "model-free" numerical devices: they pick one free parameter (k or OR) to force the probabilities to renormalize correctly, with the choice of transformation (exponentiation vs. log-odds shift vs. Shin's z) determining how the correction is distributed across favourites vs. longshots. None of the three requires external data to fit — each is solved purely from the odds of a single market — making them only modestly more complex than proportional devigging (a 1-D root-find per market vs. a simple division).
- Secondary/practitioner sources are reasonably consistent that power and Shin methods both beat plain proportional devigging on calibration, but which of power vs. Shin wins appears to be bookmaker- and market-dependent rather than one method dominating universally.

## What other notable devigging approaches exist in the literature (logistic/odds-ratio, Margin Weighted Proportional, Balanced Book, Clarke & Norman, Cain/Law/Peel, Štrumbelj 2014)?

### Takeaway
Beyond Shin and power, the literature/practitioner toolkit includes: the odds-ratio (log-odds shift) method; the "Margin Weights Proportional to the Odds" (MPTO) method from Buchdahl's "Wisdom of the Crowd," which distributes margin in proportion to each outcome's odds; a "Balanced Book" method structurally similar to Shin's but assuming the bookmaker minimizes worst-case loss rather than maximizing expected profit against insiders; and academic contributions from Clarke (2017, "Adjusting Bookmaker's Odds to Allow for Overround"), Cain/Law/Peel (favourite-longshot bias and insider trading across markets), and Štrumbelj (2014, International Journal of Forecasting), the key peer-reviewed empirical comparison paper in this space.

### Cited Findings
- Margin Weights Proportional to the Odds (MPTO / "margin proportional to odds"): pᵢ = (n − M·Oᵢ) / (n·Oᵢ), where n is the number of outcomes, Oᵢ the decimal odds for outcome i, and M the total bookmaker margin; this method assumes the margin applied to each outcome is proportional to the size of the odds (inversely proportional to the outcome's raw probability), explicitly built to reflect the favourite-longshot bias — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html); attributed to Joseph Buchdahl's "Wisdom of the Crowd" — [Using the Wisdom of the Crowd to Find Value in a Football Match Betting Market](https://football-data.co.uk/The_Wisdom_of_the_Crowd_updated.pdf)
- Balanced Book method: structurally parallel to Shin's method (also estimates an implied "insider" proportion via numerical solving) but derived from an assumption that the bookmaker sets odds to minimize losses in the worst-case outcome rather than Shin's expected-profit-maximization-under-informed-trading framing — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- Odds-ratio method (Keith Cheung / Buchdahl "Wisdom of the Crowd"): OR = pᵢ(1−rᵢ) / [rᵢ(1−pᵢ)], solved for a single OR such that the resulting pᵢ = rᵢ/(OR + rᵢ − OR·rᵢ) sum to 1; this is equivalent to applying one constant additive shift on the log-odds scale to every outcome — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html); described as "the logarithmic method" in some practitioner write-ups and reported to "reproduce the asymmetry of bookmaker margins naturally," coming out best in some empirical studies "across tens of thousands of matches" (claim from a non-academic blog/tool source, not independently verified) — [Fair odds calculator — strip the bookmaker margin (logarithmic method), statsnbet.io](https://www.statsnbet.io/en/tools/fair-odds); [No-Vig Calculator: Fair Odds & Bookmaker Margin Removal (impliedscore.com)](https://impliedscore.com/bookmaker-margin-removal-methods/)
- Clarke (2017), "Adjusting Bookmaker's Odds to Allow for Overround," is a specific academic/technical paper on overround-adjustment methods (found via Outlier.bet-hosted PDF), but the PDF could not be parsed as readable text by available fetch tools, so its specific formulas and empirical results could not be extracted in this research pass — [Clarke (2017) — Adjusting Bookmaker's Odds to Allow for Overround (PDF)](https://outlier.bet/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/2017-clarke-adjusting_bookmakers_odds.pdf)
- Cain, Law & Peel's paper on the favourite-longshot bias across betting markets is covered under the Shin section above — it is as much a test *of* Shin's model as an independent method, confirming that bookmaker margins rise with the number of competitors, consistent with Shin's insider-trading prediction — [Cain, Law & Peel — SSRN](https://autopapers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=418822)
- Štrumbelj, E. (2014), "On determining probability forecasts from betting odds," International Journal of Forecasting, Vol. 30, Issue 4, pp. 934–943: compares basic normalization, Shin's (1993) procedure, and regression-based approaches for converting odds to probabilities, concluding that probabilities derived via Shin's procedure are more accurate forecasts than those from basic normalization or regression models, and that betting odds (once properly devigged) are among the most accurate available sources of sports forecasts — [On determining probability forecasts from betting odds — ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533); corroborating secondary summary: [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/On-determining-probability-forecasts-from-betting-%C5%A0trumbelj/8074edd44af6a4a64ab1fffee2642893d693720a)
- A separate paper, "The Conversion of Money Lines into Win Probabilities: Reconciliations and Simplifications" (Berkowitz, Depken & Gandar, Journal of Sports Economics, 2017, published from an earlier working paper), shows that seven methods for converting American money lines into win probabilities actually reduce to only three mathematically distinct estimates of bookmaker commission/win probability once redundancies are accounted for; it finds that one of the three distinct estimators is biased when the money line implies a very heavy favourite, and recommends standard (proportional) normalization for its simplicity and compatibility with the existing literature, despite the bias finding — [Berkowitz, Depken & Gandar — SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2658335); [Working paper PDF, UNC Charlotte](https://pages.charlotte.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/850/2012/10/wp2016-006.pdf); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315916612_The_Conversion_of_Money_Lines_Into_Win_Probabilities_Reconciliations_and_Simplifications)
- A follow-on/related paper, "A Comment on the Bias of Probabilities Derived From Betting Odds and Their Use in Measuring Outcome Uncertainty," engages directly with this literature on biased devigging estimators (located but not deeply read in this pass) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273592715_A_Comment_on_the_Bias_of_Probabilities_Derived_From_Betting_Odds_and_Their_Use_in_Measuring_Outcome_Uncertainty)

### Inferences
- The field effectively has three conceptual families of devigging correction beyond plain proportional: (1) economically-motivated insider-trading models (Shin, Balanced Book), (2) odds-dependent/favourite-longshot-aware heuristics with a single fitted parameter (power, odds-ratio, MPTO), and (3) methods that reduce to combinations of the above when examined closely (per Berkowitz/Depken/Gandar's finding that "seven methods" collapse to three distinct estimators) — suggesting substantial redundancy in the devigging literature and that apparent proliferation of named methods overstates how many genuinely distinct mathematical ideas exist.
- Štrumbelj (2014) is the clearest peer-reviewed, directly-on-topic empirical comparison located in this research; it should be treated as the anchor academic citation for "Shin beats basic normalization" claims.

## What empirical comparisons exist of devigging method accuracy across large samples, and in which sports?

### Takeaway
The clearest peer-reviewed empirical comparison is Štrumbelj (2014), which found Shin's method more accurate (in forecasting accuracy terms) than basic normalization and regression-based methods across tennis, horse racing, and greyhound racing data, with the Shin advantage shrinking as market size (number of competitors/bettors) increases; additional non-peer-reviewed/practitioner comparisons on European football (log-loss across multiple bookmakers) corroborate that Shin and power methods generally beat plain proportional normalization, though no single method dominates across all bookmakers.

### Cited Findings
- Štrumbelj (2014) compared basic normalization, Shin probabilities, and regression-model-based approaches and found "Shin probabilities are better than basic normalization and regression model-based approaches for all bookmaker/sport pairs" tested, covering tennis, horse racing, and greyhound racing betting markets — [search-derived summary of Štrumbelj (2014), International Journal of Forecasting](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533)
- The same line of findings reports that "the advantage of using Shin probabilities and the differences between bookmakers decrease with an increasing market size," and that basic normalization "might still be preferred in applications where forecasting accuracy is not crucial," given its simplicity — [search-derived summary of Štrumbelj (2014)]
- A 2026 arXiv working paper comparing Numerical Shin, Analytical Shin, Power, and basic normalization/proportional methods on European football odds (Bet365, Bet&Win, Interwetten, Pinnacle, William Hill, multiple seasons via football-data.co.uk) using log-loss found that specialized methods (Shin, Power) generally outperform plain proportional normalization, with which specific method wins varying by bookmaker (e.g., Power significantly best for William Hill) — [Forecast Sports Outcomes under Efficient Market Hypothesis (arXiv 2604.17194)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17194)
- A practitioner comparison (non-peer-reviewed) reports Shin method winning for 10Bet, Betfair, Pinnacle, and bet365, while the power method wins for 1xbet, TitanBet, and WilliamHill, with an approximately 1.5% predictive-power gain for power/Shin over simpler methods specifically at high odds (>5.3 decimal) — [Outlier.bet help center: How to Devig Odds — Comparing the Methods](https://help.outlier.bet/en/articles/8208129-how-to-devig-odds-comparing-the-methods)
- A master's thesis, "Assessing the Accuracy of Betting Odds in European Football" (Joseph Edward Pym), appears directly on-topic for comparing devigging method accuracy on European football odds but could not be parsed as text by available fetch tools in this pass — [Assessing the Accuracy of Betting Odds in European Football (PDF)](https://jep00.github.io/docs/work/bettingaccuracy.pdf)
- Related market-structure comparisons (not strictly devigging-method comparisons, but adjacent): a ScienceDirect paper on "Prediction accuracy of different market structures — bookmakers versus a betting exchange" examines whether exchange (Betfair) prices are more accurate than fixed-odds bookmaker prices across multiple European leagues — [Prediction accuracy of different market structures (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207010000105)

## How computationally/conceptually complex is each method relative to standard proportional devigging?

### Takeaway
Proportional (multiplicative) and additive devigging are both closed-form, one-line-formula methods requiring no iteration; power and odds-ratio methods require solving a single nonlinear equation (one free parameter, k or OR) via numerical root-finding per market, a modest step up; Shin's method and the Balanced Book method require a genuine iterative numerical procedure (following Jullien & Salanié 1994 for Shin) to jointly solve for an unobserved parameter (the insider proportion z) and the probabilities, making them the most complex of the commonly cited methods, though all of these are now available as packaged, low-effort library calls (e.g., R's `implied`, Python's `shin`) rather than requiring bespoke numerical code.

### Cited Findings
- Multiplicative/basic method: pᵢ = rᵢ/Σr — direct, closed-form, no solver needed; described as "the simplest and most common method" — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- Additive method: pᵢ = rᵢ − ((Σr−1)/n) — also closed-form/no solver, but can yield negative probabilities for low-probability outcomes, requiring a guard/edge-case check in implementation — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- Power method: pᵢ = rᵢ^(1/k) with k chosen numerically so Σpᵢ=1 — requires a 1-D numerical solve (e.g., bisection) per market; no external data/free parameters beyond the market's own odds — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- Odds-ratio method: similarly requires solving for a single OR parameter numerically so probabilities sum to 1 — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- Shin's method: for exactly two outcomes, reduces to an analytical solution equivalent to the additive method; for 3+ outcomes requires an iterative algorithm (standard implementation follows Jullien & Salanié 1994) to solve jointly for z and the probability vector — meaningfully more complex than the above, but implemented and available off-the-shelf in packages (`shin` in Python, `implied` in R) — [GitHub: mberk/shin](https://github.com/mberk/shin); [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- Balanced Book method: parallels Shin's complexity — also requires numerical solving for an implied insider-like proportion, under a different (worst-case-loss-minimization) assumption about bookmaker behavior — [CRAN `implied` package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html)
- None of the methods surveyed (proportional, additive, power, odds-ratio, Shin, Balanced Book, MPTO) requires external training data, multiple markets, or multi-book consensus data to compute — all operate on a single market's own set of odds; this differs from "regression-based" approaches mentioned in Štrumbelj (2014), which do require fitting parameters from historical outcome data across many markets — [search-derived summary of Štrumbelj (2014)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533)

### Inferences
- From an implementation-effort standpoint, there's a clean three-tier complexity ladder: (1) proportional/additive — trivial closed-form; (2) power/odds-ratio/MPTO — single-parameter numerical solve per market, still self-contained; (3) Shin/Balanced Book — iterative joint solve for an economically-interpretable latent parameter, the most complex but still tractable with existing open-source implementations and not requiring any data beyond the single market's odds.
- The "regression-based" methods referenced in Štrumbelj (2014) are a conceptually separate, higher-complexity tier since they require historical multi-market/multi-outcome data to fit coefficients, unlike the single-market methods above — this is the only family in the surveyed literature that needs more than one market's odds as input.
