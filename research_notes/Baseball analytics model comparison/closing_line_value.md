# CLV is market terminology, not a tested law

Closing Line Value (CLV) — the gap between the price a bettor obtained and the market's final price before an event starts — is a **practitioner concept, not a peer-reviewed academic construct**: no paper was located that defines, names, or empirically validates "CLV" as a bettor-skill metric, and the headline industry claim that sustained positive CLV predicts long-run profitability has never been directly tested via a bettor-level regression of realized ROI on captured CLV. What *does* exist academically is a large, mostly separate literature on sports-betting **market efficiency** — showing closing lines are generally better (though not perfectly unbiased) estimates of true outcome probability than opening lines — plus a body of work documenting persistent biases (favorite-longshot bias, home-team bias, sentiment/popularity bias) baked into those same closing lines, and at least one independent empirical study (Karl Whelan, non-peer-reviewed) directly showing that positive CLV does *not* reliably translate into positive realized profit even across thousands of NBA games. For baseball specifically, no paper uses the term CLV at all, but MLB has one of the richer market-efficiency literatures of any sport, anchored by Jay Simon's 2024 *Management Science* study of 3,681 games, which finds MLB closing lines are "mostly reliable" forecasts but exhibit statistically exploitable overreaction. The net picture: CLV is a theoretically plausible, intuitively well-motivated proxy for betting skill, borrowed from adjacent market-efficiency findings — but it is, at present, an academically under-tested hypothesis dressed in the language of certainty by the industry that sells tracking tools built on it.

## No standard formula exists — devigging choices change the number

Industry sources converge on a basic formula, **CLV% = [(X − Y) / Y] × 100**, where X is the implied probability at closing and Y is the implied probability of the bettor's own price ([search synthesis of CLV calculator guides](https://therundown.io/blog/closing-line-value-api)), but three independent methodological choices mean "CLV" is not one number. First, **raw (vigged) vs. no-vig (devigged) CLV**: no-vig CLV strips the bookmaker's margin from both prices before comparing and is considered the more rigorous convention, since vig itself tends to shrink as a line matures from open to close — one market-efficiency paper found margin falling from 2.81% near opening to 2.03% near closing as volume rose from roughly €1,000 to €10,000 ([MPRA working paper](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/87230/1/MPRA_paper_87230.pdf)) — so raw CLV conflates genuine price movement with pure vig compression. Second, **percentage vs. basis-point/"cents" framing**: percentage CLV dominates point-spread and totals markets priced near -110, while moneyline-heavy sports like baseball are typically tracked in "cents" (e.g., a bet taken at -120 that closes at -130 is described as "10 cents" of CLV) ([search synthesis](https://www.lineups.com/betting/glossary/closing-line-value/)). Third, **benchmark choice**: most practitioner sources default to a single sharp book — overwhelmingly Pinnacle — as the closing-line reference, on the logic that "the closing line is the most efficient price" at high-limit, high-volume books ([Pinnacle](https://www.pinnacle.com/betting-resources/en/educational/what-is-closing-line-value-clv-in-sports-betting)), but this logic is explicitly caveated even by sympathetic commentators as applying best to "high-limit, high-volume events rather than every small derivative market on the board" ([Joe Saumarez Smith](https://joesaumarez.co.uk/sports-betting-market-efficiency-and-the-closing-line)); an alternative convention benchmarks against a multi-book consensus closing line instead. No source — academic or industry — has tested which benchmark choice better predicts long-run profitability.

Removing vig to get a "true" probability is itself a methodological fork with a genuine, peer-reviewed literature behind it. The simplest **multiplicative/proportional method** just normalizes each outcome's implied probability by the booksum so probabilities total 1, but assumes the bookmaker spreads margin evenly regardless of how likely each outcome is — an assumption the academic literature rejects. **Shin's method**, built on Hyun Song Shin's early-1990s insider-trading model of odds-setting (a fraction of bettors assumed to hold private information), redistributes more margin to longshots and is described as "the one favoured in the academic literature on favourite-longshot bias" ([Algorithmic Sports Betting Substack](https://algorithmicsportsbetting.substack.com/p/its-time-to-retire-shins-method)); Štrumbelj (2014), published in the peer-reviewed *International Journal of Forecasting*, directly tested this and found "probabilities determined from betting odds using Shin's model are more accurate forecasts than those determined using basic normalization or regression models" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207014000533)). Clarke et al.'s **power method** — solving for an exponent *k* such that summed odds-derived probabilities equal 1 — is reported to "universally outperform the multiplicative method and outperform or [be] comparable to the Shin method" in a more recent comparison ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326510904_Adjusting_Bookmaker's_Odds_to_Allow_for_Overround)), and a 2025 practitioner critique argues Shin's method is now dated and should be retired in favor of power-method variants ([Algorithmic Sports Betting Substack](https://algorithmicsportsbetting.substack.com/p/its-time-to-retire-shins-method)). The upshot for the report's purposes: there is no single academically settled "best" devigging method — the choice depends on market type (Shin's favored for markets with strong favorite-longshot bias; multiplicative adequate for near-even markets like NFL spreads) and on whether one weights the older peer-reviewed comparison (Štrumbelj, favoring Shin) or newer practitioner-level benchmarking (favoring power).

## The core hypothesis has never been directly tested

This is the single most important finding of this research: **no peer-reviewed paper regresses individual or cohort bettor realized ROI on a measured CLV variable.** Multiple targeted searches for "CLV regression ROI," "sharp bettor closing line value regression," and similar terms returned only industry blogs, tracking-tool marketing pages (Pikkit, Unabated, SharpROI, RebelBetting), and tipster content — none of which cite a specific academic study for the claim that CLV predicts long-run profitability; it is repeated as received wisdom. The closest rigorous substitute literature instead asks a related but distinct question — is the *market*, not the individual bettor, efficient by closing time? — using tools like variance-ratio tests, autocorrelation tests, and threshold-based trading-rule backtests, applied to the price itself rather than to bettor outcomes. Foundational work in this vein (Zuber, Gandar & Bowers 1985; Gandar, Zuber, O'Brien & Russo 1988) established the closing line as the standard efficiency benchmark, generally failing to reject market rationality while still finding a handful of profitable betting rules ([UNLV Gaming Research & Review summary](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?httpsredir=1&article=1123&context=grrj)). More recent entries include Jay Simon's 2024 *Management Science* paper (detailed below), Corey Shank's series of peer-reviewed papers finding **no evidence NFL sportsbooks hold more information than bettors** — consistent with informed bettors, not books, driving line movement ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214635022000806)) — and Tobias Moskowitz's *Journal of Finance*-track working paper "Asset Pricing and Sports Betting," which treats open-to-close line movement as an asset-pricing momentum/reversal phenomenon across more than 100,000 contracts in NBA, NFL, MLB, and NHL ([Yale SOM mirror](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/08/AssetPricingandSportsBetting_JF.pdf)). None of these studies, however, track a cohort of individual bettors' CLV and correlate it with their subsequent realized returns — the exact study design the industry claim implicitly assumes exists.

Steven Levitt's foundational 2004 *Economic Journal* paper, "Why Are Gambling Markets Organised So Differently From Financial Markets?", supplies the key theoretical counterweight: he argues bookmakers set prices to **maximize profit by exploiting known, predictable bettor biases rather than to balance action** on both sides of a bet ([Levitt 2004](https://pricetheory.uchicago.edu/levitt/Papers/LevittWhyAreGamblingMarkets2004.pdf)). If true, the closing line is not a pure aggregation of informed money but partly a profit-maximizing construction that deliberately departs from true probability — which undercuts the simplifying assumption ("closing line ≈ true probability, so beating it ≈ positive expected value") that the entire CLV framework implicitly rests on. Two papers that do look closely at MLB and NFL line dynamics further complicate the "closing line = ground truth" premise rather than confirming it: Jay Simon's analysis (below) finds MLB closing lines show statistically significant negative autocorrelation (overreaction) that simple strategies could exploit, and Ryan Oswald's 2022 undergraduate thesis on NFL markets finds the closing market "generally efficient" but with persistent, specific exceptions (e.g., large underdogs in high-total games covering at a 64.7% rate against a 52.38% breakeven threshold) ([UNI ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.uni.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1538&context=hpt)). One of the only peer-reviewed papers with quantified ROI tied directly to pre-closing-vs-closing comparisons, Vlastakis, Dotsis & Markellos (2009, *Journal of Forecasting*), found a strategy of systematically backing heavy favorites above a probability threshold returned **4.45%** using best-available odds across 12 bookmakers and **2.78%** using mean odds across 21 European football leagues over 11 years — a market-level, not bettor-cohort, result, but the closest available quantified analogue ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/applec/45y2013i3p343-356.html)). Taken as a whole, this literature establishes that beating the closing line is *plausible* evidence of skill under efficient-market logic, but the chain from "I beat the close" to "I will be profitable long-run" has not itself been empirically closed by any cited study.

## Closing lines carry their own documented biases

The theoretical case against treating CLV as sufficient evidence of edge rests on a straightforward logical point with real empirical backing: CLV measures performance *relative to the closing line*, and if the closing line itself is a biased estimator of true win probability, beating it only proves a market-relative edge, not an absolute one. The peer-reviewed literature on sports-betting bias is substantial. The **favorite-longshot bias** — longshots systematically overbet, favorites underbet — is "the most widely documented empirical regularity" in parimutuel betting ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167268117301221)), and Snowberg & Wolfers (2010, NBER/*Journal of Political Economy*) found this reflects bettors' genuine probability-misperception rather than rational risk preference, using large-scale racetrack compound-bet data ([NBER Working Paper](http://www.nber.org/papers/w15923.pdf)). In fixed-odds sports betting the bias still appears but with a twist: one peer-reviewed study of college basketball and football found clear favorite-longshot bias in moneyline markets, yet betting heavy favorites produced near-zero average returns once transaction costs were considered, "suggesting these betting markets are efficient within transaction costs" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1062976916000041)). Home-team bias has also shifted over time in the NFL: Humphreys, Paul & Weinbach documented underestimation of home underdogs ("home-underdog bias"), but more recent work finds "the NFL bias against home teams has been nearly eliminated, while the bias against underdogs has increased" ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-018-9431-4)) — a reminder that documented biases are not static and a CLV benchmark calibrated against one era's bias structure may not transfer to another's.

The most directly relevant bias finding to the "beating the public" logic behind CLV is a peer-reviewed sentiment-bias study using Facebook "Likes" as a popularity proxy across seven leagues: it found bookmakers set point spreads **0.6 points more favorable per percentage point of relative NFL popularity** (about 0.1 points in the NBA) for fan-favorite teams, and critically, that "favorable prices do not translate into a higher probability of winning a bet" — meaning sportsbooks price in crowd sentiment as a factor fully independent of actual win probability ([Economic Inquiry](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ecinqu/v55y2017i2p1119-1129.html)). This is a textbook mechanism by which a closing line can encode a measurable, non-probabilistic distortion that a bettor "beating" the pre-sentiment number might not actually be beating in objective terms. Against this backdrop, the sharpest empirical critique of CLV located anywhere in this research — important to flag as **not peer-reviewed** but as the most methodologically transparent counter-evidence available — comes from economist Karl Whelan's independent analysis of **3,670 NBA games (34,944 quotes, ~10 sportsbooks, 2022/23–2024/25 seasons)**: three of five positive-CLV deciles lost money on average, and only the top decile achieved a meaningful average return (11.4%), with the ninth decile averaging 5% CLV but only 0.2% realized profit ([Karl Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/sportsbetting/the-truth-about-closing-line-value/)). This is direct evidence that positive CLV, even measured across thousands of games, does not map monotonically onto realized profit — directly contradicting the strong form of the industry claim.

Two further academic-adjacent concerns compound the picture. First, **sample-size convergence claims are entirely unverified academically**: industry sources repeatedly assert CLV becomes a reliable signal after roughly 50–300 bets versus thousands needed for raw ROI to separate from zero (one source cites ~6,800 bets for a 2% edge at even odds) ([search synthesis](https://howprosbet.com/how-to-verify-your-betting-edge/)), but no peer-reviewed paper formally derives or tests this statistical-power claim — it is logically plausible (a continuous price-difference metric has lower per-observation variance than a binary win/loss outcome) but unproven in the literature reviewed. Second, there is a **moral-hazard/selection problem** specific to how CLV is used in practice: industry and journalistic sources document that sportsbooks limit accounts specifically for consistently beating the closing line — not for the raw size of winnings — with the Massachusetts Gaming Commission reportedly having found a correlation between CLV and account limiting ([closingline.substack.com](https://closingline.substack.com/p/sportsbook-limits-get-a-closer-look)). This creates a real circularity: CLV is marketed as the gold-standard way for bettors to self-assess skill, yet it is also the exact signal books use to restrict the accounts of bettors who demonstrate it — meaning any large-scale, real-world dataset of bettors who sustained high CLV over a long track record is likely survivorship-biased toward those whose CLV went undetected or unacted-upon, a concern with no peer-reviewed treatment located anywhere in this literature.

## MLB has deep market-efficiency research but zero "CLV" papers

No peer-reviewed paper anywhere uses the literal term "closing line value" in a baseball context — this is a clean, unambiguous gap. What baseball does have, however, is one of the **richest, longest-running market-efficiency literatures of any sport**, dating to 1994 and functioning as a close (if differently labeled) substitute. Woodland & Woodland's 1994 *Journal of Finance* paper, using MLB closing lines from 1979–1989, found the moneyline market generally efficient but documented a **reverse favorite-longshot bias** specific to baseball — bettors favor underdogs relative to what is actuarially justified, the opposite of the classic racetrack pattern ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:49:y:1994:i:1:p:269-79)). The same authors' 2015/2016 follow-up in the *Journal of Economics and Finance* found the **preseason win-total market** is meaningfully inefficient, driven by bettors' "representativeness heuristic" overreaction to prior-season records ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-015-9322-x)) — establishing a clear pattern that single-game MLB markets are tight and efficient while longer-horizon season-aggregate markets carry more persistent, exploitable bias. A 2024/2025 *Applied Economics* paper tested **1,547 simple wagering strategies on MLB moneyline data from 1999–2016** and found only 2.46% profitable at the 5% significance level and just 0.45% at 1% after correcting for multiple-hypothesis testing — strong confirmation of "the extremely efficient nature of the Major League Baseball moneyline wagering market" over that window ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00036846.2024.2364115)).

The single best MLB-specific analogue to a rigorous CLV-validation study is **Jay Simon's 2024 paper in *Management Science***, "Inefficient Forecasts at the Sportsbook: An Analysis of Real-Time Betting Line Movement" (DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.00456), which analyzed detailed opening-to-closing line movement from **four sportsbooks across 3,681 MLB games** ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i12p8583-8611.html)). Simon finds MLB closing-line forecasts are "mostly reliable" — supporting the basic premise underlying CLV, that the close is a strong probability estimate — but also finds **statistically significant negatively autocorrelated line changes** (overreaction that partially reverses) that "could be exploited by sophisticated bettors," plus a non-monotonic accuracy pattern in which weekend day-game lines 90 minutes before first pitch are measurably worse forecasts than lines set earlier. This is directly relevant to the user's question: it is peer-reviewed, top-journal evidence that the MLB closing line, while a good benchmark, is **not a perfectly efficient one** — meaning CLV computed against it inherits a small, real amount of exploitable noise rather than measuring a pristine "true probability" gap. Beyond this, the gaps are notable and should be stated plainly: no MLB paper studies line movement triggered by weather, lineup changes, or starting-pitcher scratches specifically; no paper combines a Retrosheet-style predictive model with a historical MLB odds archive to run a CLV-style backtest; and the widely repeated practitioner claim that popular teams (Yankees, Red Sox, Dodgers, Cubs) are systematically overpriced due to public bias has no peer-reviewed MLB-specific confirmation — it survives only as industry/betting-media assertion (e.g., reporting that the Dodgers led 2026 World Series futures handle at 31.9% of total dollars against 14.0% of tickets, a popularity signal but not a demonstrated mispricing) ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/espn/betting/story/_/id/48294857/mlb-betting-storylines-2026-preview-top-stories-espn)).

## Conclusion

The practical upshot is that CLV should be treated as a reasonable but unproven heuristic rather than a validated predictive law: it is grounded in genuine, peer-reviewed market-efficiency findings (closing lines beat opening lines; informed money moves prices toward efficiency), but the specific industry promise — that an individual bettor's measured CLV forecasts their own long-run realized ROI — has never been directly tested in the academic literature, and the one rigorous independent attempt to check it empirically (Whelan's NBA decile analysis) found the relationship is not monotonic even over thousands of games. For an MLB-focused analytics project, this suggests two concrete implications: first, any internal validation of a predictive model should lean on the Simon (2024) overreaction finding and the Woodland & Woodland reverse-favorite-longshot-bias result as baseball-specific priors about *where* the closing line is likely to be slightly wrong, rather than assuming it is a flawless probability oracle; second, because no academic template exists for a Retrosheet-model-vs-closing-line CLV backtest in baseball, building one would be a genuinely novel contribution rather than a replication of established work — which is itself useful context for scoping ambition versus claims of rigor in any resulting write-up.
